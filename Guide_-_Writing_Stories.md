# **Writing Stories in the Post-Interdict Empire**

## **Executive Summary**

Stories in this setting work by placing specific people with specific material circumstances into situations where institutional fiction collides with lived reality. The Empire maintains systems designed for interstellar civilization on a single trapped world; stories emerge from characters navigating that gap. Effective stories are grounded in concrete sensory detail, told from perspectives of incomplete knowledge, and treat moral complexity without melodrama. Characters are neither heroes nor villainsâ€”they are people with jobs and pressures who make compromises that seem reasonable from inside their circumstances.

---

## **The Documentary Fragment**

The most effective stories present themselves as fragments of larger realities. A tax collector's ledger, a scholar's private assessment, an initiate's diary entry, a Particular Confession before a congregation. This fragmentary quality serves several purposes.

First, it maintains uncertainty about the fantastic elements. We see the ya-Tsatsan whistles that "contain knowledge that can't be written down"â€”but through the eyes of someone who isn't sure whether the knowledge exists or whether the system perpetuates itself through collective incompletion. The supernatural stays at one remove.

Second, it reflects how people actually experience institutions. No one understands the whole Empire. A tax collector understands his ledger, his Warborn, his assessment quota. An Aureate knows her family's patent, her Bureau's politics, the social calendar. Stories gain believability by respecting this natural limitation.

Third, incompleteness creates depth. When Sri Advocate Jorin adds one case daily to a court docket using his great-great-grandmother's formula, we sense institutional history stretching back centuries. We don't need to see that history; the daily ritual implies it.

## **Material Circumstances First**

Every character should have concrete economic and institutional pressures before they have abstract motivations.

Khas-Sekh lives in "three rooms on the fourth floor of a building in the old quarter, where faculty salaries could stretch to respectability if not comfort." His collection has "overflowed any reasonable display." He paid forty obols for a pottery shard "without bargaining, which marked him as either a fool or a man who knew what he was buying." These details establish his position: educated, modestly prosperous, devoted to his scholarship in ways that strain his budget.

Gavit carries "wildly optimistic revenue projections for the Steaming Sky Province based on surveys conducted before the Interdict." His Warborn have joint-ache and labored breathing. His Bureau expects ten thousand obols; he'll report three thousand. These constraints shape every decision he makes in the story.

The es-Verlaus family arrives at an Aureate gathering wearing "leather and wool instead of silk, weapons on their belts, smelling faintly of horse." Merai's application to the Third Bureau Academy was rejectedâ€”"provincial money couldn't purchase actual cultivation." They're rich by frontier standards, marginal by urban ones. Their story emerges from that tension.

Ask: What does this character's job actually require of them? What are they paid? What do they owe? What can they not afford? What institutional reports must they file?

## **Institutional Fiction vs. Lived Reality**

The Empire's defining feature is the gap between what systems claim to do and what they actually do. Stories live in this gap.

Gavit declares that "the Empire is the supreme authority over all human-inhabited space" while standing in a jungle village that barely knows the Empire exists. The mayor laughs at his tax assessment of seventeen thousand obolsâ€”they don't have seventeen hundred most days. But both parties go through the motions: the assessment is made, a token payment accepted, the ledger marked "Compliant."

Sri Advocate Jorin maintains dockets for interstellar courts that can never convene. He adds cases daily according to a formula, files quarterly reports, sells books to actual clients who need actual legal research. His real business exists alongside his theoretical one.

The ya-Tsatsan initiate climbs through degrees of revelation, certain that knowledge exists, unable to reach it. "The waiting is the structure. The incompletion is the inheritance."

Characters should generally understand that institutional claims are fiction. This understanding doesn't free themâ€”they still file the reports, maintain the appearances, add cases to dockets. The system works because everyone participates in sustaining it, even knowing it doesn't work. Ask: What fiction does this institution maintain? How does this character participate in maintaining it? What would happen if they refused?

## **Moral Complexity Without Melodrama**

The tax assessor in "The Collection" skims forty percent, manipulates census records, condemns a girl to indenture. The story describes his cruelty explicitly: "The cruelty wasn't personal. He held no malice toward the Pel family or any others. They were simply entries in a ledger, numbers to be maximized."

This is more disturbing than if he were a cackling villain. He operates within a system, following incentives the system creates. His family's estates grow by two farms per year. The es-Lahun family eventually gets all the land. Everyone gets what they need except the people who don't count as "everyone."

Khas-Sekh writes intelligence assessments about his own pseudonymous poetry, helping the Republic monitor Akama sentiment while privately hoping the Republic falls. He collects Akama artifacts with scholarly devotion while serving the state that conquered the Akama. These contradictions coexist without resolution.

Datho Kel el-Marwen confesses corruptionâ€”then justifies it. He built his political coalition through debt to merchant houses. He extracted policy concessions in exchange. He did this because he believed (genuinely believed) that advancing archaeotech transfer to the Republic mattered more than serving his constituents. "The alternative was to lose my seat to someone who would not advance the technological development of this Republic."

Avoid clear moral categories. Avoid redemption arcs. Show characters whose actions make sense from inside their circumstances, whose compromises seem reasonable given their constraints, whose choices a reader can understand while still judging. Ask: How does this character justify their compromises to themselves? What would they say if accused?

## **Concrete Sensory Detail**

The Kma-Dhol forge-knife is "etched with characters he could not read, prayers or curses in a script that had fallen out of use before the Record of the Convocation." The blade is "still sharp after centuries."

The Akama liquor "hit Vey's throat like industrial solvent mixed with winter forest. Spruce and mint fought with something that tasted like lamp fuel, while coriander provided an aftertaste that somehow made everything worse."

The Aureate gathering features a door that "dissolvedâ€”not opened, but ceased to exist." A young man's throat modification catches "light like oil on water." His synthetic fabric "needed re-treatment"â€”a tell that the family is economizing.

These details do work. The forge-knife establishes Khas-Sekh's scholarly interests and the deep time of Akama cultural decline. The liquor grounds the Akama warriors as specific people with specific customs. The synthetic fabric requiring treatment tells us about the es-Andrel family's economic position without anyone mentioning money.

Good detail is specific (not "ancient weapon" but "Kma-Dhol forge-knife with characters in a script that fell out of use before the Record of the Convocation"), sensory (not "bad liquor" but the specific combination of spruce, mint, lamp fuel, coriander), and load-bearing (revealing character, economics, history, or culture).

## **Cross-Context Encounters**

Many effective stories bring characters from different contexts into contact. The urban tax collector meets the jungle village. The Kma-Dhol engineer plays dice with Akama guards. The provincial Aureate family navigates a ya-Sattran gathering. The Republican senator confesses before a Penitent congregation.

These encounters reveal both contexts through contrast. Gavit's ledger expectations look absurd against the village's actual resources. The es-Verlaus family's wealth appears marginal once surrounded by urban Aureates. Vey's engineering education meets Akama superstitions about "singing to machines"â€”and discovers they're not entirely superstitions.

The encounters should not be occasions for either side to be simply wrong. The village's mythologized history is inaccurate but captures emotional truth. The Akama warriors accept Vey's engineering as "a recognition of function"â€”neither mystifying it nor dismissing it. The urban Aureates are petty and snobbish, but they're also surviving through careful economies in a collapsing system.

Ask: What does each party see when they look at the other? What do they correctly perceive? What do they misunderstand? How do their different knowledge systems interact?

## **Time and Decline**

The Empire is old. Not abstractly oldâ€”specifically old, with specific layers. Khas-Sekh collects pottery shards "from six other Records, a timeline of decline and persistence." His Ogon war-axe belonged to "a man named Gorin Blackwater, who had died at a place called Tsenkher Ford forty years before Khas-Sekh was born."

The decline is material. Tax assessments based on pre-Interdict surveys. Aureate patents for technologies no one can operate. Standing orders written for interstellar administration applied to a single planet. Infrastructure designed for populations that never came, maintained because it has always been maintained.

Characters should feel this layering. They live in arcologies built for other purposes, using archaeotech they don't understand, following procedures handed down through generations. The past is not distantâ€”it's physically present, in the walls and tools and traditions, but its meaning has been lost.

Ask: What physical remnant of earlier eras appears in this scene? What procedure is this character following without understanding why? What has been forgotten?

## **Caste as Condition, Not Category**

The castes are biologically real but functionally misaligned. Warborn "die of cancer before forty." Sickly "ripen for harvest." Colonist castes work as laborers in cities designed for other bodies. The engineering is real; the original purposes are gone.

Characters experience caste through material constraints. Targe's joint-ache is "visible from twenty meters." Yent's "breathing labored" as she lowers herself onto a bench. These are engineered bodies breaking down, designed for purposes no longer relevant.

But caste doesn't determine everything. Khas-Sekh is Akamaâ€”technically a Colonist caste from the high desertâ€”but he's a university professor in a Republican city. Vey is Kma-Dhol, designed for technical intuition, but he doesn't know whether the "singing to machines" is real ability or trained pattern recognition. Characters navigate caste without being reducible to it.

Avoid: caste as simple identity marker, characters whose caste determines their personality, reduction of caste to metaphor for real-world categories. Caste is a material conditionâ€”specific capabilities, specific health problems, specific ways other people perceive you. It shapes lives without dictating them.

## **Scope and Stakes**

Not every story needs to matter to the Empire. The most effective stories often involve small stakes: A tax collector accepting an inadequate tribute. A scholar buying a pottery shard. An initiate waiting for revelation that never arrives. A family at a gathering trying to secure advantageous connections.

The imperial scope provides weightâ€”these small lives exist within systems of enormous scale and ancient historyâ€”but the stories themselves stay focused on specific characters facing specific problems.

When stories do engage larger events (the Plague Crisis, political maneuvering in Ganat, succession crises), they do so through the perspective of specific participants. Datho's confession matters politically, but the story is about Dathoâ€”his justifications, his convictions, the congregation watching him.

Ask: What does this character want today? What obstacle do they face? How does the imperial context shape their options without overwhelming the story?

## **Practical Checklist**

Before writing, answer:

1. Who is this character? What is their job, their economic position, their institutional obligations?  
2. What do they want in this scene? What prevents them from getting it?  
3. What institutional fiction shapes their situation? How do they participate in maintaining it?  
4. What physical remnants of earlier eras appear? What procedures are followed without understanding?  
5. What specific sensory details ground the scene in material reality?  
6. If different contexts meet, what does each party see when they look at the other?  
7. What compromises does this character make? How do they justify them?  
8. What remains incomplete at the end? What doesn't the reader (or character) learn?

## **What to Avoid**

**Heroism and villainy.** Characters should make sense from inside their circumstances, even when their actions are harmful. Avoid redemption arcs, moral awakening, characters who transcend their context through individual virtue.

**Abstraction over material detail.** The Empire's dysfunction should be visible in specific broken systems, not described in general terms. Show the ledger with impossible quotas, not "the burden of imperial taxation."

**Complete explanations.** Characters don't understand their own systems fully. The fantastic elements stay at one removeâ€”seen through uncertain eyes, explained in ways that might be wrong, working for reasons nobody can verify.

**Resolution.** Stories can end, but problems shouldn't be solved. The tax collector will return next year. The initiate will advance one more degree and still not understand. The Aureate gathering concludes, but the economic pressures remain.

**LLM-ese.** Avoid constructions like "it's not Xâ€”it's Y," theatrical headers, overwrought metaphors, the word "weight" applied to abstractions. Know what you mean when you say it.
