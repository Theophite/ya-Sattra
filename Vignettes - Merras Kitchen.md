# Merra's Kitchen

The queue began forming before Merra had even adjusted the dampers on the stew pot, though "queue" suggested more organization than actually existed. Salvage crews didn't queue so much as cluster, their positions determined by an intricate calculus of who had found what below, who owed whom for yesterday's advance, and whether anyone had brought contributions worth jumping the line.

"Back already, Vin?" Merra didn't look up from her ladle, recognizing the particular pattern of dripping that meant someone had come straight up from the third transition chamber without properly wringing out their gear. "Must have found something worth rushing."

Vin, one of Keras Venn's nephews, pulled a cloth bundle from inside his wetsuit. "Pre-Interdict circuit boards. Clean ones. Den Vesh's people will want first look." He paused, calculating. "Worth three days' meals?"

"Two days, and only because those boards mean the Venns can fix the lift brake next week." Merra made a mark in her mental ledger while serving out the first bowl. The stew today had good balance—the mussels were at peak freshness, the koji-fermented miso from yesterday's batch had achieved proper depth, and someone had contributed actual black pepper, probably fallen from a spice ship's loading.

The kitchen's architecture forced specific movement patterns. The salvage crews, still dripping, stayed near the drain grates by the entrance. The stevedore orphans who'd been adjusting counterweights all morning clustered by the engine-side wall where the heat helped their perpetually strained muscles. The pure-line sewer-fishers, including two Sarns who rarely surfaced, occupied the corner where generations of embedded fish oil created an olfactory boundary that others found difficult to cross.

"Heard there was a collapse in the Penitent Ward tunnels," Tam Loak mentioned, not quite casually, while adding dried eel to the pot. "Anyone working that section?"

The room's attention shifted subtly. A collapse meant either opportunity—newly exposed salvage—or disaster—blocked routes, trapped crews. Three separate conversations paused while everyone calculated whether this information affected their operations.

"South end or north?" asked Korim Sarn, his voice carrying the strange tonal qualities of someone who spent too much time speaking the mathematical pressure-language of the deep populations.

"South. Near the old detention complex."

Korim made a sound that might have been relief or disappointment, modulated in frequencies that made nearby metal fixtures vibrate sympathetically. His breakfast bowl contained things Merra had added specifically for the Sarns—certain algaes that grew near the third transition chamber, processed with techniques that remained deliberately unexamined. The other crews pretended not to notice when Korim added three drops of something viscous and faintly luminescent from a vial at his belt.

The morning's intelligence was already crystallizing. Jossa Loak-Benru had arrived with documentation that suggested the Salvage Guild was about to revise copper prices—her carefully neutral expression as she mentioned "bureaucratic adjustments" meant prices were going up. Two crews immediately began discussing whether to hold their current copper salvage or sell quickly before official word spread.

A new arrival disrupted the flow—Hannok, one of the Chain-Men from Bright-Spire, his presence immediately shifting the room's dynamics. Conversations didn't stop but became performative, discussing nothing while watching everything. Merra served him without comment, charging the standard rate despite knowing he wouldn't pay, adding the debt to a different mental ledger that tracked obligations of violence rather than commerce.

"Your boy's doing well with the Companions," Hannok mentioned to Yuli, who had emerged from the engine room wiping oil from her scarred hands. "Saw him at the clinic yesterday. Good with the Mutterers."

The statement hung between threat and compliment. Bos's Companion training was the clan's largest investment, representing years of saved resources. Hannok's attention to it could mean protection or targeting. Yuli's response—a noncommittal nod while ladling her own portion—gave away nothing while acknowledging everything.

By full daylight, the kitchen had served ninety-three bowls, processed seventeen information exchanges, prevented two potential conflicts through strategic seating intervention, and established the day's salvage priorities through collective consensus that appeared casual but operated with parliamentary precision. The perpetual stew had been refreshed with contributions ranging from prime dock-spillage barley to questionable but protein-rich additions from the deep tunnels that only the Sarns could identify.

Merra added water to compensate for the morning's consumption, adjusted the heat to maintain the eternal simmer, and began preparing for the evening shift. The lunch crowd would be different—dock workers on break, children sent with family contributions, the occasional Bureau inspector pretending to investigate health violations while actually gathering intelligence on salvage flows.

The kitchen operated on trust that existed nowhere else in the Middens. The unspoken agreement that this space remained neutral, that information traded here wouldn't be immediately weaponized, that everyone needed at least one place where nutrition took precedence over conflict. It was a fragile peace, maintained through Merra's careful management and the collective understanding that destroying it would hurt everyone equally.

The stew continued its eternal simmer, accepting all contributions, transforming them into something that kept several hundred people functional if not quite nourished, its consistency the only true constant in the vertical economy of the Middens.
