# **The Middens: A RAG Navigation Guide**

## **Executive Summary**

This document provides comprehensive coverage of ya-Sattra's poorest district—approximately 15,000 people living in seven tenement buildings that jut into the Rift on partially flooded pre-Interdict foundations. The Middens exists because the Rotten Quarter needs somewhere for people who have fallen as far as they can fall, and because the flooded foundations provide access to salvage, fishing, and tunnel systems that more stable ground cannot reach.

The document covers three domains: the seven tenements (geography, populations, power structures, key locations), the major organizations (Hanged Men and their three crews, six sewer-fisher clans, the Valve-Saint Parish), and aesthetics (visual culture, sound, architectural character). Approximately fifty named individuals appear across these sections, with the Fen family leadership, sewer-fisher matriarchs and patriarchs, and religious figures receiving the most detailed treatment.

The document's central concern is water. The Hanged Men's power derives from controlling distribution through the Lockworks facility. The sewer-fishers' survival depends on access to flooded infrastructure. The Valve-Saint Parish worships in an overflow chamber where seawater seeps through the floor. Understanding who controls water, who needs it, and what they trade for it explains most of what happens in the Middens.

**Businesses** provides granular detail on specific establishments: Lift-3 and its operations, the Benru documentation desk, Merra's kitchen, drinking establishments, repair services, trade and exchange operations, specialty goods vendors, lodging, and service providers. ## **When to Use This Document**

This document provides enough information to write scenes set in the Middens without supplemental research for most purposes. Use it when introducing Middens characters, establishing the power dynamics between Hanged Men and their subjects, depicting sewer-fisher clan life, portraying Penitent practice in industrial settings, or determining what a particular tenement looks, sounds, and smells like.

The document does not provide deep biological detail on sewer-fishers (their cognitive architecture, reproductive patterns, Technical Caste inheritance), extended treatment of Hanged Men crew dynamics (Valvehand operations, Iron Yards Boys extraction methods, Chain-Men succession politics), or information about the Ridge, the Iron Yards, or broader Rotten Quarter geography. These topics have standalone documents that should be consulted when the Middens material gestures toward them.

The document deliberately obscures certain elements: what the Sarns trade with in the deep tunnels, what the dome at Sump-Gate's lowest level actually does, the full extent of the tunnel systems accessible through Lift-3. These uncertainties are features, not omissions—the Middens residents don't know either.

# **The Tenements of the Middens**

## **Geographic Overview**

The Middens occupy a promontory of partially flooded pre-Interdict foundations jutting into the Rift, ya-Sattra's harbor carved from the former undersea canyon of Monterey Bay. The district's position creates its defining characteristic: water surrounds and permeates everything. The Rift's dark water laps at building foundations, fills basement levels during high tide, and provides the maritime access that makes the Middens economically viable despite its poverty.

The terrain follows a gradient from the Rift's edge inward. The northern and western boundaries face open water, where fishing boats and salvage vessels tie up at improvised docks. The southern edge rises toward the Ridge, that fallen arcology whose angled bulk provides the Rotten Quarter's northern border. The eastern edge blurs into the Iron Yards without clear demarcation—the quarried depression of salvage operations begins where the Middens' flooded foundations end.

Seven tenement buildings rise from this foundation, each having developed distinct character based on its position, structural inheritance, and relationship to power. The Gallows and Bright-Spire dominate the southern edge, their height making them visible from throughout the district. Hollow-Egress and Sump-Gate occupy the western waterfront, their lower floors extending into the harbor. Lantern-Heaps and Knife-Edge cluster in the transition zone toward the Iron Yards, their industrial functions serving the salvage economy. Vetch-Rise fills the remaining space, absorbing population that other buildings reject.

## **How the Buildings Connect**

Only Vetch-Rise connects directly to the mainland, making it the district's sole pedestrian entry point. Everyone entering or leaving the Middens on foot passes through Vetch-Rise's ground floor or pays for boat transport. This position explains why the Yen Tam Society maintains influence there despite Hanged Men dominance elsewhere—they control access.

The Gallows stands centrally, its height allowing observation of movement between buildings and its water-control infrastructure determining who gets water. The Hanged Men's headquarters position isn't accidental; it provides both surveillance and leverage.

Bright-Spire rises from the harbor itself, foundations submerged, accessible primarily by boat or by bridges connecting to Hollow-Egress one building inland. This makes it defensible—the Chain-Men need only hold bridges and stair-landings—but also isolates it from commercial life. Hollow-Egress sits between Bright-Spire and the Gallows, its Lift-3 operation making it the economic heart of salvage work despite the Gallows' political centrality.

Sump-Gate extends furthest into the water, northwest of the other buildings. Fishing boats and salvage vessels dock there because reaching other buildings requires navigating canal networks that flood unpredictably. The building's pumping infrastructure processes water for the entire district, making it essential regardless of who controls it politically.

Lantern-Heaps occupies the southern edge nearest the Ridge, its position making it the district's connection to legitimate commerce. The Cho gondola runs from here to Ridge workshops, the only direct route between Middens poverty and Rotten Quarter prosperity. Knife-Edge leans against the eastern rubble, its forges processing salvage arriving through networks connecting to the Iron Yards.

Movement follows predictable daily patterns. Workers descend from sleeping quarters in Vetch-Rise or Bright-Spire, cross to Hollow-Egress for Lift-3 access, eat at Merra's kitchen, and return by evening. Commerce flows through the Gallows' exchange market and the Benru desk. Religious life centers on Sump-Gate's Valve-Saint Parish. The Chain-Men's territorial control of Bright-Spire and the Hanged Men's infrastructure control from the Gallows create competing authorities that residents navigate constantly.

Total population is approximately 15,000, though census figures are unreliable. The Bureau of the Lens estimates 12,000; the Hanged Men's collection records suggest 18,000; the Valve-Saint Parish baptismal registry shows 16,500 over the past decade with unknown survival rates. The discrepancy reflects the Middens' fundamental character—a district where documentation exists in parallel versions, each serving different purposes, none capturing complete reality.

The climate is perpetually damp. Fog rolls in from the Rift most mornings, burning off by midday only during summer months. Rain falls frequently, channeled through elaborate collection systems that supplement the Hanged Men's controlled water supply. The air carries salt from the harbor, coal smoke from Knife-Edge's forges, and the particular smell of industrial runoff mixed with decomposing organic matter that locals stop noticing within weeks of arrival.

## **The Gallows**

The Gallows dominates the Middens both physically and psychologically. Fifteen stories of rust-stained metal and reinforced concrete rise from pre-Interdict foundations that have settled unevenly over centuries, requiring constant structural reinforcement to support additions never part of the original design. The building houses approximately 200 Hanged Men plus support personnel—cooks, cleaners, the families of senior members who've earned the right to residential security.

The building's lower floors present an administrative facade. Ygren Fen's exchange market occupies the ground through third floors, where the Hanged Men's complicated economy resolves into fungible currency. Protection fees paid in salvage, food, labor vouchers, or more exotic currencies are evaluated by assessors who've developed specialized knowledge of the district's informal markets. The conversion rates favor the house, naturally, but maintain enough consistency that merchants prefer Fen-backed scrip to the chaos of direct barter.

The middle floors house the organization's workforce. Fourth-floor residents share dormitory rooms and communal latrines; eighth-floor lieutenants have private spaces and windows that open. This internal hierarchy creates competition that the Knot encourages—men work harder knowing that success means better quarters, that failure means descent to shared rooms where privacy doesn't exist. The barracks maintain military discipline despite the organization's evolution from militia to protection racket, with morning formations, equipment inspections, and the perpetual readiness that Luka Fen considers essential to organizational culture.

The ninth floor serves recreation and surveillance simultaneously. Gambling tables, drinking halls, and fighting rings provide release valves for violent men confined to barracks life. More subtly, the space allows Luka to observe his subordinates in unguarded moments—who drinks too much, who accumulates gambling debts, who shows signs of ambition that might threaten organizational stability. Sera Galen manages these spaces with the same attention to detail she brings to her Ridge liaison work, understanding that entertainment is intelligence when properly observed.

The upper floors belong to leadership. Luka commands the entire north face of the tenth and eleventh floors; Jarek controls the south. An empty room reserved for Garren Fen, who physically cannot navigate the upper stairs, serves as reminder that power sometimes requires accommodation to those who cannot fully participate in it. Riva Fen-Cho's Lockworks command center occupies the twelfth floor, where maps accurately reflect the district's hydraulic reality—every pipe, every valve, every point where water can be redirected, restricted, or shut off entirely.

The cisterns occupy floors thirteen and fourteen. Six massive tanks hold months of water for the building itself plus the reserves that make the Hanged Men's distribution monopoly possible. Treatment facilities—sand filtration, chemical treatment, the ultraviolet sterilization systems that require Lantern-Heaps' electrical expertise—produce water that is reliably safe when nothing else in the district qualifies. The cisterns represent the organization's true power: not violence, though violence matters, but the control of something everyone needs to survive.

The fifteenth floor houses the council chamber and crane platform. The Knot meets here monthly or when crisis demands, seven seats arranged around a table salvaged from some pre-Interdict administrative facility, its surface scarred by decades of different purposes. The crane platform extends from foundations that run through the entire building's core, engineered to support loads the original structure never anticipated. The crane serves practical functions—lifting cargo from ships, moving materials between districts—but its symbolic purpose overshadows utility. Public executions follow formal ritual: the condemned is lifted at dawn, suspended over the district for one hour while residents observe, then dropped. The stories everyone knows create more control than the executions themselves.

## **Bright-Spire**

Bright-Spire's architecture embodies its social reality. The structure tapers as it rises, each floor smaller than the one below, compressing its 2,700 residents into increasingly constrained spaces while 83 Chain-Men control movement through strategic bottlenecks. The building's proximity to the Gallows creates a binary power structure: administrative authority flowing from one tower, raw violence emanating from the other. The connecting bridge at the eleventh floor allows Chain-Men to access their drinking halls and gambling dens without descending to street level, where their victims might organize.

The sub-basements house the Bannick aquaculture complex, six levels of partially flooded space maintaining different chemical compositions for specialized protein production. The perpetual dampness has created unique ecological niches—bioluminescent algae providing dim light, eels adapted to industrial toxins, mussels that concentrate metals in their shells rather than flesh. The Bannicks work in near-darkness, their skin showing characteristic chemical lesions from chronic exposure to water that technically supports life but slowly destroys those who live in it. Their operation produces approximately 100 kilograms of protein weekly, most of which the Chain-Men tax or contaminate as punishment for perceived infractions.

The ground through second floors contain the majority of residents, the widest part of the spire housing families in conditions that redefine density. Eight people occupy spaces designed for two, using salvaged materials to create vertical subdivisions within already tiny rooms. The architecture here still allows lateral movement—corridors wide enough for two people to pass, communal spaces capable of holding gatherings—which makes these floors the most politically significant. Organized resistance remains theoretically possible where people can meet without passing through controlled chokepoints.

The third floor houses Deacon Sela Loak's kitchen and chapel, where communal functions persist despite Chain-Men surveillance. The kitchen serves increasingly thin porridge to queues that grow longer as resources diminish. The chapel altar—a salvaged industrial valve painted with revolutionary iconography disguised as religious imagery—provides focal point for the prayer circle's barely concealed militancy. Two hundred armed residents maintain membership, their weapons hidden as religious objects: sapphire-glass knives sold as prayer beads, crossbows tensioned with leaf springs, cargo hooks that serve obvious legitimate purposes until they don't.

The fourth through ninth floors demonstrate the gradient of compression. Each ascending floor loses approximately fifteen percent of the area below it. By the fifth floor, corridors require single-file movement. By the seventh, adults must duck through doorways. By the ninth, even small residents navigate by turning sideways. This architectural funnel transforms the stairwell into a series of defensible positions where one Chain-Man can control all vertical movement. The compression creates unique psychological pressures—residents develop compensatory behaviors, walking single-file even in open spaces, automatically turning sideways at doorways, calculating passage width before attempting movement.

Sela's choice to live on the tenth floor, directly below the Chain-Men barracks, represents calculated provocation. The floor vibrates with their movement above, a constant reminder of proximity to violence. Her prayer circle maintains visible presence here, their weapons obvious enough to establish deterrent, concealed enough to avoid triggering the massacre that overt armament would provoke. The mathematics favor eventual revolution—2,700 against 83—but the architecture favors defense. The question isn't whether violence will erupt but whether it will flow upward through narrowing stairs or downward from positions of elevation.

The Chain-Men have modified the eleventh and twelfth floors to suit their preferences, physically breaking through floor structures to create vertical connections that trust the pre-Interdict core to hold despite their vandalism. Their common area reeks of home-distilled alcohol—industrial ethanol poisoned with methanol, fusel oils, and occasionally hallucinogenic compounds from Bannick pools. They don't care. Warborn cancer will kill them by forty regardless of what they drink.

At the spire's peak, where the building narrows to eight meters across, the Benku family maintains the crane that generates Bright-Spire's only legitimate income. Their single room represents maximum elevation with minimum freedom—they can see the entire harbor but cannot leave without passing through Chain-Men territory. The crane's reach extends fifty meters horizontally from the building's peak, allowing direct loading from ships to Ridge-bound wagons, bypassing dock fees and ground-level taxation. This geographic advantage would make the Benkus wealthy if they could keep their earnings. Instead, they exist in the peculiar torture of handling fortune while living in poverty.

## **Hollow-Egress**

Hollow-Egress represents the Middens' most successful experiment in cooperative governance. Nine stories of stable construction house approximately 1,800 residents under the joint administration of five sewer-fisher clans who have created functional interdependence that resists both external exploitation and internal collapse. The building's stability—physical and social—stems from the massive cargo elevator at its core, Lift-3, which provides exclusive access to salvage-rich underground areas that surface operations cannot reach.

The sub-level infrastructure extends far deeper than the building's visible height. Lift-3's shaft descends through pre-Interdict foundations into tunnel systems that predate current understanding. The first transition chamber at twenty meters allows surface-dwellers with equipment. The second at three hundred meters requires sewer-fisher physiology—the ability to navigate spaces that would trigger claustrophobia in baseline humans, to breathe air that carries industrial contaminants, to perceive in darkness through senses that Technical Caste inheritance provides. Below that, the tunnels continue, but only Clan Sarn knows how far.

The ground floor divides between Lift-3's massive doors and the communal spaces that make cooperative governance possible. Loading bays handle the daily flow of salvage ascending from below—exotic materials, pristine components, occasional items showing manufacture dates that surface chronology cannot explain. Administrative functions occupy the eastern section, where representatives from each clan manage the schedules, quotas, and revenue sharing that keep five families working together rather than against each other.

Clan Loak controls Lift-3 itself. Yuli Loak manages operations with the burn-scarred face that testifies to her commitment—hot lubricant from a feedline explosion, an accident that would have killed someone less determined to maintain position. Her mother Merra runs the kitchen on the eighth floor, that legendary operation where the perpetual stew has fed salvage crews for decades. The queue begins forming before dawn, positions determined by complex calculations of who found what below, who owes whom, which clans are cooperating and which are feuding this week.

Clan Benru occupies the fifth floor archive, maintaining the documentation that allows sewer-fishers to work in a system that doesn't officially acknowledge their existence. Every morning, workers arrive to collect the temporary permits, work authorizations, and identity documents that Korel Benru's staff produce from nothing but stamps and paper. The fiction is transparent—the same workers appear under different names in different ledgers—but the forms satisfy inspection requirements, which is all that matters.

Clan Venn handles rigging throughout the building and beyond, their expertise in cables, pulleys, and load distribution making them essential to any operation requiring vertical movement. Eldin Venn can read metal fatigue by sound alone, hearing stress patterns in cable tension that predict failure before visible signs appear. His children learn the trade from infancy, developing the spatial awareness and technical intuition that no formal education can replicate.

Clan Hasket provides physical security and aquaculture products. Their unusual composition—big cousins fostered from stevedore orphans alongside little cousins of traditional sewer-fisher descent—creates capabilities neither population possesses alone. The big cousins provide visible deterrent; the little cousins provide technical expertise. Together, they maintain mussel beds in the building's flooded basement, producing protein that supplements the grain economy everyone else depends on.

Clan Sarn controls deep access and trades with whatever exists in the tunnels below the second transition chamber. Patriarch Vennas Sarn rarely surfaces, spending months at depths where the pressure changes human perception in ways he no longer tries to explain. When he does appear, his skin shows lichen crusts that don't quite look terrestrial, his eyes carrying bioluminescent glow that persists even in bright light. What the Sarns trade for, what they trade with—these questions remain unanswered because the answers might be worse than uncertainty.

## **Sump-Gate**

Sump-Gate exists because ya-Sattra needs its water pumped somewhere, and the Middens provided convenient foundations. The pumping station occupies nine levels below ground and four above, processing millions of gallons daily through machinery that predates current Imperial records. Approximately 1,500 residents live in spaces that industrial function didn't originally intend for habitation, their lives shaped by the constant thrum of pumps that never stop.

The deepest level, nine stories below the surface, houses the dome. Workers report consistent experiences when assigned to this level—the sensation of understanding mathematical principles just beyond conscious grasp, the conviction that the dome responds to presence rather than action, temporary improvements in spatial reasoning that fade after returning to the surface. The dome itself shows no visible connections to the pump infrastructure above, yet the correlation is undeniable: when the dome is accessible, pumps work; during the one recorded incident where the sub-basement flooded completely, pumps failed until water was cleared.

Mutterers congregate near the dome in groups that shift composition according to logic no Companion has decoded. They apply modifications to its surface—geometric patterns in conductive materials, components that serve no obvious purpose, arrangements created and destroyed according to internal imperatives. These modifications correlate with pump efficiency improvements that persist for weeks after application. No one understands the mechanism. Everyone accepts the results.

The middle basement levels house the actual pumping mechanisms, massive constructions that move water through the building and out to sea. Each pump is maintained by specific families who've developed generational knowledge of their particular machines. The Mutterers nest in unused sections of these levels, their presence tolerated because their obsessive optimization occasionally prevents failures that scheduled maintenance would miss. Curate Aram rings disruption chimes hourly to prevent the cascading vocalizations that once resulted in three Mutterers attempting to rebuild a pressure valve while congregants were still arriving for service.

The Valve-Saint Parish occupies the original overflow chamber at sea level, a vast space extending into the harbor from the building's base. The floor sits below high tide, with grated sections allowing controlled water entry—not flooding but deliberate architectural feature. Seawater seeps through during spring tides and storms, never more than ankle-deep but enough that congregants learn appropriate footwear. The altar is the legendary wheel, four meters in diameter, its corroded surface bearing generations of carved prayers. According to tradition, when catastrophic flooding threatened the building, a Redback stevedore whose name is lost turned this wheel that hadn't moved in centuries, activating the pumps through pure physical determination. His heart failed from the strain, but the water receded. The wheel probably never controlled the pumps—any engineer can see the lack of mechanical connection—but the story provides meaning that technical accuracy cannot replace.

Curate Aram es-Thabit conducts services that acknowledge the building's nature without surrendering to it. His sermons incorporate industrial reality—the sound of pumps as background rather than impediment, the tide as rhythm rather than threat, the Mutterers above as witnesses rather than intrusions. The parish serves fishers, pump workers, and anyone seeking meaning in mechanical systems that exceed human understanding. The grain dole operates from the adjacent kitchen, where Deacon Mer Hasket manages supplies with the obsessive documentation that two years ago proved the parish wasn't selling donated food. The inspector left. The documentation habit remained.

The upper floors house residential and technical functions. Waterwheel generators in outflow pipes convert water pressure to modest electricity—untaxed because no one has figured out how to meter it, distributed through informal agreements rather than official allocation. The short-wave relay station on the third floor provides communication links to maritime traffic, its operators timing transmissions for atmospheric conditions rather than pump cycles. Cisterns on the roof store processed water from osmotic membranes that the pump pressure drives, producing freshwater with slight residual salinity that meets basic safety standards.

## **Lantern-Heaps**

Lantern-Heaps exists because ya-Sattra produces more electronic waste than its formal recycling systems can process, and someone realized that waste contained value. Eight stories of salvaged shipping containers, industrial modules, and improvised construction house approximately 2,200 residents, most of them employed in the sorting, testing, and resale of electronic components that flow through the building in rivers of silicon and copper.

The Cho family controls the operation through ownership of the transformer station that provides the building's power. Their hybrid heritage—sewer-fisher technical intuition combined with Fen organizational instincts from a marriage three generations past—created the expertise to maintain electrical systems that formal engineering couldn't service. The rainbow-colored lights that mark Cho territory throughout the Middens advertise their monopoly: if you want electricity, you deal with the Chos.

The ground floor houses the chip bazaar, tables arranged with obsessive precision displaying sorted electronic components. Capacitors cluster by voltage rating, resistors by resistance value, integrated circuits by package type and suspected function. Children as young as six work desoldering stations, their small fingers nimble enough for precision work that adult hands struggle with. A clipped jargon has developed: "pregnant capacitor" means bulging from heat damage, "whisker bridge" means tin dendrites shorting adjacent pins, "magic smoke escaped" means the component is dead.

The sorting floors above follow the value gradient. First floor handles bulk scrap—wire by the kilogram, boards stripped of obvious components, materials destined for metal recovery rather than reuse. Second floor processes testable components, each piece checked against salvaged specification sheets before pricing. Third floor handles the premium items—working processors, intact memory modules, the rare functional assemblies that emerge from the salvage stream intact.

The Cho family apartments occupy the upper floors, their spaces distinguished by the reliable lighting that elsewhere in the Middens requires careful rationing. The contrast is deliberate—visitors see what consistent electricity provides, understand what they're paying for when they negotiate Cho rates. The family's technical expertise extends beyond simple distribution; they maintain the Lockworks' control systems under contract to the Knot, their knowledge of the water infrastructure making them essential despite their hybrid status.

The gondola station occupies a reinforced platform on the eighth floor, its cables stretching to the Ridge where Sera Galen's ancestors established the connection decades ago. The ride costs four obols round trip, expensive enough to limit casual traffic, cheap enough that workers can afford it when necessity demands. The gondola provides the only direct Middens-Ridge link, carefully controlled by a single family who profit from the monopoly while maintaining just enough accessibility to prevent alternatives from developing.

## **Knife-Edge**

Knife-Edge leans fifteen degrees from vertical, a structural defect that would have condemned the building in any district with functioning inspection services. Instead, the lean has become the building's defining characteristic, shaping everything from furniture design to resident psychology. Approximately 1,600 people live and work in spaces that fight them constantly, developing the tilted gait that marks Knife-Edge residents anywhere in ya-Sattra.

The ground floor houses the forge operations that give the building economic purpose. Coal-fired furnaces produce the heat for metalworking—cable production, tool repair, the specialized components that salvage operations require. The forges burn constantly, their smoke contributing to the haze that hangs over the eastern Middens. Workers time their communications to gaps in the hammering, entire conversations happening in spaces between strikes.

The cable works occupy the second through fourth floors, where Clan Venn maintains the equipment for producing the rigging that vertical life requires. Raw wire arrives from Iron Yards suppliers; finished cable departs for every building in the district. The work requires precision despite the angled environment—tension must be consistent, splice joints must hold, failure means someone falls. Workers have adapted their techniques to account for the lean, developing methods that wouldn't work in level spaces but produce reliable results in Knife-Edge's particular geometry.

Residential floors above the industrial zone house workers and their families. Every room shows adaptation to the fifteen-degree angle. Furniture sits on legs of different lengths. Beds wedge against the high wall. Liquids require covered containers or they slide toward the edge. Children grow up navigating by feel, developing the instinctive calculations of slope and grip that become unconscious by adulthood.

The building's lean is increasing—perhaps a degree every few decades—as foundations settle unevenly into the saturated soil beneath. Engineers estimate another century before the structure becomes genuinely uninhabitable, but in the Middens, a century might as well be forever. Current residents will be long dead before the final collapse. Their children might live to see it. Their grandchildren almost certainly will. But that's someone else's problem.

## **Vetch-Rise**

Vetch-Rise occupies the liminal space between the Middens and the Iron Yards, serving as first landing for those desperate enough to accept any shelter and last resort for those with nowhere else to fall. Seven stories of improvised construction house approximately 2,500 residents, though the population fluctuates weekly as arrivals and departures follow rhythms that have nothing to do with the building and everything to do with circumstance.

The building lacks institutional coherence. No dominant clan, no industrial focus, no unifying infrastructure. The Yen Tam Beneficial Society provides theoretical protection from their third-floor offices, but their influence extends only as far as their ability to negotiate with Chain-Men who treat the building as hunting preserve. President Lao Duc, identifiable by his missing fingers—tribute paid to maintain even minimal authority—manages a treasury too thin to prevent all violence. The Society's protection extends mainly to preventing complete massacre, ensuring the building remains profitable enough to exploit rather than simply destroy.

The Transient Market assembles wherever Chain-Men aren't currently occupying, its vendors carrying entire inventories ready to move at moment's notice. The goods are marginal but essential: used clothing that marks the wearer as employed rather than desperate, food that's technically still edible, documentation that might pass casual inspection. When danger appears, the market dissolves like smoke, vendors vanishing into stairwells and maintenance passages, reassembling elsewhere once danger passes. This architectural guerrilla commerce requires no infrastructure beyond memory of where today's safe spaces might be.

The Astal population has claimed the fourth and fifth floors, their tiny frames allowing subdivision of spaces that larger bodies couldn't navigate. They've installed platforms that cut rooms to half-height, drilled bolt-holes through floors, created the vertical warrens they prefer. Their presence provides unexpected stability—Chain-Men find Astal spaces too cramped to exploit, while the Astals' rapid breeding produces population density that deters casual violence.

Rain-net repair operates from the sixth floor, the only genuine industry in the building. Nets from throughout the district arrive for patching—polymer mesh that captures fog moisture, actual netting that channels rain, fabric sheets worn by constant exposure to weather. The work requires delicate hands and patient attention, paying subsistence wages to residents who've found nothing better. Chain-Men rarely raid this floor, understanding that disrupting rain-net repair would anger every building that depends on water collection.

## **District Relationships**

The tenements relate through function rather than formal administration:

**The Gallows** controls water that **Hollow-Egress** distributes through deep infrastructure. **Hollow-Egress** provides salvage that **Knife-Edge** processes into usable materials. **Knife-Edge** produces cables that **Lantern-Heaps** requires for electrical distribution. **Lantern-Heaps** powers the systems that **Sump-Gate** uses to pump water. **Sump-Gate's** pumps prevent flooding that would destroy **Bright-Spire's** aquaculture. **Bright-Spire's** crane serves **Vetch-Rise's** transient population by enabling cargo movement that bypasses ground-level taxation.

These relationships create stability through interdependence. No single tenement can be destroyed without cascading effects that damage the others. The Hanged Men exploit this stability while being constrained by it—they could crush Hollow-Egress's independence, but doing so would disrupt the salvage flow that the entire district's economy depends on.

**With the Iron Yards** to the east, the relationship is symbiotic. The Yards provide salvage; the Middens provide labor and housing for workers who can't afford Iron Yards rates. The Pipefitters Union maintains delicate truce with the Hanged Men after the Dry-Dock 3 incident, where union rifles killed Chain-Men attempting extraction from protected operations. Luka Fen paid weregild rather than escalate—a decision that maintained peace while creating grudges the Iron Yards Boys still nurse.

**With the Ridge** to the south, the relationship is aspirational. The Cho gondola provides physical connection; social distance remains absolute. Ridge families view the Middens as source of salvage and labor, not as community deserving respect. Middens residents view the Ridge as proof that survival isn't enough—that stability, respectability, and technical expertise can produce something better than mere continuation.

**With the Rift** to the north and west, the relationship is maritime. Fishing boats and salvage vessels provide food and economic opportunity that land-based extraction cannot reach. The Valve-Saint Parish serves this population, its tidal worship schedule accommodating workers whose lives follow harbor rhythms rather than sun cycles.

# **The Guilds and Organizations of the Middens**

# **The Sewer-Fisher Clans**

The sewer-fishers embody the Empire's central contradiction: dependence on populations it cannot acknowledge. They descend from Technical Caste survivors who avoided sterilization by retreating into infrastructure spaces where Bureau enforcers couldn't reach. Three centuries later, they remain genetically distinct, reproductively isolated, and essential to the systems keeping the Middens habitable.

Their bodies are built for confined spaces. Adults stand between 1.35 and 1.55 meters, with bone composition favoring cartilage over calcium. Slip-joint articulation allows voluntary skeletal compression through specialized joints, enabling passage through openings as narrow as 25 centimeters. The skull never fully sutures, maintaining fontanelles that allow cranial compression. Slate-grey skin carries a lipid-silicate coating providing chemical resistance. Void-black eyes, enlarged to a third of facial area, show tapetum reflection in low light. Blade-shaped ears extend backward for acoustic sensing in resonant spaces.

Their cognition processes mathematics as sensation rather than abstraction. They perceive when systems are unbalanced, feel fluid dynamics as physical intuition, recognize patterns without conscious calculation. This inheritance from Technical Caste ancestors enables interpretation of Mutterer vocalizations and maintenance of infrastructure that baseline understanding cannot parse. But the same architecture creates communication barriers—sewer-fishers cannot articulate the mathematical relationships they perceive to those lacking the cognitive substrate to receive them.

Without caste registration, they cannot legally work, pay taxes, or access Imperial services. The Bureau of the Creche suspects their ancestry but destroyed the genetic records that would confirm it, creating permanent surveillance without possible resolution. Every formal transaction flows through documentary fiction: the Salvage Guild's "temporary auxiliary workers" whose status has persisted for generations, the Pipefitters' "provisional apprentices" who will never advance, the Benru clan's unique registration allowing all other sewer-fisher economic activity to appear legitimate.

## **The Clans**

Six families control different aspects of the Middens' hidden economy. Each maintains specialized knowledge through ancestral treatises stored in Oracle remembrance shrines—practical documentation lacking theoretical framework, useful only to those with the cognitive architecture to apply it.

**Clan Loak** operates Lift-3 in Hollow-Egress, the cargo elevator providing exclusive access to salvage-rich underground areas. Matriarch Yuli Loak manages the lift itself; her mother Merra runs the eighth-floor kitchen that feeds salvage crews and gathers intelligence. The perpetual stew and the queue that forms before dawn create an information nexus where workers discuss business that Merra filters upward. Jak Loak possesses Black Calculus abilities—the capacity to induce selective forgetting in observers—providing unacknowledged protection for clan operations.

**Clan Benru** maintains the documentation allowing sewer-fishers to exist in systems that don't acknowledge their category. Patriarch Korel Benru manages relationships with the Salvage Guild and Bureau clerks; Chief Broker Nyma Benru handles the daily negotiations transforming illegal labor into legally acceptable paperwork. Their archive in Hollow-Egress produces temporary permits and identity documents satisfying inspection requirements through transparent fiction. Their household shrine holds Tatius Benru's actual Bureau registration certificate—one of three official documents acknowledging sewer-fisher ancestry.

**Clan Venn** supplies rigging throughout the district. Their workshops produce cable, rope, and assemblies other families cannot replicate. Patriarch Eldin Venn assesses metal fatigue by sound, hearing stress patterns predicting failure before visible signs appear. The clan's horizontal connections—cables and bridges deployed along routes invisible from Chain-Men positions—partially bypass the Hanged Men's vertical control.

**Clan Hasket** combines physical security with aquaculture through unusual composition. A drainage collapse three generations ago killed most of their little cousins; survivors began fostering stevedore orphans whose Ironback and Redback genetics provide physical capability traditional sewer-fisher bodies lack. The current clan is perhaps eighty percent big cousins, with leadership split between Brakk (external negotiations) and Tasme (technical operations). Both groups eat from the same pot, work the same mussel beds, and identify as Hasket regardless of genetic origin.

**Clan Sarn** controls deep access. Patriarch Vennas Sarn spends months at depths where pressure changes perception in ways he no longer tries to explain. When he surfaces, his skin shows lichen crusts, his eyes carry bioluminescent glow. The materials his clan brings up—alloys surface chemistry cannot replicate, components with impossible manufacture dates—raise questions the Sarns decline to answer. They maintain the gas-harvesting operation fueling Lift-3's engine.

**Clan Cho** bridges sewer-fisher and Fen authority. A marriage three generations past created the connection; their mixed genetics sometimes produce fertile crosses between bloodlines. Matriarch Anira Cho manages Lockworks oversight through mathematical intuition no formal engineer can match. Her niece Riva Fen-Cho sits on the Knot council, her hybrid biology—1.97 meters with sewer-fisher flexibility—valuable for infrastructure requiring both reach and compression. The clan controls Lantern-Heaps' electrical systems and the gondola to the Ridge.

# **The Hanged Men**

## **Overview: The Knot and the Hanged Men**

This document details the power structure and history of the Hanged Men, a criminal organization controlling water infrastructure in the Middens district of ya-Sattra. The organization evolved from revolutionary war veterans into a hereditary protection racket over three centuries.

## **The Knot: Seven Seats of Power**

### **1\. The Chairman: Luka Fen**

**Age:** 42  
 **Territory:** The Gallows proper, overall Hanged Men authority  
 **Bloodline:** Seventh-generation direct descent from Garren Fen I

At 2.40 meters, Luka's height comes from Springheel elongation rather than simple scaling—he’s double-jointed in every direction, creating proportions that seem wrong until you see him move. His face abandons baseline humanity entirely: exterior ears absent, nose reduced to flattened vents, mouth a lipless seam that barely moves when speaking. Pale Warborn nodules ridge his brow and dot his jaw corners like partially erupted ivory, while his oversized knuckles bear matching bone tubercles. His black waxed-canvas coat bears dozens of brass and ceramic buttons as trim, each taken from a defeated rival, and the circular Knot badge at his throat depicts an inverted hanging figure with arms spread wide.

Luka occupies the seat through hereditary succession but maintains it through careful balance. His Springheel-dominant genetics grant him the physical capability to outmaneuver challenges from Garren, while his genuine Penitent faith provides moral legitimacy that older Valvehands respect. He understands infrastructure through both practical knowledge and inherited wisdom, making him irreplaceable to those who grasp how fragile the Middens' water system truly is. His morning inspections with Riva aren't performance but actual technical work. His protection of Curate Aram isn't sentiment but calculation about martyrdom's consequences.

### **2\. The Iron Yards Commander: Jarek Fen**

**Age:** 39  
 **Territory:** Iron Yards salvage operations, industrial extortion  
 **Bloodline:** First cousin to Luka, competing branch of the main line

Standing 2.50 meters tall but lean as a drawn blade, Jarek's frame suggests a weapon stretched on a forge until only function remains. His face is even more inhuman than Luka's—the nose merely a flat ridge with minimal vents, ears just depressions in the skull, mouth a lipless slash that forces words through clenched teeth. Chain-link scarification runs from cheekbone to jaw in raised black ridges, each representing a year of Iron Yards command. His functional leather clothing has been treated with industrial sealants until it's essentially armor, while the polished iron rod he carries serves as pointer, baton, and weapon depending on need.

Jarek controls the Hanged Men's primary revenue stream through systematic extraction from salvage operations. His bottomless cynicism means he sees all complexity as fraud, all expertise as gatekeeping, all limits as weakness. He can eat dinner while Chain-Men pull fingers off debtors because suffering is just noise unless it affects revenue. His lean Springheel-Warborn hybrid build makes him dangerously quick but not stronger than Luka. He relies on Garren's violence while positioning himself as the rational alternative to both Luka's restraint and Garren's chaos.

### **3\. The Chain-Men Commander: Garren Fen XI**

**Age:** 37  
**Territory:** Bright-Spire, enforcement operations  
**Bloodline:** Warborn-Dominant, second cousin to Luka

At 2.85 meters and 160 kilograms, Garren represents the Ironbone variant's terminal expression—overlapping pale bone plates cover his brow, cheeks, jaw, shoulders, forearms, and knuckles like natural armor grown wrong, ridging outward in shell-like scales. His face has abandoned human proportionsentirely: no ears at all, just smooth bone; nose reduced to permanently flared ventilation slits; mouth locked half-open by his oversized jaw. His skin between the plates has the texture and color of tarnished pewter, cross-hatched with fight scars, while the chain-link tattoos from cheek to jaw incorporate actual metal fragments beneath the skin.

Garren represents the terminal generation of Ironbone breeding—2.85 meters of bone-plated violence that will be dead from cancer by forty. He experiences his Warborn rage as divine inspiration rather than neurological dysfunction, believing his violent impulses are wisdom rather than misfiring ganglia. He's killed three wives. The Knot’s covered it up. His Chain-Men follow him because he's the biggest monster in a band of monsters. Jarek points him at problems, and Garren breaks them, unable to conceive of solutions that don't involve breaking.

### **4\. The Lockworks Captain: Riva Fen-Cho**

**Age:** 36  
 **Territory:** Water infrastructure, technical operations  
 **Bloodline:** Half-Fen, half-sewer-fisher, probably infertile

At 1.97 meters, Riva appears stretched and compressed simultaneously—elongated limbs attached to a torso that seems perpetually folded in on itself, creating the impression of someone permanently navigating a space too small for them. Her graphite-grey skin carries a petroleum sheen that causes water and machine oil to bead and roll off, the coloration extending uniformly across her entire body including lips and nail beds. Her eyes are full black sclera with gun-metal grey limbal rings that catch light like reflective tape, while her blade-long ears sweep tight against her skull, pierced with ascending rows of copper rings which chime subtly when she turns her head quickly.

Riva exists as living proof that biology doesn't determine destiny while being entirely constrained by her biology. Her unique hybrid genetics—Springheel height with sewer-fisher compression ability—make her irreplaceable for maintaining infrastructure that requires both reach and the ability to squeeze through child-sized passages. Her arthritis worsens daily, adding urgency to the fact that she has no successor. She's loyal to Luka alone because he's the only Knot member who understands that when she fails, everything fails. The others see her as a useful anomaly; he sees her as the lynchpin.

### **5\. The Master of Counting House: Ygren Fen**

**Age:** 65  
 **Territory:** Financial operations, tribute collection oversight  
 **Bloodline:** Springheel/Ironbone line, distant cousin

At 2.05 meters, Ygren shows the softening of age overlaying a frame built for loading cargo—broad shoulders now supporting accumulated weight, powerful hands adapted to turning ledger pages through fingerless gloves. His face carries diluted Fen modifications: nose flattened to vents but not as severely as Luka's, small ears reduced but still present, fine Warborn nodules along his brow subtle enough to pass for unusual bone structure in poor light. His drab ledgersman's coat and brass abacus bracelet project deliberate respectability, though the slight limp from an old chain-break fracture and the lead-weighted cosh hidden in his coat hem hint at his enforcer past.

Ygren operates as the Knot's institutional memory and accountant. His son Derren's rise in the Salvage Guild represents his only hope for legitimacy, driving his conservative position in Knot politics. He'll support whoever maintains stability because instability threatens his son's advancement. He drinks alone at night, reconciling what he does against the revenue which buys his son’s escape.

### **6\. The Night Watch Captain: Sera Fen-Galen**

**Age:** 31  
 **Jurisdiction:** Intelligence, surveillance, information control  
 **Bloodline:** No Fen blood—married to Ruk Fen (Luka's younger brother)

Sera's baseline humanity makes her alien among the Knot—at 1.78 meters and 62 kilograms, she's unremarkably human in a council of genetic extremes, her athletic build coming from climbing the Middens' vertical geography rather than engineered advantages. Her face achieves perfect forgettability: pleasant but not beautiful, distinctive but not memorable, the kind of features that witnesses struggle to describe afterward. Only her pale blueirises with faint reflective rings suggest variance. Her charcoal coat conceals hidden pockets for two Lectors while the silver cipher-wheel bracelet that appears decorative actually serves as her Lens-issued encoding device, its rings rotating throughout the day as she updates encryption keys.

Sera is the only baseline human on the Knot, positioned through marriage rather than blood. As the Galen family's connection to the Hanged Men and the Lens Bureau's Asset LS-4-113, she operates as a triple agent whose primary loyalty is to continued operation. She receives the weekly Gray Sheet briefings from the Lens, filtering intelligence to serve multiple agendas simultaneously. Her lack of genetic stake in Fen succession makes her paradoxically trustworthy—she'll betray everyone equally based on rational calculation rather than family loyalty. She takes contraceptives from three suppliers to avoid the pregnancy that would compromise her mobility or create leverage others could exploit.

### **7\. The Empty Seat**

**Former Territory:** Unknown/Disputed  
 **Vacancy Duration:** Twelve years

The seventh seat has remained empty since Korvain Fen died in a "construction accident" that everyone knows was murder but no one can prove. He'd controlled gambling and prostitution operations in the southern tenements, territories that were quickly absorbed by Jarek's Iron Yards operations and various Valvehand crews.

The seat remains vacant because filling it would require consensus the Knot cannot achieve. Luka wants someone who'd balance Jarek's aggression. Jarek wants an ally to tip votes his way. Garren doesn't understand why they need seven when six works fine. The other captains prefer the current ambiguity to any specific resolution.

The empty seat has become a useful fiction. It's a position to promise potential allies, a threat to existing captains (anyone could be replaced by filling the seventh seat and removing them), and a convenient excuse for deadlock ("we can't make such a major decision without a full Knot"). Some younger Fens campaign for it, not understanding that the campaigning itself disqualifies them—anyone who wants it that obviously would disrupt the careful balance that keeps the Knot functional.

## **The Hanged Men During the Malpais War: From Conscripts to Revolutionaries**

### **The Conscription and Battle**

During the war against the Child of the Malpais, the Empire faced a crisis that forced abandonment of traditional military doctrine. The Child—that failed experiment combining Highborn intelligence with Warborn strength, exiled to the Thousand Kingdoms and presumed dead—had returned with revolutionary military innovations that shattered Imperial forces repeatedly. In desperation, the Empire issued emergency conscription orders that pulled in every able-bodied human, including the mixed-caste laborers of the Middens.

The original Garren Fen commanded a unit composed primarily of Ironback dock workers and Springheel scouts—men whose genetic modifications made them useful for specific military roles but who'd never been considered soldier material. These conscripts were assigned to hold the outer defensive arrays of the City of Glass, essentially sacrificial positions expected to buy time while regular Imperial forces regrouped.

Instead, they held for three days. The mixed unit's caste advantages—Ironbacks anchoring positions with their natural bone armor, Springheels conducting devastating harassment raids with their jumping ability—proved unexpectedly effective against the Child's tactics. They were part of the desperate defense that lasted long enough for the sewer-fishers to activate something within the City of Glass itself, that mysterious intervention that turned the tide.

### **The Refusal to Disarm**

When the Child's forces finally withdrew and the war ended, Imperial command issued standard demobilization orders. Return weapons, return to previous status, pretend the emergency conscription never happened. Garren Fen and his veterans understood what this meant—they'd proven that mixed-caste units could be militarily effective, that armed laborers could defeat enemies the Empire's Warborn couldn't handle alone. They would be seen not as heroes but as threats.

The refusal wasn't dramatic. Fen's unit simply didn't report to the demobilization points. They kept their weapons—mostly crude rifles the Mutterers had mass-produced, nothing sophisticated but proven effective. When military police arrived to enforce the order, they found several hundred armed veterans who'd held against the Child's forces and weren't interested in surrendering to bureaucrats.

### **The Brief Revolt**

The "revolt" lasted less than a week and involved no actual combat. The veterans fortified positions in the industrial district, made clear they weren't surrendering their arms, and waited. The Empire, exhausted from the war, faced a choice: fight the soldiers who'd just helped save the City of Glass, or negotiate. The City-of-Glass Charter (or Compact) that emerged from these negotiations represented a compromise—the veterans could keep their weapons and form local militias, but Warborn production would be strictly limited, weapons manufacture regulated, and certain restrictions placed on those with Warborn genetics.

### **Return to the Middens**

The Fen veterans returned to the Middens as heroes to the local population but threats to the Bureau of the Creche. During the war, the Creche's enforcement had lapsed. Genetic registration hadn't been updated. Unregistered variants had armed themselves and proven their worth. The Creche saw a narrow window to reassert control before these precedents solidified.

The pogrom began within days of the veterans' return. Creche Warborn—specialized for urban pacification rather than battlefield combat—deployed to conduct "registration enforcement." They began hanging residents from bridge cables, the traditional Creche method of public intimidation. The bodies hung inverted, visible from every tenement window, a reminder of what happened to genetic undesirables who forgot their place.

### **The Eighteen-Hour Resistance**

The returning veterans responded with military efficiency. They distributed themselves throughout the district's vertical geography, established defensive positions, and when the Creche forces entered in strength, they fought back with the weapons they'd refused to surrender. The Creche Warborn, trained for controlling unarmed populations, found themselves in running firefights against veterans who'd just fought the Child of the Malpais and won.

The resistance lasted eighteen hours. The veterans used every tactic learned in the war—overlapping fields of fire, harassment and withdrawal, exploitation of vertical terrain. When the Creche finally withdrew, they left behind their collaboration with Rod Bureau overseers who'd manipulated water supplies to flush out resistance positions. The veterans executed these overseers on the same bridges where residents had been hung, then seized the Lockworks administrative building.

### **Taking the Name**

The name "Hanged Men" was adopted not in celebration but in memory. The veterans cut down the bodies of residents the Creche had hung, documented their names, and took the inverted hanging figure as their symbol. They would be the Hanged Men—those who survived where others hadn't, who turned an image of oppression into one of resistance.

The building they seized became known as the Gallows, and from this headquarters, they established themselves as the district's new authority. They'd defended the Middens when the Bureaus couldn't or wouldn't. They controlled the water infrastructure that kept the district alive. They had weapons and the proven will to use them.

### **The Transformation**

Initially, the Hanged Men operated as genuine community defenders. They'd fought for the Empire, been betrayed by it, and defended their neighbors from the Creche. They collected contributions rather than taxes, maintained infrastructure rather than exploiting it, provided actual security services rather than protection rackets.

But three centuries is a long time. Each generation inherited a little less revolutionary legitimacy, a little more institutional power. The descendants of heroes became hereditary rulers. The community defenders became extractive parasites. The weapons kept to prevent another pogrom became tools of oppression against the very population they'd once protected.

The current Hanged Men invoke their origins when convenient—Luka still references the "militia origins" when justifying their authority. But they've become exactly what their founders fought against: an oppressive force extracting tribute from a marginalized population, maintaining power through violence and infrastructure control, different from the Creche only in that they emerged from the community rather than being imposed upon it.

## **Crews: The Valvehands**

### **The Dual Nature of the Band**

The Valvehands serve as the Hanged Men's most essential band, combining technical infrastructure work with the daily business of extraction. They're the ones who knock on doors at dawn demanding water fees, who maintain the pumps that justify those fees, who know every family's payment history and every pipe's repair schedule. This dual role—part technician, part collector—makes them the face of the Hanged Men that most residents actually encounter.

The band skews older, typically thirty-five to fifty, because the work requires both technical knowledge and social intelligence that younger enforcers lack. You need to know when a family genuinely can't pay versus when they're holding out. You need to understand which infrastructure complaints are real emergencies versus which are stalling tactics. You need enough physical presence to be taken seriously but enough restraint to avoid triggering the kind of desperate violence that comes from pushing people with nothing left to lose.

### **Composition and Hierarchy**

Perhaps half the Valvehands are some variety of Fen—distant cousins, married-in relations, bastard children acknowledged just enough to use the name. The other half are competent locals who've proven themselves useful enough to trust with collection routes and valve keys. This mixed composition creates interesting dynamics. The Fen-blood Valvehands have family stake in maintaining the system. The non-Fen members have technical skills that make them irreplaceable despite lacking genetic loyalty.

Men like Hennis represent the non-Fen technical specialists—a pure-line stevedore whose father taught him pump maintenance, who joined the Valvehands because it paid better than dock work and came with protection the Salvage Guild couldn't offer. He'll never sit on the Knot, never have real authority, but his knowledge of pressure dynamics makes him more valuable than many blood Fens.

Then there's someone like Dek Fen, one of Luka's third cousins, who can barely read a pressure gauge but knows every family in his collection district by name, their payment histories, their problems, their breaking points. He inherited a collection route the way others inherit trade skills, learning from his father which doors to knock on softly and which require the cargo hook's weight against the frame.

### **The Collection Rounds**

The Valvehands' morning routes follow water's path through the district. They start at the top floors where the wealthy-enough pay premium for consistent pressure, work down through middle floors where payment plans are negotiated, end in the floods where people pay in salvage, labor vouchers, or increasingly desperate barter.

Old Kern, fifteen years a Valvehand, describes the calculation: "You knock different for different people. New residents get the official knock—firm, professional, like you're doing a job. Old residents who always pay get the courtesy tap—they know what you're there for. Problems get the hook-knock, metal on wood, so they know this isn't a social call."

### **Three Generations of Stability**

The Knot's relative stability under three generations of Fen leadership has shaped the Valvehands' institutional culture. They remember Luka's grandfather, who squeezed out the last independent water operations. They served under Luka's father, who formalized the collection operation and payment structures that still exist. Now they work for Luka, who doesn’t innovate, and everyone likes it that way..

This continuity creates a professionalism unusual in gang operations. Valvehands think in terms of decades rather than seasons. They maintain infrastructure expecting to still be using it in twenty years. They cultivate relationships with residents' children, knowing those children will inherit their parents' debts and payment histories.

Sev, who joined under Luka's father, explains the generational perspective: "Luka's grandfather took the water by force. His father made it profitable. Luka keeps it functional. Each generation inherits what the previous one built, tries not to break it worse than it was."

### **How They See Luka**

To the Valvehands, Luka represents continuity rather than leadership. He's the third iteration of a system they've been operating for decades, neither savior nor tyrant but simply the current manager of an established machine. They appreciate his technical knowledge—he genuinely understands the plumbing—but more importantly, he understands the social infrastructure that makes collections possible without constant violence.

When Luka reduces fees for genuine hardship, the Valvehands see pragmatism rather than mercy. Dead residents don't pay anything. Desperate residents sabotage infrastructure. The calculation is practical: extract the maximum sustainable rate, not the maximum possible rate. Luka understands this because he was raised watching his father and grandfather make the same calculations.

His morning inspections with Riva aren't performances of diligence but actual technical work that needs doing. The Valvehands appreciate that he doesn't pretend collections are anything other than extortion, doesn't dress it up with ideology or pretense. He's a gang leader managing a protection racket built on a hydraulic monopoly. The honesty, paradoxically, makes him easier to follow than someone who'd insist on noble purpose.

### **The Succession Problem**

What terrifies the Valvehands isn't change but specific change. They've survived three generations of Fen leadership because each generation understood that water infrastructure and the social systems around it require careful maintenance. Jarek's contempt for technical knowledge, his certainty that sewer-fishers are lying about complexity, his belief that everything he doesn't understand is a scam—these attitudes would destroy in months what took fifty years to build.

Some older Valvehands have begun informal conversations about what happens "after." Not rebellion—they're too invested in the current system—but adaptation. How to maintain essential infrastructure when no one in leadership understands it. How to continue collections when the authority structure collapses. How to preserve the technical knowledge that keeps water flowing when the social knowledge of how to extract payment without triggering riots is lost.

Merk, senior among them, frames their position: "We're not loyal to Luka personally. We're loyal to fifty years of functional system. Luka happens to be the only one who understands that breaking that system breaks everything, including the Knot itself."

### **The Daily Reality**

A Valvehand's day starts before dawn, checking overnight pressure logs, identifying which sectors need adjustment, which pumps ran rough, which cisterns are low. By sunrise they're on collection rounds, knocking on doors in carefully calculated sequences, extracting payment through combinations of threat, negotiation, and occasionally genuine technical service.

Afternoons bring maintenance work—replacing gaskets, clearing blocked lines, cranking up the flow for an apartment that’s finally out of arrears, the technical labor that justifies their existence beyond pure extraction. Evenings are for updating the books, comparing notes with other collectors, identifying problems before they become crises.

They're criminals, certainly, but criminals who've professionalized their criminality into something approaching civil service. They extract payment for water that should cost less, but they also ensure water actually flows. In the ecology of the Middens, they occupy the niche between pure predation and actual service, forever balanced on the edge of both.

## **Crews: The Iron Yards Boys**

### **Extraction**

The Iron Yards Boys operate on principles of crystalline simplicity: generate maximum revenue with minimum complications. Unlike the Valvehands who must balance extraction with infrastructure maintenance, or the Chain-Men who exist primarily as a threat, the Iron Yards Boys run an actual profit center. They control the salvage yards where archaeotech and industrial scrap flow through the district, extracting percentages from every transaction, every cut, every sale.

These aren't the Hanged Men's traditional thugs but industrial extortionists who understand commodity prices, salvage values, and the economics of desperation. They know which salvage crews are skimming, which are holding back choice pieces, which are one bad week from collapse. More importantly, they know how to extract value without killing the productivity that generates it.

The band draws from a different pool than other Hanged Men forces—younger men, typically twenty to thirty-five, ambitious and cynical enough to appreciate Jarek's worldview. Many are failed Salvage Guild apprentices who couldn't afford certification, dock workers who got tired of being cheated, or simply clever locals who recognized that taking percentages paid better than earning wages.

### **Composition and Character**

The Iron Yards Boys include surprisingly few Fens. Jarek doesn't care about bloodline—he only cares about profit. A capable baseline human who can accurately assess salvage values is worth more than a Fen cousin who can't distinguish brass from gold. This meritocracy of greed creates a different culture from other Knot bands. Advancement comes from generating revenue, not from genetics or seniority or violence.

Men like Chill represent the typical Iron Yards Boy—a former assessor from the Salvage Guild who got caught skimming and lost his guild card. Instead of starving, he approached Jarek with a proposition: he'd identify which salvage crews were underreporting their finds in exchange for a percentage of the recovered value. Now he runs a network of informants among the cutting crews, knowing which teams found archaeotech before they even leave the yards.

Then there's Brass Tomm, who earned his name by always knowing the real composition of salvage. He can tell by sound, by weight, whether something's actually valuable or just dressed up to look it. Salvage crews hate him because he's never wrong, can't be fooled, and takes his percentage based on the actual value.

### **The Operation**

The Iron Yards operate on the shift rhythm. The Boys arrive with the morning shift at 5:30, positioning themselves at yard entrances to track what comes in. They don't need to threaten—their presence is threat enough. Salvage crews know that underreporting means broken fingers as a straightforward consequence, delivered without emotion or anger.

By mid-morning, the Boys are walking the cutting floors, observing which teams are working what salvage, noting who's trying to conceal components in scrap metal, identifying archaeotech that someone's hoping to pass off as waste. They don't intervene immediately—that would slow production. They just note discrepancies. Later, they reconcile.

Afternoons bring the actual collections. Teams line up to sell their morning's work, and the Iron Yards Boys stand beside the Guild buyers, taking their percentage before the sale. The smart crews pay honestly and promptly. The stupid ones try to negotiate, not understanding that negotiation implies options. There are no options.

**Management** 

Jarek runs the Iron Yards like a corporation with a violence department. He holds morning briefings where Boys report overnight intelligence—which ships arrived, which crews are planning cuts, which Guild journeymen are trying to bypass taxation. He reviews reports with the same attention Ygren gives to Knot finances.

His payments are prompt and generous When Chill identifies a hidden archaeotech cache worth thousands of obols, he receives his promised percentage immediately, in cash, publicly. When Brass Tomm prevents a crew from passing off pig iron as titanium, his cut arrives on the day. This reliability creates more loyalty than fear ever could.

Jarek's personal involvement stays minimal. He doesn't dirty his hands with collections or enforcement—that's what the Boys are for. He appears for strategic decisions, major disputes, or when example needs to be made. Otherwise, he manages through lieutenants who understand that their continued prosperity depends on his leadership.

### **Jarek**

To the Iron Yards Boys, Jarek represents the future—not ideologically, but practically. His complete lack of sentiment appeals to men who've been disappointed by every institution they've encountered. Violence is just process. Not personal. Not emotional. Necessary. This flatness feels honest to men who've betrayed by every cause they’ve pretended to believe in.

Kray, one of Jarek's lieutenants, explains the appeal: "Luka asks us to pretend we're providing service, maintaining order, protecting the community. All lies to make extraction feel noble. Jarek just says 'take the money.' No pretense, no justification, just honest theft. It's refreshing."

### **Dry-Dock 3**

The incident that almost triggered a gang war has become defining trauma for the Iron Yards Boys. They'd identified a salvage crew copper from a crashed hauler—thousands of obols worth of pure metal the crew claimed was "already spoken for" by the Pipefitters. Brass Tomm knew bullshit when he heard it. The copper was clean, unclaimed, and the Boys deserved their forty percent.

The crew refused payment. The Iron Yards Boys followed standard procedure—called in six Chain-Men to make an example. Garren's boys arrived with their usual equipment: chains, clubs, that absolute certainty that their heritage made them immortal. They walked into Dry-Dock 3 expecting to break some arms, maybe crack a skull, establish proper order.

Pipefitters were waiting with rifles.

Not energy weapons. Not blades that would skip off bone plating. Heavy-bore, bolt-action cannons which put slugs through anything. Two Chain-Men died immediately. Warborn genetics can't stop a bullet through the brain. Four more were wounded badly enough that they couldn't fight. The Iron Yards Boys, watching from what they'd thought was safe distance, scattered like roaches.

### **Luka**

The humiliation should have triggered immediate retaliation. In Garren's binary worldview, the equation was simple: they killed ours, we kill theirs. Jarek saw it similarly, though his calculation was economic—allowing the Pipefitters to establish violent precedent would undermine extraction from every union-protected operation.

But Luka paid the weregild instead. A thousand obols in electrical tokens to each dead Pipefitter family, formal acknowledgment that the Hanged Men had overreached, agreement to respect union protection of essential salvage operations. It prevented Bureau intervention—a gang war would draw Lens attention, might trigger military response, could destroy everything they'd built.

The Iron Yards Boys experienced this as betrayal. They'd identified legitimate extraction. They'd followed procedure. Chain-Men had died enforcing their claim. And Luka's response was to pay the families and say some salvage operations were off-limits? It confirmed everything Jarek had been telling them about Luka's weakness.

### **Grudge**

The Iron Yards Boys still track every union-protected salvage operation, calculating lost revenue, documenting what they see as theft. They maintain a shadow ledger of what they're "owed"—not just the obols from that initial incident but every subsequent extraction the Pipefitters have blocked.

Chill keeps a particular tally: "Seventeen thousand obols in copper wire. Eight thousand in brass fittings. That's our money sitting in Pipefitter warehouses because Luka's too weak to take it."

The Boys have developed elaborate fantasies about what happens when Jarek takes over. They'll hit every union operation simultaneously. The Chain-Men will come armed with guns, ready for projectile weapons. The Pipefitters will learn that industrial solidarity means nothing against organized violence.

## **Crews: Chain-Men**

### **The Perfect Life for Terrible People**

Garren’s Chain-Men have built themselves a paradise of consequence-free violence. They're huge, armored in bone, practically invulnerable to conventional weapons, and backed by an organization that not only permits but rewards their brutality. They don't need complex philosophy or biological excuses. They hurt people because they enjoy it, take what they want because they can, and live without consequences because who's going to stop them?

The Blink exists, yes—those ganglia fire and make violence easier, faster, more instinctive. But plenty of Chain-Men feel the Blink and choose not to act on it when it would inconvenience them. They don't snap on each other over card games because that would disrupt their own entertainment. They don't destroy the bars where they drink because then they'd have nowhere to drink. The violence they commit is selective, targeted, chosen. The Blink might suggest it, but they're the ones who decide.

The morning combat isn’t about establishing necessary hierarchy—they're about starting each day with sanctioned violence, getting the blood flowing, reminding themselves how good it feels to hurt someone who can't hurt you back. Even these fights are rigged. Garren always wins because anyone who might actually challenge him knows they'd die later. The hierarchy is determined by agreement to pretend the combat matters.

### **Frat House**

The Chain-Men barracks in Bright-Spire aren't military housing but a continuous party where violence is entertainment. They've got stolen furniture, looted alcohol, "comfort women" who know that leaving means their families suffer, and zero responsibility.

The space reeks of stale alcohol, sweat, and violence. Broken furniture that could be replaced isn't, because the breaking was fun and the broken pieces remind them of good times. Blood stains on walls stay because they're trophies. The medical facility isn't for treating Chain-Men—they heal in days—but for keeping victims alive long enough to be entertaining.

They tell each other stories about their violence. Remember when Krug held that debtor upside down until his eyes bled? Remember when Big Rem made that family watch while he broke every bone in their father's hands? Remember when they hung those Pipefitters from the bridge—not dead, just hung there until their shoulders dislocated? These aren't traumatic memories. They're the highlight reel.

### **Concubines** 

The women in the Chain-Men barracks aren't there by choice. Some are explicitly traded by families who can't pay tribute—a daughter for a debt. Others are just taken because a Chain-Man decided he wanted them. They last a few months usually, before violence or despair or "accidents" remove them from the picture. Then the Chain-Men get new ones.

The Chain-Men don't even pretend these are relationships. They're just another form of consumption, like alcohol or food or violence—appetites to be satisfied without thought for consequence. When Garren beat three wives to death, the other Chain-Men didn't see tragedy. They saw a man enjoying his property until it broke, then getting new property. The surprise was that anyone thought it was worth mentioning.

The children that occasionally result from these arrangements are problems to be disposed of. If they're obviously future Chain-Men, the mother might get exiled to raise a new bastard to join the barracks. If not, they disappear. The Chain-Men don't want families. They don’t want futures. They want an eternal present of violence.

### **How They See Garren**

To the Chain-Men, Garren is the biggest bully in a gang of bullies. He gets first pick of everything good and last blame for everything bad. They follow him because he lets them do whatever they want as long as they do what he wants first. It's not respect. He’s living the life they aspire to. Just more so.

When Garren kills someone for serving him wrong food, the Chain-Men don't see the Blink misfiring. They see a man who can face zero consequences. When he stands confused by Luka's restraint, they're seeing  genuine bewilderment that anyone would choose not to take what they can take.

Big Rem puts it simply: "Garren gets it. This life is short. Shorter for us. Why pretend to be good? Why hold back? We're going to die at forty anyway. Might as well enjoy every second of power we get."

### **Insult**

When the Pipefitters killed two Chain-Men, it wasn't an existential crisis about vulnerability. It was rage that someone had successfully said "no" to them. The Chain-Men are used to being untouchable, not because of their armor. Because of the terror. The Pipefitters demonstrated that Chain-Men could be defied.

The grudge they nurse isn't about their dead brothers. Chain-Men die all the time—from cancer, from fights, from overdoses, from finally pushing someone too far. The grudge is about disrespect. The Pipefitters should have submitted, should have accepted that they take what they want. Instead, they fought back and won.

Every day that the Pipefitters avoid retaliation is another day the Chain-Men are reminded that their reign of terror has limits. That's what they can't accept. Not the deaths—death is inevitable. But the defiance? That threatens the entire structure that lets them live as monsters.

### **Predation**

The Chain-Men's daily routine is structured around maximizing opportunities for consequence-free cruelty. Morning stair tolls aren't about revenue—it's about the power to make people pay to move through their own buildings, the daily reminder that life requires permission. They charge different amounts to different people based on whim, just to watch faces fall when today's toll is double yesterday's for no reason.

The afternoon "enforcement" is just hunting for excuses to hurt people. Someone's payment is short? Break fingers. Someone looked at them wrong? Break face. Someone walked too fast, too slow, too confidently, too fearfully? Break something. The justifications are retroactive—violence happens first, then they figure out what the victim did to deserve it.

Evenings in the barracks are for drinking, fighting, raping, and planning tomorrow's cruelties. They share intelligence about who has pretty daughters, who's accumulated enough wealth to be worth robbing, who showed defiance that needs correcting. It's not organized crime—it's organized sadism with crime as a side effect.

# **The Valve-Saint Parish**

The parish survives by being useful to everyone while threatening no one. It provides services the Bureau cannot acknowledge and intelligence the Hanged Men need, creating space for mutual aid within systems designed to prevent it.

Pumping Station \#7 continues moving water through the district's substrate despite three centuries of deferred maintenance. The parish occupies the overflow chamber at sea level—a cavern designed for storm surge, now repurposed for worship. Salvaged benches face the wheel altar, a four-meter valve wheel that tradition claims once saved the building from catastrophic flooding. Engineers confirm it connects to nothing. The story provides meaning that technical accuracy cannot replace.

The floor uses original drainage grates. During spring tides, seawater seeps through—never more than ankle-deep but enough that congregants learn appropriate footwear. Above the congregation, Mutterers have colonized the machinery spaces, modifying components in ways correlating with improved pump efficiency.

Curate Aram es-Thabit documents everything—not from paranoia but experience. Two years ago, a Bureau inspector claimed the parish was selling donated grain. The ledgers proved otherwise. The documentation habit remained. His sermons address immediate concerns: contaminated water sources, changes in Bureau policy, navigation of gang territories. When discussing exploitation, he names incidents without naming perpetrators. "The family on Ladder Seven paid three separate tolls to reach the clinic." Luka listens without expression; by Monday the Chain-Men have received new territorial assignments.

Four deacons handle different functions. Mer Hasket manages the grain dole, his arithmetic laborious but determined, converting human need to mathematics that might reveal solutions. Sela Loak runs the kitchen, adjusting portions by labor type—more carbohydrates for dock workers, more fat for sewer-fishers. She also runs the prayer circle in Bright-Spire, the barely concealed resistance whose "tithing" funds weapons and whose "confession" exchanges intelligence. Kesh Ritt operates the clinic in regulatory grey zones, his provisional Companion status technically covering Mutterer management but not the suturing and bone-setting occupying most of his time. Brann Olt maintains security through visible presence—his part-Warborn genetics and maintained weapons discourage casual extortion. Everyone knows Luka assigned him here.

Services run Tuesday and Thursday evenings, Sunday mornings, timed to shift schedules and tidal patterns. Luka arrives early each Sunday, taking the same position in the third row. His lieutenants scatter throughout the congregation for intelligence gathering, noting who attends and who receives community support.

The parish serves three thousand people whose documentation ranges from questionable to nonexistent. The capitation tax—forty obols per person per quarter—creates constant financial pressure. Luka needs the intelligence, the legitimacy, the weekly reminder that his power has limits. The Bureau needs someone managing a population they prefer not to acknowledge. The congregation needs structure for mutual support that would exist anyway but might lack coordination.

The wheel altar accumulates another week's carved prayers. The pumps shift pitch for reasons no one documents anymore. Services continue.

# **The Aesthetics of the Middens**

## **Overview**

The Middens looks nothing like the Medina Quarter's merchant prosperity or the Inner City's ancient bronze grandeur. Where those districts cultivate impressions—geometric precision, chromatic restraint, preserved magnificence—the Middens simply exists. The buildings accommodate survival rather than impressing visitors. Materials come from whatever salvage provides. The color palette emerges from corrosion, accumulated grime, and the particular grey of structures that have absorbed harbor damp for generations.

The Rift dominates the visual field from any point in the district. Looking north or west, dark water extends to the horizon, its surface marked by fishing boats, salvage vessels, and the occasional larger ship navigating toward the main harbor facilities. The tenements rise from this waterline in silhouettes that tell experienced eyes everything about internal power—the Gallows' height marking administrative authority, Bright-Spire's taper suggesting compression, Knife-Edge's lean visible even at distance.

A visitor from the Medina Quarter might find the Middens ugly, chaotic, uncomfortably damp. A resident sees home—a place where survival matters more than appearance, where caste categories blur into functional cooperation, where the systems that maintain life require constant attention rather than passive consumption. The Middens exists because someone has to live at the bottom, and the people who live there have made something that works.

---

## **The Landscape**

The Middens occupies foundations that predate Imperial records, pre-Interdict structures modified across centuries for purposes their builders never anticipated. The original construction—whatever it was—provided the stability that allows seven tenement buildings to rise from soil that would otherwise swallow them. The foundations extend below the waterline, their lower levels permanently flooded, their upper levels perpetually damp.

Water defines the landscape more than any architectural feature. The Rift's dark water laps at building foundations, fills canals between tenements, rises during high tide to flood ground-level streets. Rain falls frequently, channeled through collection systems that supplement the Hanged Men's controlled supply. Fog rolls in most mornings, burning off by midday only during summer months. The air carries salt, always, along with industrial smoke from Knife-Edge and the particular smell of decomposing organic matter that accumulates in any place where water and people coexist.

The gradient from water to stability runs north-to-south. The northern and western edges face the Rift directly, their foundations extending into harbor water. The southern edge rises toward the Ridge, that fallen arcology providing backdrop against which the tenements appear small despite their height. The eastern edge blurs into the Iron Yards, the quarried depression of salvage operations beginning where the Middens' flooded foundations end.

---

## **Architecture**

The tenements share common materials despite their different characters. Pre-Interdict foundations provide stability—smooth grey surfaces that resist water penetration, joints sealed through processes no one can replicate. Above these foundations, construction uses whatever salvage provides: shipping containers stacked and welded, industrial modules repurposed for residence, hull plates from decommissioned vessels covering frameworks of timber and scrap metal.

The Gallows shows the most deliberate aesthetic: rust-red paint covering large wall sections in geometric patterns, teal horizontal bands marking floor levels, copper alloy plates carved with interlacing designs framing major doorways. These decorations signal organizational identity rather than wealth—the Hanged Men's colors visible throughout the district, marking territory as surely as the crane that extends from the building's peak.

Bright-Spire shows no decoration at all. Grey metal and rust-orange corrosion cover surfaces assembled from whatever materials came available during each phase of construction. The plates are random—hull sections beside door panels beside structural elements—unified only by their universal neglect. The narrowing is visible from ground level, each floor smaller than the one below, the geometry of compression made architectural.

Hollow-Egress demonstrates what cooperation can achieve. The building maintains better condition than its neighbors, not from wealth but from consistent attention. Five clans share responsibility for different systems, their combined investment producing maintenance that individual families couldn't sustain. The industrial elevator housing at the building's center, that massive shaft descending through pre-Interdict foundations into depths no one fully maps, shows the pragmatic engineering that keeps critical infrastructure functional.

Sump-Gate extends into the harbor, its lower structure jutting beyond the building's footprint to create the space that the Valve-Saint Parish occupies. The overflow chamber's grated floors, the pump housings visible through inspection hatches, the constant thrum of machinery—these industrial elements become architectural features through long habitation. Residents have learned to find beauty in function, to appreciate the massive wheel altar despite (or because of) its mechanical irrelevance.

Lantern-Heaps glows at night, the Cho family's electrical monopoly made visible through lights that other buildings cannot match. The rainbow signage marking their territory appears garish against the district's prevailing grey, deliberate contrast advertising what consistent power provides. The building itself shows the improvised construction common throughout the Middens—shipping containers stacked six high, industrial modules converted to residence, frameworks of scrap metal supporting additions that the original structure never anticipated.

Knife-Edge leans. The fifteen-degree angle, visible from anywhere in the district, marks the building more distinctly than any decoration could. Everything about the structure accommodates the lean—windows installed at compensating angles, doorframes built rhomboid rather than rectangular, staircases switching direction at landings to prevent accumulated slope from becoming unclimbable.

Vetch-Rise lacks defining character, its improvised construction reflecting the transient population it houses. Each floor was added when need demanded, using materials available at that moment. The result shows in structural irregularity—floors of different heights, load-bearing walls that don't align vertically, staircases following expedient rather than logical paths.

---

## **Color Palette**

The dominant colors emerge from environment and material rather than choice. Rust covers every ferrous surface, ranging from fresh orange-brown on recent installations to deep black-red on structures that have corroded for decades. Grey pervades everything—the grey of weathered metal, the grey of salt-stained concrete, the grey of fog that hangs in the air most mornings. Tarnished copper shows verdigris where drainage concentrates moisture. Exposed timber weathers to silver-grey where sun reaches it, to black where perpetual damp promotes fungal growth.

The Hanged Men's colors—rust-red and teal—appear throughout the district on surfaces the organization claims. The combination provides recognizable identity without requiring expensive pigments: rust-red approximates natural corrosion, teal uses copper-based compounds available from salvage streams. The colors mark territory, warn visitors, and remind residents whose authority governs their lives.

The Cho family's rainbow breaks the prevailing grey with deliberate excess. Their signage—each color representing a different service tier, a different price point, a different level of electrical access—advertises what monopoly provides. The contrast with surrounding buildings makes the message impossible to miss: the Chos have what everyone else lacks.

Personal coloring varies with caste and circumstance. Sewer-fishers show the pale skin and enlarged eyes of Technical Caste heritage, their features marking them as something other than baseline despite generations of mixing. Stevedore orphans fostered into Clan Hasket show Ironback density and Redback coloring that their adopted siblings lack. The Chain-Men's Warborn bone plating extends from shoulders toward skulls, visible beneath skin that shows the cancerous discoloration their genetics guarantee.

---

## **Sound**

Water provides the constant backdrop. Lapping against foundations, dripping from condensation, flowing through pipes whose sounds indicate system status to experienced ears. The Rift's waves create rhythm that residents incorporate into daily life, timing activities to tidal cycles that affect ground-level flooding.

Knife-Edge's hammering carries throughout the district, constant during working hours, its rhythm providing temporal reference when fog obscures the sun. Workers time conversations to gaps between strikes, developing speech patterns that accommodate industrial noise.

Lantern-Heaps contributes the sixty-hertz hum of transformers and electronics, audible from adjacent buildings, providing the sonic signature of electrical power that elsewhere requires silence to notice.

The Mutterers' mathematical vocalizations drift from Sump-Gate, their perpetual calculations creating white noise that the Valve-Saint Parish has incorporated into worship. The chimes that interrupt their cascading choruses—necessary to prevent dangerous optimization attempts—mark time more reliably than any clock.

Voices echo off metal and water in ways that stone construction cannot replicate. Sound carries farther than visual lines suggest, warning residents that conversations intended as private may reach unintended ears. The paranoia this creates shapes social interaction—important discussions happen in specific locations known to absorb sound, their acoustic properties as valuable as their physical security.

---

## **Visual Markers of Identity**

Caste recognition follows different rules in the Middens than in formal Imperial districts. The Bureau of the Creche's registration system doesn't acknowledge most variations that residents display; the categories that matter locally have no official existence.

Sewer-fishers recognize each other through subtle markers—the enlarged eyes that indicate Technical Caste heritage, the joint flexibility that allows navigation through tight spaces, the particular pallor of skin that rarely sees direct sun. These markers cross official caste boundaries, creating kinship categories based on capability rather than registration.

Hanged Men identification follows organizational hierarchy. Valvehands carry the valve-key badges that grant access to infrastructure. Iron Yards Boys display salvage-related credentials—Guild tokens, assessor certifications, the documentation that legitimizes their presence in extraction operations. Chain-Men need no identification beyond their bodies—Warborn bone plating and the cancerous discoloration it produces mark them as effectively as any badge.

Religious markers appear throughout the district. Prayer beads that might be sapphire-glass knives. Valve-wheel pendants marking Valve-Saint congregation membership. The subtle iconography of Sela's prayer circle—revolutionary imagery disguised as Penitent devotion, visible only to those who know what they're seeing.

Wealth markers barely exist. In a district where everyone struggles, conspicuous consumption attracts attention no one wants. The exceptions—Cho family lighting, Knot leadership quarters, the occasionally visible luxury of someone temporarily flush—stand out precisely because they're exceptional.

# **Businesses of the Midden**

## **Lift-3**

The massive cargo elevator providing exclusive access to the salvage-rich underground areas beneath Hollow-Egress. The lift shaft descends through pre-Interdict infrastructure into tunnel networks that extend beyond anyone's complete mapping. What comes up—archaeotech components, strange alloys, materials with properties that shouldn't exist—feeds the Middens' entire salvage economy.

**Yuli Loak** controls operations, her burn-scarred face testimony to commitment: hot lubricant from a feedline explosion, an accident that would have killed someone less determined. She inherited management from her mother Merra, who now runs the kitchen that feeds the crews. The lift runs on methane harvested from deep tunnels by Clan Sarn, creating interdependence between families that keeps the operation stable.

The lift's scheduling determines who profits from salvage. Priority slots go to crews with Loak relationships; others wait, sometimes for days. This control makes the Loaks essential to everyone working the underground while creating resentment they must constantly manage. The engine room adjacent to Merra's kitchen requires continuous maintenance—the machinery is pre-Interdict, irreplaceable, understood through inherited knowledge rather than technical documentation.

Lift-3 isn't the only access to the depths, but it's the only safe access. Other routes require sewer-fisher anatomy to navigate, or pass through areas where the air itself becomes dangerous, or descend into territories where Clan Sarn trades with whatever lives below. For baseline humans hauling salvage, Lift-3 is the only option.

## **The Benru Desk**

The documentation operation making the Middens' shadow economy legally visible, operating from the fifth floor of Hollow-Egress. Every morning, workers arrive to collect temporary permits, work authorizations, and identity documents produced from stamps, paper, and careful fiction. The same workers appear under different names in different ledgers, but the forms satisfy inspection requirements.

**Nyma Benru** handles daily negotiations, particularly the complex documentation transforming illegal contract labor into legally acceptable paperwork. She's thirty-four, chief broker for the clan, spending her days converting human desperation into bureaucratic categories the Empire can process. A salvage worker without caste documentation becomes a "temporary auxiliary worker" attached to a registered Guild operation. A sewer-fisher performing maintenance becomes a "provisional apprentice" in perpetual training.

The desk charges fees scaled to transaction complexity. Simple identity verification costs a few obols. Creating documentation for someone who technically doesn't exist costs considerably more. The Salvage Guild pays the Benrus a retainer for maintaining the fiction that their workforce is legally employed; this arrangement benefits everyone except the workers themselves, who remain permanently provisional regardless of decades of service.

The clan's archive behind the desk holds generations of accumulated paperwork—not just current documents but historical records providing templates for whatever documentation a customer might need. Somewhere in those files is Tatius Benru's actual Bureau registration certificate, one of three documents officially acknowledging sewer-fisher ancestry.

## **The Benru Counting House**

The financial terminus of the Benru documentation network, located just outside Vetch-Rise in technically Iron Yards territory. The position is deliberate: close enough to the Middens for easy access, far enough to fall under different jurisdictional assumptions. The Hanged Men's writ weakens here; the Salvage Guild's strengthens. The counting house operates in the gap between authorities.

**Korel Benru** oversees operations personally, though his son Dran handles daily transactions. The building—a squat two-story structure of salvaged brick—processes the actual money flowing through the clan's documentation business. Fees collected at the Desk in Hollow-Egress arrive here for counting, conversion, and distribution. Payments to the Salvage Guild, the Hanged Men, and various Bureau clerks whose cooperation the system requires all flow outward from these ledgers.

The counting house also handles escrow for transactions too large or too sensitive for direct exchange. A salvage crew selling something valuable might deposit payment here, releasing funds only when the buyer confirms receipt. The Benrus take a percentage for this service and accept the risk of holding other people's money in a district where theft is constant. Their reputation for honoring escrow agreements—built over generations—makes them irreplaceable for high-value trades.

Dran Benru is twenty-eight, trained in arithmetic by his father, developing the same talent for remembering numbers that characterizes the clan's cognitive inheritance. He lacks his father's diplomatic skills but compensates with meticulous record-keeping. The ledgers he maintains exist in triplicate: one for the Bureau if they ever inspect, one for actual operations, one hidden in locations only Korel knows.

# **Food and Drink**

## **Merra's Kitchen**

The definitive food operation in the Middens, occupying Hollow-Egress's eighth floor adjacent to the Lift-3 engine room. The kitchen's proximity to the engine provides year-round warmth, and the height means the canal's sewage smell and the smoke from lower-floor trash fires don't reach it. The space has expanded over decades from Merra's personal cooking area into a mess hall feeding every salvage crew dependent on Lift-3.

**Merra Loak** is fifty-seven, former council chair for the Loak clan, now running an operation that makes her simultaneously untouchable and indispensable. The perpetual stew—miso-barley-mussel base with whatever protein and vegetables the clan's networks acquire—has fed the Middens' working population for decades. The queue forms before dawn, positions determined by complex calculations: who found what below, who owes whom, which clans are cooperating this week.

The kitchen's real function is intelligence. Workers discuss business over meals, assuming the background noise covers their conversations. It doesn't. Merra remembers everything—which crews found valuable salvage, which families are struggling, which Hanged Men are pushing too hard on collections—and filters relevant information to her daughter Yuli, who runs Lift-3 operations. The scratched salvage tables seat perhaps forty; hundreds cycle through daily.

No one attacks the person who provides tomorrow's meal. This protection extends partially to the entire Loak clan. Disrupting Merra means disrupting food supply for the salvage economy's workforce.

## **The Canted Anchor**

A drinking house on Knife-Edge's ground floor, built into the building's lean so thoroughly that the bar counter runs level while the floor slopes fifteen degrees. First-time visitors stumble; regulars develop the characteristic Knife-Edge gait without noticing. The establishment serves fermented grain alcohol and whatever protein the kitchen acquired that morning—usually mussel fritters or eel skewers from the Hasket beds.

**Nils Carro** runs the place, a near-baseline man in his fifties whose family has held the lease for three generations. He maintains neutrality between factions, which means everyone uses his back room for negotiations they'd rather not conduct publicly. He charges for privacy by inflating drink prices during "meetings." His wife Desta handles the kitchen.

The Anchor's value lies in its position between Knife-Edge's committee politics and the broader Middens economy. Salvage assessors drink here after shift; forge workers eat here before. The conversations between these groups create an informal labor market operating faster than official channels.

## **Broth and Bolt**

A soup kitchen in a converted maintenance closet on Hollow-Egress's fourth floor, run by the Benru clan as community service that doubles as intelligence operation. The space holds perhaps twelve people standing. The soup changes daily based on what the clan's networks acquire.

**Vela Benru**, Korel's youngest daughter, ladles soup and listens. The midday queue brings residents from every floor, creating vertical mixing that otherwise rarely occurs. Someone from the second floor mentions Chain-Men activity; someone from the seventh mentions Lift-3 scheduling. Vela remembers everything and reports to her father.

The operation loses money consistently but the Benrus consider it investment in social capital. When they need favors from another floor, they've usually fed that person's children.

## **The Wet Step**

A tavern on Sump-Gate's ground floor, where the floor is perpetually damp and the water level rises noticeably during high tide. Patrons learn to lift their feet or accept wet boots. The establishment serves harbor workers, pump maintenance crews, and anyone whose business involves the Middens' maritime economy.

**Oram Dasker** owns the place, a former merchant sailor who lost his ship to a storm and his savings to Yen Tam lending. Now sixty, he tends bar with philosophical resignation. He waters the alcohol less than most Middens establishments and extends credit to regulars whose ships haven't come in yet.

The back room hosts the informal harbor exchange, where fishing captains negotiate crew shares and salvage divers bid on recovery contracts. Oram takes a small percentage for facilitating introductions.

## **Ketta's Brazier**

A food stall at the Transient Market's more stable edge, where Vetch-Rise meets the path to Hollow-Egress. The setup is minimal: charcoal brazier, wok, cutting board, and whatever ingredients Ketta acquired before dawn. The menu consists of fried grain with vegetables and protein, adjusted for availability and customer's payment.

**Ketta Sorn** is Astal—rust-colored scales, enlarged thermal pits—who came to the Middens eight years ago from circumstances she doesn't discuss. She works alone, saves everything, and maintains one of the few constants in the Transient Market's chaos. Chain-Men leave her alone; rumor says she once fed Garren Fen when he wandered through drunk.

The stall serves as informal message drop for Astal refugees. Ketta connects people without payment, considering it obligation to others sharing her circumstances.

# **Repair and Services**

## **Tam Venn's Rigging Loft**

A workshop on Hollow-Egress's sixth floor where **Tam Venn**—Eldin's nephew—runs the clan's primary rope and cable retail operation. The loft stocks everything from fishing line to structural cable. Certified cable, tested through the Venns' apparatus and stamped with their mark, costs triple uncertified stock but comes with implicit guarantee.

Tam is thirty-eight, married to a Hasket cousin, and serves as the clan's primary face for small transactions. Unlike his uncle Eldin, who can hear metal fatigue, Tam's talents run toward negotiation. He extends credit to established accounts and occasionally forgives balances when doing so creates useful obligation.

The loft also serves as hiring hall for Venn riggers. Construction projects requiring vertical work check with Tam about crew availability.

## **The Salvage Priests**

A Mutterer handicraft assessment operation on Sump-Gate's third floor, where **Kora Hasket** and her team sort through debris from Mutterer nests, separating useful objects from worthless modifications. The work requires patience, technical knowledge, and tolerance for the Mutterers themselves.

Kora is forty-five, a big cousin whose stevedore strength proves useful when restraining agitated Mutterers. She learned assessment from a Companion who died of fever, inheriting his disruption chimes and partial methodology. She's developed reliable categories: things that hum, things that warm, things that glow, things that do nothing obvious but sell to Ridge collectors, and things that interest no one.

Her actual interest involves documenting which Mutterer modifications correlate with improved pump efficiency. She suspects they understand the building's systems better than anyone.

## **Needle and Patch**

A clothing repair shop on Bright-Spire's fourth floor, where **Shira Osten** repairs work clothes and maintains the fiction that she's merely a seamstress rather than a key node in Sela's prayer circle.

Shira is fifty-three, baseline, widowed when her husband died in a Chain-Men "toll dispute" twelve years ago. She stitches by day and passes messages by night, using legitimate traffic—customers dropping off and retrieving garments—to cover communications. A specific thread color indicates message waiting. A particular patch placement signals meeting time.

Her actual sewing is competent but unremarkable. Her value lies in position: close enough to Chain-Men territory that reaching her requires passing checkpoints, far enough that they don't monitor her constantly.

## **Old Kem's Metalwork**

A repair shop on Lantern-Heaps' second floor, where **Kem Dorran** fixes anything mechanical that isn't electrical—the Cho clan handles electrical work exclusively. Kem is seventy-two, hands steady despite age, with burn scars covering his forearms. He came from the Iron Yards after losing his Guild certification to a political dispute he won't explain.

His shop handles small jobs: tool repair, lock work, blade sharpening. He charges fairly, works quickly, and asks no questions about why someone needs a pry bar modified or a blade rehardened.

The back room stores items Kem repairs but customers never retrieve. After a year unclaimed, they become his inventory. Some customers deliberately abandon items this way, using Kem as a fence without the term being spoken.

# **Trade and Exchange**

## **The Honest Weight**

A pawnshop on Hollow-Egress's ground floor, where **Renna Vo** assesses items and extends loans against collateral with terms that are exploitative but transparent. The shop's name is a joke—Renna uses honest scales precisely so her dishonesty in other matters won't seem suspicious. She weighs metals accurately and calculates interest at rates that compound to ruination.

Renna is forty-one, near-baseline, originally from the Iron Yards. She'll assess radiation-tagged salvage, stolen goods with identifying marks, even artifacts that might technically belong to the Bureau of the Lens.

Her arrangement with the Benrus provides legal cover; her arrangement with the Hanged Men provides security. She pays both and maintains careful records, understanding survival depends on being useful to everyone while beholden to no single faction.

## **Three Corners Exchange**

A currency conversion operation in the Gallows' ground floor, nominally independent but actually controlled by Ygren Fen's financial apparatus. The shop converts between Imperial obols, Knife-Edge salvage chits, Valvehand water credits, Yen Tam scrip, and a dozen other local currencies at rates favoring the Hanged Men.

**Mira Fen** runs daily operations, a distant cousin who earned her position through mathematical ability. She's twenty-nine, standing only 1.90 meters—short for the family. Her height relegated her to administrative work rather than enforcement, a fate she's converted into advantage by making herself irreplaceable.

The shop maintains a small-loans sideline competing with the Yen Tam Society, offering worse rates but simpler terms. Borrowers who can't navigate Yen Tam's ya-Tsatsan cultural requirements sometimes prefer dealing with Mira despite the cost.

## **The Drowned Archive**

A document recovery operation in Sump-Gate's basement levels, where **Sarif Benru**—Korel's grandson—leads a team salvaging paperwork from waterlogged lower floors. The operation recovers contracts, property records, and identification papers from centuries of accumulated debris.

Sarif is twenty-four, trained by his grandfather but drawn to physical recovery work rather than clerical processing. He and three sewer-fisher cousins navigate flooded chambers, extracting papers from sealed containers and waterproof cases.

The legitimate business involves recovering records for families seeking property claims or identity proof. The actual value lies in forgery potential—genuine paper, genuine ink, genuine formats, requiring only careful modification. Sarif handles recovery; Korel handles modification; the arrangement remains unspoken.

# **Specialty Goods**

## **The Saint's Portion**

A religious goods shop adjacent to Valve-Saint Parish, where **Dessa Tam** sells prayer candles, offering tablets, and devotional texts. The shop operates on margins so thin that Dessa supplements income through message delivery, package holding, and discreet introductions.

Dessa is thirty-five, baseline, from a ya-Tsatsan family that assimilated generations ago. She maintains loose Yen Tam connection through distant kinship but doesn't participate in their operations. Her shop serves as neutral ground where Yen Tam representatives and Hanged Men can conduct business without losing face.

The religious goods are genuine, sourced from Ridge chandlers and Medina Quarter printers. The shop's value lies in its other functions.

## **Glass and Gleam**

A jewelry shop on Lantern-Heaps' fifth floor, where **Ost Cho** deals in decorative items emerging from electronics salvage. Broken circuit boards yield gold contacts; damaged equipment contains silver wiring. Ost transforms these byproducts into jewelry.

Ost is fifty-one, carrying the Cho clan's graphite-grey skin. His hybrid heritage—Fen grandfather, sewer-fisher grandmother—makes him valuable for negotiations requiring both technical understanding and social authority. He prices jewelry based on customer's apparent wealth, charging Ridge visitors triple what Middens residents pay.

The back room handles items too valuable for display. Occasionally, salvage crews find intact pre-Interdict jewelry—gold untarnished across millennia, gems cut with impossible precision. Ost connects sellers with Ridge buyers and takes commission both parties consider reasonable.

## **Smoke and Vapors**

An inhalants shop on Vetch-Rise's third floor, where **Pol Durren** sells tobacco, poppy derivatives, and herbal preparations. The shop operates with Yen Tam permission—Pol pays them a percentage and sources poppy products exclusively through their network.

Pol is forty-four, near-baseline, a former Guild assessor who developed a taste for his own products. He's functional but visibly damaged, hands tremoring, attention drifting mid-sentence. The shop survives because the Yen Tam need retail distribution and Pol is too compromised to cause problems.

The establishment serves as gathering space for the Middens' addicted population. Pol extends credit he knows won't be repaid, creating debts the Yen Tam eventually collect through other means.

# **Lodging**

## **The Hooks**

A bunkhouse on Vetch-Rise's second floor, named for ceiling hooks where residents hang belongings to prevent theft while sleeping. **Mara Tisker** rents sleeping slots by the shift. A slot includes a thin mattress, latrine access, and the right to hang one bag from the ceiling.

Mara is fifty-eight, baseline, operating with grim efficiency. She tolerates no fighting, no excessive intoxication, and no theft—violations mean immediate expulsion. Her rules create order that attracts slightly better clientele than competing bunkhouses.

The establishment houses forty people per shift, with three shifts daily. Mara maintains a small private room where she sleeps in fragments between shift changes.

## **The Warden's Rest**

A rooming house on Hollow-Egress's third floor offering private rooms—actual walls, actual doors—for residents who can afford more than bunkhouse rates. **Kollis Hasket**, a big cousin, manages the property for his clan.

Kollis is thirty-three, massive even by stevedore standards, his presence sufficient security. He rents eight rooms to long-term residents, mostly skilled workers whose income supports privacy but not relocation. His tenants include a Guild assessor, forge supervisors, a Companion journeyman, and others whose occupations remain deliberately vague.

The rooms are small—three meters by four—but they have doors that lock and walls that muffle sound. For Middens residents, this privacy represents meaningful luxury.

# **Services**

## **The Numbers Woman**

**Avra Tal** operates from no fixed location, helping residents navigate bureaucratic requirements. She reads documents for the illiterate, completes forms for the confused, and translates between Imperial administrative language and practical understanding. Her fees scale to resources—wealthy customers pay full rate, desperate ones pay what they can.

Avra is sixty-one, educated in the Medina Quarter before circumstances brought her to the Middens thirty years ago. Her satchel contains reference materials, blank forms, and the stamps and seals that make unofficial documents appear official.

She maintains no permanent address, sleeping in different locations to avoid targeting. The Benrus provide occasional shelter; she provides occasional document work. Everyone benefits from her skills; no one controls her exclusively.

## **Clean Tam's Laundry**

A washing operation on Sump-Gate's second floor, using the building's water access to provide laundry service. **Tam Osley**—no relation to the Yen Tam family, despite confusion that has caused him considerable trouble—runs the operation with his wife Benna and three adult children.

Tam is fifty-five, baseline, originally from the Ridge before business failure forced him downward. He maintains Ridge standards of cleanliness, which creates reputation drawing customers throughout the Middens.

The family's competitive advantage involves their Valvehand arrangement: reduced water rates in exchange for intelligence about customers. A family suddenly washing finer clothes might have money; a regular reducing laundry volume might be struggling. Tam passes this information reluctantly, understanding the bargain keeps his water affordable.

## **Bone-Setter Marsh**

**Yenna Marsh** provides medical services from a room on Lantern-Heaps' third floor, handling injuries too minor for Kesh Ritt's clinic but too serious for self-treatment. She sets broken fingers, stitches cuts, drains infections.

Yenna is forty-seven, with enough Companion training to understand anatomy but not enough for Guild membership. She was expelled from apprenticeship for reasons involving a patient who died—details vary by storyteller. She's competent for routine work, charges less than legitimate practitioners, and doesn't report injuries to any authority.

Her room serves workers who can't afford to miss shifts and can't risk documentation. She treats, they pay, no records exist.

# **Writing in the Middens**

The Middens rewards attention to the physical. Characters climb constantly—to reach anything safe, anything valuable, anything private. They descend to work, to trade, to worship. This vertical geography shapes bodies and habits: the tilted gait of Knife-Edge residents who've internalized fifteen degrees of lean, the calculated breathing of salvage workers ascending nine flights after a shift, the way everyone in Bright-Spire turns sideways at doorways even in open spaces.

Water provides both setting and plot. A scene in the Middens should establish where the water is: lapping at foundations, dripping from condensation, flowing through pipes, pooling in basement levels, seeping through the Valve-Saint Parish floor during high tide. Characters notice water pressure, water quality, water access—not because they're obsessed but because these details predict whether tomorrow will be manageable or disastrous. When the Hanged Men want to punish someone, they don't need to send Chain-Men. They adjust a valve.

Sound carries information before vision does. The hammering from Knife-Edge marks time. The transformer hum from Lantern-Heaps marks wealth. The pitch-shift of pumps at Sump-Gate marks system status to ears trained to hear it. Characters listen for Chain-Men footsteps on metal stairs, for the particular splash of a boat approaching an unauthorized dock, for the disruption chimes that interrupt Mutterer vocalizations before they cascade into something dangerous.

The tenements have distinct characters that should shape scenes set within them. Hollow-Egress scenes involve cooperation among people who have learned to share power because the alternative is losing everything—five clans maintaining equilibrium through constant negotiation. Bright-Spire scenes involve compression, surveillance, and the specific psychology of people who cannot escape observation—2,700 residents passing through checkpoints controlled by 83 men who will die of cancer by forty. Sump-Gate scenes involve water and machinery and religious accommodation of industrial reality—worship happening against the constant thrum of pumps that no one fully understands.

Characters in the Middens have generally fallen as far as they can fall, which changes what they want. Ambition points sideways rather than upward: better position within a tenement, better relationships with the clans that control access, better arrangements with the Hanged Men who control water. The few exceptions—the Cho family's electrical monopoly, the Benru clan's documentation operation—represent generations of accumulated advantage, not individual achievement. A new arrival cannot replicate what those families have built. They can only find a niche within the existing structure.

The violence is real but not constant. Chain-Men extract and brutalize, but they cannot occupy every space at every moment. The Hanged Men tax and threaten, but they need the population alive enough to produce value. Most scenes should involve the threat of violence rather than violence itself—the calculation of which route avoids checkpoints, the negotiation that might turn ugly, the debt that might bring collectors. When violence does occur, it should matter: a beating changes a character's week; a killing changes a building's politics.

Religious practice in the Middens has adapted to industrial reality. Curate Aram's sermons address contaminated water sources and gang territorial assignments. Deacon Sela's prayer circle in Bright-Spire disguises weapons as religious objects and revolutionary iconography as devotional imagery. The wheel altar at the Valve-Saint Parish probably never controlled the pumps, but the story of the Redback stevedore who turned it provides meaning that technical accuracy cannot replace. Characters participate in these practices for reasons that range from genuine faith to community connection to practical necessity—the grain dole requires showing up.

Caste categories blur in the Middens in ways that official registration cannot capture. The sewer-fishers carry Technical Caste inheritance that the Bureau of the Creche suspects but cannot prove. Clan Hasket's big cousins are fostered stevedore orphans whose Ironback and Redback genetics have nothing to do with their sewer-fisher siblings except shared meals and shared identity. The Chain-Men's Warborn bone plating marks them as something the military caste system produced but cannot acknowledge. Characters should carry this mixed heritage visibly—the enlarged eyes, the joint flexibility, the chemical skin lesions, the cancerous discoloration—without needing to explain it. The Middens exists because caste categories fail to account for what people actually are.
