# Taho: Gateway City of Contradictions

## Executive Summary

Taho sprawls across the western slopes above former Lake Tahoe at 6,200 feet elevation, the largest city in the Republic of Ganat with 700,000 inhabitants. Built where Taho Pass crosses the Sierra Nevada, the city controls the primary trade route between Republican territory and the Undying Empire. Everything about Taho embodies contradiction: revolutionary citizens trading with the imprisoning Empire, Dusk Party merchants governed by a Dawn Party arch-curate, cosmopolitan sophistication coexisting with frontier revolutionary rhetoric, wealth accumulation justified through theological interpretation that makes profit into atonement. The city is simultaneously the Republic's commercial heart and its ideological embarrassment—prosperous, pragmatic, essential, and deeply suspect to everyone who lives anywhere else.

---

## Geography and Climate

Taho sits on granite slopes overlooking what was Lake Tahoe, now a smaller lake 400 feet lower than historical levels due to glaciation-era climate shift. The city occupies west-facing mountainside between 6,200 and 6,800 feet elevation, with the commercial port district at lake level and residential districts climbing the slope in terraces. The Sierra Nevada rises behind to over 9,000 feet. Taho Pass to the west provides the lowest crossing point for hundreds of miles, making Taho the natural gateway between desert and coast.

The climate is harsh. Winter brings 200 inches of snow, temperatures to \-10°F, months when the pass closes completely. Summer reaches 85°F but nights drop to freezing even in August. The growing season is maybe 90 days. Nothing about this location suggests building a city here except the geographic necessity of controlling the pass.

The lake itself sits in a granite basin carved by glaciers, surrounded by peaks that catch snow and release it gradually. The water is extraordinarily clear—you can see 70 feet down in calm conditions. The clarity results from minimal nutrients and low algae growth at altitude. The lake provides year-round water, fish, and trade route connections to settlements along its shores, but it's cold enough to kill you in minutes if you fall in.

Granite dominates everything. Grey stone fractured into massive blocks, weathering through exfoliation that creates curved surfaces and rounded boulders. The granite here is coarse-grained, crystals visible to the naked eye: quartz (glassy, translucent), feldspar (white or pink), mica (flaky, reflective). In direct sun the mica sparkles across building faces. The stone is extraordinarily hard, difficult to work, but essentially permanent once placed.

The pass to the west—Taho Pass—rises to 7,000 feet before descending toward Imperial territory. The road through it is pre-Interdict construction, granite blocks fitted so precisely that no mortar was needed and after millennia the joints remain tight. Maintaining this road is central to Taho's existence. When the pass closes in winter, the city depends entirely on stored supplies and lake trade. When it opens in spring, caravans move both directions carrying the commerce that makes the city wealthy and necessary.

## The Electrified City

Taho blazes with light. Every building—from wealthy merchant compounds in the Upper City to workers' tenements in the Port District—has electric lighting and electric heating. Streetlights line every major thoroughfare, their amber glow turning the granite surfaces gold at night. The wealthier streets have lights every thirty feet. The poorest have them every hundred. But they have them.

This universal electrification is Taho's pride and its most visible demonstration of what Republican commerce achieves. The power comes from multiple sources: hydroelectric turbines on streams feeding the lake, water wheels on the lake itself, even some salvaged Imperial generators in merchant kilit compounds. The distribution network is copper wire strung on wooden poles or run through conduits in newer construction, creating a web that connects every building to power.

Power is not free. Households pay monthly fees based on usage, measured by mechanical meters that merchant kilits install and maintain. But power is provided at cost—not for profit. This is where competitive philanthropy becomes visible. The Khen-Masot Enterprise operates three hydroelectric plants and provides power to fifteen thousand households. Their monthly statement includes prominent notice: "Service provided at 15% below operational cost. The subsidy is 2,847 dinars this month."

The Dresh-Okan Consolidated operates five plants and provides power to twenty thousand households. Their statement reads: "Service provided at 18% below cost. Subsidy: 4,102 dinars this month." The competition isn't subtle. Merchant families brag at social gatherings about their subsidies. They commission stone plaques outside their facilities listing cumulative public investment. They publish annual reports documenting how much collective capacity their commerce funds.

This creates strange dynamic where even the poorest workers have electric heat and light provided by merchant kilits who use that provision as demonstration of their wealth and generosity. A dock worker paying 3 dinars monthly for power that costs 3.5 dinars to provide is receiving charity while the charitable party announces it loudly as proof of revolutionary virtue.

The streetlights are maintained collectively—different kilits responsible for different districts, competing to keep theirs lit most reliably. When lights fail in one district and remain lit in another, everyone notices. The kilits repair their sections quickly not just for public service but for reputation. The Port District might be poor, but its streets are lit as reliably as the Upper City's because the Dockers' Fraternal and associated labor kilits maintain them obsessively.

At night, Taho seen from the surrounding mountains looks like fallen stars—thousands of points of amber light climbing the terraces, concentrated in the Port District and Upper City, sparse in between. The city glows. Visitors from Ghemar-yan or eastern settlements arrive after dark and stop on the road, staring. This is what Republican commerce built. This is what accommodation with the Empire purchases. The lights make the contradiction visible.

## The City Structure

Taho divides vertically by class and function, geography making hierarchy unavoidable despite ideology. The Port District at lake level handles trade, warehousing, and industrial work. The Merchant Terraces climb the slope in five distinct levels, each 100-150 feet higher than the last, connected by granite staircases and switchback roads. The Upper City at 6,800 feet holds the academy, the arch-curacy, and the wealthiest residential districts. Goods flow downhill by gravity. People flow uphill for status. The division is functional and social, marking hierarchy the Republic officially rejects but geography makes inescapable.

### The Port District

The Port District occupies two square miles of relatively flat ground at 6,200 feet where streams from the mountains reach the lake. It's industrial, dense, noisy. Warehouses built from granite blocks with timber roofs house goods moving both directions: Imperial manufactures coming east, Republican raw materials and reverse-engineered products going west. The warehouses are massive—some 200 feet long, 80 feet wide, 40 feet high—because seasonal trade requires storing six months of goods when the pass is open.

Electric lights blaze from every warehouse door and loading dock. At night the Port District is bright as day—merchant kilits keep lights burning because nighttime loading and unloading is often necessary, and showing you can afford to keep lights on constantly demonstrates both wealth and commitment to productivity. The Salvage and Cartage Consolidated maintains lights on their docks 24 hours daily throughout trading season. The plaque outside their main warehouse reads: "Lighting subsidy for public safety: 892 dinars monthly."

The docks extend into the lake on pilings driven into granite bedrock. Boats tie up here: flat-bottomed cargo vessels with square sails and sweep oars, fishing boats with nets and traps, occasional Imperial factor vessels that come through the pass then cross the lake rather than use roads. The boats are working craft, maintained for function rather than beauty, but even they show electrification's reach—running lights on every vessel, required by harbor regulations the Dockers' Fraternal enforces.

Manufacturing happens here too. Smithies working salvaged Imperial metal and local copper. Textile workshops weaving maguey-silk imported from warmer elevations. Carpentry shops building the furniture and tools that supply the rest of the Republic. Every workshop has electric lighting—no more working by oil lamps or limiting operations to daylight hours. The productivity increase from universal lighting is measurable and dramatic. A textile workshop can run two shifts instead of one. A smithy can work after dark in winter. The lights make commerce more efficient, which makes merchant kilits wealthier, which funds more subsidies, which makes everyone dependent on the system.

The population here is mixed: Serrulata dock workers whose desert adaptations are useless at altitude but who do the work anyway, near-baseline humans in technical trades, Ranga refugees working warehouse jobs for citizenship, occasional Akama doing seasonal labor. The Port District is where the Republic's caste-blind ideology meets economic reality—everyone works for wages, but wages vary by what work your body can do and what training you can afford.

Housing in the Port District is dense: multi-story granite buildings with shared courtyards, exterior staircases, minimal insulation. But every unit has electric heating elements—copper coils that warm when powered, mounted on walls or under sleeping platforms. The heating isn't luxury—winter at this altitude kills without it. The workers pay monthly for power, the merchant kilits provide it at subsidized rates, and everyone lives with the awareness that revolutionary commerce keeps them warm.

The buildings themselves are older construction, Sultanate period, with modifications. The granite walls are carved with bas-reliefs—geometric patterns and figurative scenes. But if you look closely, you can see that some reliefs are carved deeper than others. The deeper carvings are newer: revolutionary scenes replacing monarchical ones. A panel showing tribute collection to the Sultana has been carved away and replaced with communal harvest. The replacement is carved 2-3 inches deeper because they had to remove the original first. The stone remembers even when people prefer to forget.

### The Merchant Terraces

Five terraces climb the slope, each developed in stages as the city grew. The lowest terrace—the First Terrace—dates to the Sultanate period: granite buildings with carved facades showing desert motifs, interior courtyards, underground storage. The Second and Third Terraces expanded during the late Sultanate as trade increased. The Fourth and Fifth Terraces are revolutionary-era construction, built in the past twenty years as commerce exploded.

Each terrace is 100-150 feet higher than the last, requiring switchback roads and granite staircases to connect them. The staircases are pre-Interdict engineering in the oldest sections: granite blocks forming steps that rise consistently, drainage channels preventing ice accumulation, walls on the downslope side preventing falls. Electric streetlights line the major stairways every fifty feet, maintained by different kilits competing to keep theirs most reliably lit. At night the stairs become rivers of light flowing upward through the city.

The terraces themselves are relatively flat areas maybe 400 feet deep created by cutting into the slope and building retaining walls. The walls are massive: granite blocks 3-6 feet per side, fitted without mortar in the oldest sections, mortared with lime in newer construction. Behind the walls, buildings rise 2-4 stories: ground floors for shops and workshops, upper floors for residences or offices.

The architecture shifts between terraces. First Terrace maintains Sultanate aesthetic adapted for Republican purposes: carved facades showing geometric patterns and figurative art, but the figures have been re-carved. Scenes of Sultana Ulayah receiving tribute now show collective assemblies deciding distributions. Panels depicting aristocratic hunting parties now show cooperative agriculture. The re-carved sections are deeper—you can trace the original forms underneath if you know how to look, see where they chiseled away monarchy to install revolution.

Higher terraces show Republican construction: less carving, more granite left natural, larger windows (glass when affordable), pitched roofs to shed snow, electric lines visible running along building faces where they haven't been buried in conduit yet. The Fourth and Fifth Terraces look unfinished compared to First—the granite isn't carved or plastered, the buildings are simpler geometric forms, the decoration is minimal. But they're fully electrified, which the oldest First Terrace buildings achieved only through expensive retrofitting.

### The Upper City

At 6,800 feet, the Upper City occupies a relatively flat area maybe half a square mile before the terrain rises too steeply for building. This is where wealth and power concentrate: the Academy, the Arch-Curacy, the headquarters of major merchant kilits, the residences of the commercial elite. And this is where competitive philanthropy becomes most visible—the streetlights here are spaced closer, maintained more obsessively, shine brighter. Multiple merchant kilits provide power to this district, and all of them want their service quality visible to Senate colleagues and business partners.

The Academy is pre-Interdict construction adapted to Republican purposes: a complex of connected buildings forming a rough square around a central plaza, 300 feet per side. The buildings are 3-4 stories, seamless fused ceramic in cream and grey, windows that were clearly designed for the structure rather than cut into it afterward. The Academy occupies buildings whose original purpose is unknown but whose construction quality is extraordinary and whose utilities still function partially—some rooms maintain temperature automatically, some have lighting that responds to presence, some have acoustics that amplify sound perfectly.

The classrooms, dormitories, library, and administrative offices represent the Republic's attempt at systematic education. The Academy trains military officers, engineers, administrators, teachers—the professional class the Republic needs to function without depending on Imperial expertise or traditional aristocracy. Students attend on scholarship from their kilits or personal funds if wealthy. The Academy installed its own generating capacity eighteen years ago—three turbines on the stream that runs through the complex, providing power independent of merchant kilit subsidies. This independence is itself political statement about what institutions should control what resources.

The Arch-Curacy sits adjacent to the Academy: a massive granite structure that was the principal Penitent church even before the Revolution, when the Sultanate maintained Penitentism as majority faith. The building is four stories, 200 feet by 150 feet, with carved facades that tell the history of Taho's religious evolution. The lowest reliefs are oldest: Penitent scenes from early Sultanate period showing human submission to divine judgment, acceptance of the Interdict as justified punishment, hope for eventual redemption through patient endurance.

Higher up, the carvings shift: late Sultanate period showing more active theology, humans demonstrating wisdom through works, material progress as evidence of growing capacity. And highest up, newest: revolutionary period scenes carved over monarchical imagery. You can see where scenes of Sultana's religious authority have been replaced with collective assemblies consulting sacred texts. The new carvings are 3-4 inches deeper than the surrounding stone, the granite removed to eliminate what came before and install what came after.

The interior combines centuries of religious practice in one space. The main sanctuary seats 800, with vaulted ceiling rising 60 feet, carved stone columns supporting weight, windows (now glazed, previously open) admitting light from south and east. The windows show stained glass—not original, installed piecemeal over centuries as techniques and resources permitted. The oldest glass is simple: colored panes creating geometric patterns. The newest is revolutionary: scenes of collective labor, figures working together, images that justify commerce through works-based theology.

Arch-Curate Tewodo Khamun delivers sermons from a granite pulpit carved with intertwined hands—symbolizing collective labor, shared burden, mutual support. The pulpit is 500 years old. The symbolism has been reinterpreted repeatedly. Originally it meant humans supporting each other through divine punishment. Then it meant workers cooperating for Sultanate prosperity. Now it means revolutionary solidarity and collective material progress. The stone hasn't changed. The theology attached to it transforms with each generation.

The merchant quarter fills the rest of the Upper City: large residences built around interior courtyards, multi-generational family compounds, buildings that serve as both homes and kilit headquarters. These structures are newest construction, post-revolutionary, built by families who profited from the revolution's disruption and subsequent stabilization. The buildings are substantial: granite walls 2 feet thick, timber framing imported at enormous cost, glass windows showing wealth, pitched roofs with proper drainage, electric heating in every room, electric lighting throughout. Some compounds have their own generating capacity. Others receive power from the family's own commercial electrical enterprise—the Khen-Masots provide their own power from their own generators in their own compound, demonstrating complete self-sufficiency while also providing subsidized power to fifteen thousand other households.

The contrast with Port District housing is absolute. A merchant family compound might house thirty people in 8,000 square feet of space with multiple fireplaces supplementing electric heat, proper kitchens, private chambers, interior courtyards with actual vegetation. A Port District building houses eighty people in half that space with shared kitchens, electric heating elements as primary warmth source, minimal privacy. But both have electric light and heat. The worker pays for power monthly from wages the merchant's enterprise provides. The merchant subsidizes that power. The system works. The contradiction persists.

## Economic Life

Taho's economy rests on a simple fact: the city controls the pass, and therefore controls trade between the Republic and the Empire. Everything flows through Taho. Every Imperial manufacture coming east. Every Republican raw material going west. The merchant kilits that organize this trade have grown wealthy enough to dominate Republican politics while maintaining they're just facilitating necessary commerce.

### The Imperial Trade

Caravans arrive from the west carrying Imperial goods: manufactured tools, salvaged archaeotech, lector components, medical supplies, weapons (sold despite official restrictions), luxury items for those who can afford them, and most importantly—components the Republic cannot yet manufacture itself. Bearings, precision instruments, electronic components, specialized alloys. The items that make Republican technology work despite Republican rhetoric about independence.

The caravans are substantial: twenty to fifty wagons pulled by teams of cattle (destriers won't cross the pass—the terrain breaks their legs), guarded by hired mercenaries, carrying goods worth thousands of obols. They arrive in spring when the pass opens and continue through fall until snow threatens. Winter means isolation.

Imperial factors operate from warehouses in the Port District. They're not Imperial citizens—the Empire doesn't send officials here—but merchants authorized to trade on behalf of Aureate families or Bureau enterprises. They negotiate with Republican merchant kilits, exchange goods for agreed prices, and carefully avoid discussing politics. The fiction is maintained that this is private commerce between independent parties, not state-to-state trade. Everyone knows it's fiction. The fiction is essential.

What the Republic exports: Copper and turquoise from mines in Ganati territory. Wool and leather from pastoral kilits. Preserved foods. Maguey-silk and maguey fiber products. Destrier stock (carefully screened bloodlines—the Republic won't sell its best). Archaeological salvage from sites the Empire can't access. And increasingly—reverse-engineered products. Ganati turbines, looms, tools that Imperial manufacturers won't sell but Republican engineers have learned to build from principles rather than patent-locked designs.

The exchange rates are carefully negotiated annually. A Republican dinar equals an Imperial obol in theory, but actual exchange depends on goods. Imperial manufactures command premium prices. Republican raw materials sell at commodity rates. The merchants conducting these negotiations make fortunes on the margins.

### The Merchant Kilits

Taho's economy is organized around merchant kilits, economic units that became political through the Republican system. The largest kilits control hundreds of thousands of obols in trade goods and employ thousands of people in warehouses, workshops, and transport.

The Khen-Masot Textile Enterprise employs three hundred workers in workshops spread across the Merchant Terraces. They import raw maguey fiber from lower elevations, spin it into thread, weave it into fabric, and produce finished goods for both Republican use and Imperial sale. The enterprise operates its own electrical generation—three hydroelectric plants on mountain streams—and provides subsidized power to fifteen thousand households. Their monthly subsidies are published prominently: "This month's collective service: 2,847 dinars below cost."

Factor Yula Khen-Masot manages daily operations. She's forty-six, Serrulata, from a merchant family that supported the revolution early and profited from it enormously. She wears Ganati merchant style: elaborate robes in teal and cream, copper jewelry set with turquoise, scales dyed in geometric patterns showing wealth and taste. She sits in the Senate representing her kilit. She attends Arch-Curate Tewodo's services weekly. She negotiates contracts with Imperial factors for selling maguey-silk fabric at prices that make her family wealthier while funding programs that make her reputation sterling.

This is the model: accumulate wealth through commerce including Imperial trade, reinvest that wealth in collective capacity (schools, training, infrastructure, subsidized utilities), maintain revolutionary credentials through conspicuous philanthropy, defend it all as demonstration of mankind's growing wisdom. Arch-Curate Tewodo preaches it. The Khen-Masots practice it. Everyone else watches resentfully while depending on the commerce it enables and the subsidies it provides.

### The Working Population and the Workingmens' Kilits Caucus

Below the merchant elite, Taho houses hundreds of thousands of people whose labor makes commerce work. Dock workers loading and unloading vessels. Warehouse workers moving goods. Transport workers driving wagons and maintaining roads. Workshop laborers spinning thread, weaving fabric, forging metal, building furniture. Domestic servants in merchant households. Market vendors. Street maintenance workers. The administrative clerks handling the paperwork that commerce requires.

These workers organize into their own kilits—smaller, less wealthy individually, but discovering collective power through coordination. The Dockers' Fraternal Kilit represents 2,800 dock workers and their families. The Warehouse Hands' Association represents 3,200 warehouse workers. The Carters' and Haulers' Collective represents 1,900 transport workers. The Workshop Federation represents 4,100 laborers across multiple trades. Separately, none of these kilits reaches the 8,000-member threshold for a Senator.

Eighteen months ago, they formed the Workingmens' Kilits Caucus—a coalition of seven labor kilits totaling 21,000 workers and families. The Caucus elects Senators collectively: enough members for three Senate seats, distributed by agreement among participating kilits. This year the Dockers' Fraternal holds one seat, the Workshop Federation holds one, and the Textile Workers' Association holds one. In two years they'll rotate according to Caucus bylaws negotiated collectively.

This organization shifts power dramatically. Merchant kilits still control more total Senate seats—the wealthy kilits are individually large enough for representation. But the Workingmens' Caucus votes as a bloc on issues affecting all labor. When they strike, they strike together. When they negotiate wages, they negotiate collectively. The merchant kilits complain but ultimately accommodate because coordinated labor stoppage shuts down all commerce.

The Caucus also maintains the streetlights in the Port District and lower terraces—their territory, their responsibility, their pride. When lights fail anywhere the Caucus controls, repair crews respond within hours. The lights staying lit demonstrates collective capability and organizational discipline. It's symbolic and practical: we keep the lights on, literally, through coordinated effort.

Wages in Taho are higher than elsewhere in the Republic—a dock worker makes maybe 8 dinars monthly compared to 5 dinars for agricultural labor in eastern settlements. But cost of living is proportionally higher. Housing is expensive due to limited space. Food is expensive because little grows at this elevation and everything must be imported. Fuel costs nothing because electric heat is universal, but the monthly power bill still costs 3 dinars even with subsidies. Workers make more but save little. The system ensures perpetual dependence on continued work, which ensures labor remains available for commerce.

### The Academy Economy

The Academy creates its own economic ecosystem. Students need housing, food, books, clothing, supplies. Faculty need similar. The institution employs cooks, cleaners, groundskeepers, administrative staff. Local businesses cater to Academy needs: bookbinders, scribes, tailors specializing in academic dress, taverns where students and faculty drink, boarding houses where poorer students rent rooms.

The Academy also generates knowledge economy: engineering consultations, legal advice, technical training for non-students, research commissioned by merchant kilits or Republican Guard. Faculty supplement teaching income through private work. Students work part-time jobs to afford tuition. The boundary between academic institution and commercial enterprise blurs constantly.

The Academy provides its own power from its turbines but sells surplus to surrounding buildings—at cost, naturally, because demonstrating that knowledge institutions don't profiteer enhances the Academy's reputation. The plaques outside Academy power distribution stations read: "Educational surplus provided at zero markup. Service to collective: fundamental principle."

## Social Dynamics

Taho's population is more diverse than anywhere else in the Republic, which creates both cosmopolitanism and tension. Serrulata still dominate numerically at maybe 60%, but near-baseline humans are proportionally higher at 30% because technical and commercial work favors those with broader adaptability. Ranga refugees cluster in Port District, working their way toward citizenship. Small populations of virtually every caste appear because Taho's size and commerce attract people from everywhere.

### Class Formation Despite Ideology

The Republic officially rejects class hierarchy. Taho makes this impossible to sustain. The merchant families living in Upper City compounds, sending children to the Academy, participating in Senate politics, and funding philanthropic projects are obviously different from dock workers living four to a room in Port District tenements. Wealth, education, political access, social connections—every measure of class exists despite ideological denial.

The revolutionary response is that this isn't class formation but differential contribution to collective capacity. Factor Yula Khen-Masot isn't wealthy because she exploits workers but because her enterprise generates surplus that funds schools and training and subsidized power. Her wealth is temporary—it serves the collective and will eventually be redistributed through philanthropic investment.

The Workingmens' Caucus response is that this is sophisticated justification for accumulation. Wealth concentrates intergenerationally. Merchant children attend the Academy. Dock workers' children become dock workers unless they're extraordinary and lucky. Khen-Masot generosity provides subsidized electricity while ensuring Khen-Masot children receive advantages that perpetuate family position. Temporary inequality becomes permanent hierarchy through mechanisms that claim to prevent exactly that.

Neither side is entirely wrong. The Khen-Masot Enterprise genuinely does fund programs that benefit the collective—the subsidized power is real, the schools they support are accessible, the training programs they finance create opportunity. But it also ensures Khen-Masot children receive advantages dock workers' children don't—tutoring, social connections, financial security that permits risk-taking, inherited wealth that compounds across generations.

The universal electrification complicates this dynamic. Every worker has electric light and heat, benefits that would be luxuries elsewhere. The poorest Tahan lives more comfortably than middle-class citizens in eastern settlements. But comfort is relative. When you see merchant families with multiple generating stations and private compounds lit like daytime 24 hours, your own subsidized heating element feels less like revolutionary achievement and more like managed inequality.

### Education and Mobility

The Academy is supposed to enable mobility based on merit rather than birth. Students from any background can apply if their kilit sponsors them or they have personal funds. Examinations are supposedly neutral, measuring knowledge and capability rather than family connection.

In practice: merchant families can afford tutors preparing children for entrance examinations. They can afford tuition even when kilits won't sponsor. They can afford having children attend Academy for three years rather than working. Dock workers' children rarely attend because even with Caucus sponsorship, losing three years of wages costs more than the family can afford.

But some do. The Workingmens' Caucus sponsors maybe thirty students yearly from member families—collective investment in future leadership. These students work part-time jobs in Port District (their kilits ensure employment), live in cheaper housing (shared rooms, minimal amenities), wear simpler clothes (functional rather than fashionable), and study harder because they have no family resources to fall back on. Captain Sekh came from pastoral kilits, attended on military sponsorship, graduated and served. The system permits mobility. But it's exhausting mobility requiring advantages most don't have.

### Political Division and Unity

Taho votes Dusk overwhelmingly in most elections—maybe 75% in municipal contests. The merchant kilits need stable Imperial trade. The working kilits benefit from wages generated by commerce. Even Dawn sympathizers in Taho tend toward moderate positions prioritizing economic stability over revolutionary purity.

But Taho has a Dawn Party arch-curate, appointed from Ganat proper specifically to prevent the city from becoming completely divorced from revolutionary principles. Arch-Curate Tewodo Khamun was elevated to that position six years ago by Patriarch Kaleb, chosen because he could navigate Taho's contradictions while maintaining theological rigor.

This creates constant productive tension: Tewodo preaching revolutionary theology to congregations that want religious legitimacy without political disruption, defending Imperial commerce as works-based atonement while Dawn radicals from Ganat proper call him a collaborator, insisting that wealth must serve collective capacity while presiding over city where wealth accumulates intergenerationally.

The Workingmens' Caucus provides counterweight to merchant dominance, but their relationship with the merchant kilits is complicated. They oppose merchant control while depending on merchant commerce for employment. They demand higher wages while recognizing that wage increases might make Taho goods uncompetitive with Imperial alternatives. They resent subsidized utilities being framed as philanthropy while accepting that coordinated merchant subsidy keeps power affordable. They're simultaneously dependent on and opposed to the system that makes Taho function.

### Cultural Life

Taho is the most cosmopolitan place in the Republic. Imperial factors bring news from ya-Sattra. Merchants travel to Imperial territories and return with stories. The Academy brings students from across the Republic who exchange regional perspectives. Multiple languages are spoken: Ganati trade-tongue dominant, Imperial Common frequent, Akama dialects from northern trade, occasional others.

This creates sophistication that frontier settlements lack and resent. Tahans know Imperial music, literature, fashion. They discuss Oracle theology and Penitent doctrine with academic precision. They debate Senate proceedings with reference to political theory. They eat imported foods, wear imported fabrics, own imported goods. They're educated, traveled, knowledgeable about the world beyond their city.

The electric lights make this sophistication visible. At night, Taho glows—streetlights, building lights, workshop lights burning late, the constant illumination that demonstrates wealth and capability. Visitors from dark settlements see this and experience complicated emotions: admiration for what commerce achieves, resentment at what it costs, recognition that this is what the Republic could become if everyone accepted Taho's compromises, fear that this is what the Republic is becoming regardless of whether anyone accepts them.

From Ghemar-yan or eastern settlements, Tahans look like people who forgot what the revolution was about. From ya-Sattra, they look like ambitious provincials pretending sophistication. From Taho's own perspective, they're navigating complexity that simpler places don't understand—maintaining revolutionary principles while accommodating necessary pragmatism, building the Republic's future through commerce critics consider betrayal but can't provide alternatives for.

## Religious Life

The Arch-Curacy of Taho is the most politically fraught religious position in the Republic. It must maintain Patriarch Kaleb's revolutionary theology while serving a population dependent on Imperial trade, must answer to distant ecclesiastical authority while responding to local economic reality, must preach collective sacrifice to people accumulating individual wealth.

### Arch-Curate Tewodo Khamun

Tewodo is fifty-three, Serrulata, born in Ganat proper but educated in Taho at the Academy. He's been arch-curate for six years, elevated by Patriarch Kaleb specifically because he could navigate Taho's contradictions without either abandoning revolutionary theology or alienating the commercial class that makes the Republic economically viable.

He's tall even for Serrulata, scales dyed in subtle geometric patterns (more restrained than merchant ostentation, more elaborate than frontier simplicity), wearing religious vestments that combine Penitent black with Ganati teal. He moves through the city constantly—visiting parishes, attending kilit assemblies, consulting with Senate members, mediating disputes—because he understands that his authority derives from presence and engagement rather than hierarchical position.

His sermons are precisely crafted exercises in theological justification for necessary compromise. Every Sunday he stands in the Arch-Curacy's massive sanctuary before congregations that include merchant families and Workingmens' Caucus representatives, Academy faculty and Port District laborers, Imperial factors (who attend cautiously, maintaining that their interest is cultural rather than political) and revolutionary veterans suspicious that he's too comfortable with wealth.

"You trade with the Empire," he tells them. "This is necessity, not betrayal. The Interdict makes us dependent. We cannot build everything we need from principles alone when the Empire controls the patents and the manufacturing capacity. But necessity becomes betrayal when profit becomes purpose."

He pauses, making eye contact with merchant families in the front rows. "The obol earned through Imperial commerce must serve Republican capacity. The enterprise maximizing profit through Imperial partnerships while providing minimal local employment fails the collective. The enterprise generating surplus that funds schools, trains workers, builds infrastructure, provides subsidized utilities—this demonstrates growing wisdom."

Then he turns to address the working sections. "But works must serve everyone. The merchant who builds private wealth while claiming public service is not demonstrating wisdom—he's demonstrating the same exploitation the Empire practices. The subsidy must be real. The schools must be accessible. The training must create opportunity. We judge enterprises not by their rhetoric but by their actual reinvestment in collective capacity."

Every Sunday he performs this balancing act. The merchant families nod along because he's legitimizing their wealth accumulation as revolutionary practice. The Workingmens' Caucus listens skeptically because he's setting standards merchants routinely fail to meet while calling that failure revolutionary as long as some reinvestment occurs. The Dawn Party observers from Ganat proper write reports noting dangerous accommodation with Imperial system and wealth accumulation. The Dusk Party members are satisfied that their theology permits what they're already doing.

Tewodo continues because someone has to make revolutionary theology work in the city that makes the Republic economically viable. His private view—shared with close colleagues but never publicly—is that the theology is probably incorrect and the compromises probably permanent, but that admitting this would destroy the ideological framework that prevents Taho from becoming simply another Imperial client city. Better to maintain the fiction that wealth serves revolution than to acknowledge that revolution now serves wealth.

### The Parishes and the Re-Carved Past

Taho has seventeen parish churches spread across the terraces. They range from the grand First Terrace church seating 500 (elite congregation, conservative theology, wealthy donors) to small Port District chapel serving 80 (working families, more radical theology, Caucus funding). Every parish building predates the Revolution—Penitentism was majority faith even under the Sultanate, which maintained churches to prevent population from turning to Oracle Cult or other alternatives.

The buildings themselves tell Taho's religious history through their stone. The facades are carved with bas-reliefs showing Penitent theological scenes: humanity's transgression, the Foreigners' judgment, the Interdict as punishment, hope for redemption through patient endurance. But if you examine closely, you see that many reliefs are carved deeper than others—sometimes 2-3 inches deeper into the granite.

These deeper carvings are replacements. The original Sultanate-era reliefs showed passive acceptance: humans bowing before divine judgment, waiting patiently for forgiveness, demonstrating submission as virtue. The revolutionary church replaced those scenes with active engagement: humans working collectively, building infrastructure, demonstrating capacity through material progress, earning redemption rather than waiting for it.

At the First Terrace church—the oldest and most elaborate—you can trace this evolution across the entire facade. The lowest reliefs are oldest, still showing original Sultanate scenes with minimal revolutionary modification. Higher up, more aggressive re-carving: scenes of Sultana's religious authority chiseled away and replaced with collective assemblies. The replaced sections are obvious if you know what you're looking for—the granite is carved deeper, the edges sharper because they're newer, the style slightly different because the carvers had different theology to express.

Inside the churches, similar evolution. Wooden panels, painted frescos, carved details—all showing layers of theological reinterpretation. A panel originally showing humans accepting punishment now shows humans learning from mistakes. A fresco depicting patient endurance now depicts active progress. The theology hasn't changed completely—it's still Penitent Christianity believing in the Interdict and hoping for redemption—but the emphasis shifted from passive to active, from waiting to working, from accepting to earning.

The parish curates are mostly Dusk despite Dawn arch-curate. They interpret Tewodo's revolutionary theology through their congregations' needs. First Terrace curate emphasizes individual responsibility and charitable giving—comfortable theology for wealthy congregation. Port District curate emphasizes collective struggle and shared sacrifice—militant theology for working families. Both claim Patriarch Kaleb's authority. Both are technically correct because the theology is deliberately flexible enough to permit both interpretations.

The religious calendar structures Taho's year. Common Plea services happen weekly. Major feast days draw massive attendance—Harvest Assembly, Winter Vigil, Revolution Day (commemorating Ulayah's assassination). These gatherings combine religious observance with community business: marriages announced, deaths mourned, political disputes mediated, collective decisions made. The Church remains parallel power structure to kilits and Senate, inherited from Sultanate period, re-carved for revolutionary purposes, maintained through Republican transition.

## Daily Life and Rhythms

Winter in Taho is survival enabled by infrastructure. From November through March, the pass closes. The city depends entirely on stored supplies and lake trade with other settlements. Temperature drops to \-10°F. Snow accumulates 15 feet deep in some areas. But people don't huddle in darkness burning fuel for warmth. Every building has electric heating. Every street has lights. The city glows through winter darkness, demonstrating that Republican infrastructure defeats climate.

The Port District continues operations: fish must be caught, warehouses maintained, goods prepared for spring. The work happens in electrically lit buildings with electric heat, making winter labor tolerable rather than brutal. The Merchant Terraces slow but don't stop—workshops continue production, though at reduced pace. The Upper City becomes quieter—the Academy holds winter term but many students leave if they can afford travel, commerce pauses, religious observance provides primary social structure.

This is when Taho feels most separate from the Empire and most Republican. No caravans arrive. No Imperial factors negotiate deals. The city survives on its own resources, demonstrates its capacity for independence. The lights staying on through winter darkness prove Republican engineering works. Dawn Party rhetoric sounds more plausible when snow isolates you from the system you're supposedly dependent on and your electric lights keep burning anyway.

Spring arrival is dramatic. The pass opens in April, sometimes May. The first caravan through receives celebration: markets open early, people gather to see what Imperial goods arrived, merchants negotiate frantically because six months of trade happens in six weeks of intense activity. The Port District explodes with work. Warehouses empty and refill. Ships cross the lake constantly. The economic engine that makes Taho wealthy roars to life. The streetlights that burned all winter now illuminate constant activity—loading docks operating 24 hours, workshops running double shifts, transport wagons moving through streets that never fully empty.

Summer is peak activity. Caravans arrive weekly. Imperial factors maintain permanent presence in Port District. Merchant kilits negotiate contracts, arrange shipments, collect profits. The Workingmens' Caucus coordinates labor across multiple kilits to maximize bargaining power—if one group strikes, all groups strike. The merchant kilits discovered this coordination makes negotiations more complicated and wages higher, but also makes labor disputes resolve faster because nobody wants city-wide work stoppage during trading season.

Fall is preparation. The pass closes in October or November. Everyone calculates: do we have enough stored food? Enough supplies? Enough goods to sell next spring? Merchant families who miscalculated face reduced profits. Working families who didn't save face harder winters. The Arch-Curacy organizes charity—collective granaries, emergency funds, loans to struggling kilits. Revolutionary principles become practical necessity when winter threatens.

But even the hardest winter, even the poorest family, has electric light and heat. The lights stay on. The heating elements warm sleeping platforms. The city glows. This universal infrastructure is Taho's greatest achievement and its greatest justification for the compromises that enable it: we trade with the Empire, we accumulate wealth, we tolerate inequality, and in exchange, nobody freezes in darkness. The poor in Taho live better than the middle class in eastern settlements. That's not revolutionary rhetoric. That's material fact made visible every winter night.

## The Academy

The Academy occupies maybe twenty acres in the Upper City: connected pre-Interdict buildings forming complex around central plaza, dormitories housing 400 students, lecture halls, laboratories, library, administrative offices. It's the Republic's primary institution for training professional class: military officers, engineers, administrators, teachers. The buildings themselves are mysterious—original purpose unknown, construction techniques beyond current capability, utilities that still partially function after millennia.

### Curriculum and Purpose

Three-year program combining practical skills with theoretical knowledge. Students study mathematics, engineering, military science, law, Republican history, Penitent theology, Imperial systems (know your enemy/trading partner), languages (Imperial Common required, others optional). The curriculum attempts creating citizens who understand both revolutionary principles and practical governance—not easy when those frequently contradict.

The engineering track produces graduates who can maintain and build Republican infrastructure without depending on Imperial expertise. They study hydraulics, metallurgy, electrical systems, mechanical engineering. They learn by maintaining actual infrastructure—Taho's waterworks, its generating plants, its electrical distribution, its port facilities. The Academy isn't just teaching theory. It's creating people who can keep the Republic functioning independently. Many Academy-trained engineers work for merchant kilits providing subsidized utilities—the engineering expertise that makes competitive philanthropy possible comes from Academy graduates whose tuition was funded by those same merchant kilits.

The military track trains officers for the Republican Guard. Students study tactics, logistics, military history, command principles. They spend summers with active units gaining practical experience. Many come from veterans kilits or the Workingmens' Caucus, sponsored by communities that need leadership. Captain Sekh graduated from military track twenty years ago, serves in Ghemar-yan, teaches at the Academy occasionally during winter when the pass is closed and she's based in Taho anyway.

The administrative track is newest, created because the Republic needs people who can actually govern. Students study law, economics, political theory, bureaucratic systems. They're being prepared to staff ministries, run kilit administrations, serve as judges and mediators. This is hardest curriculum to justify revolutionarily—you're training bureaucrats, which sounds dangerously Imperial. But the Republic needs bureaucracy or it gets chaos. Dawn accepts necessity grudgingly while insisting graduates remember they serve kilits rather than state.

### Student Life and Class Dynamics

Students come from across the Republic. About 60% are Serrulata, 25% near-baseline, 15% other castes. About 30% come from merchant families who pay full tuition. About 50% receive kilit sponsorship contingent on service commitment after graduation. About 20% are on military scholarship and guaranteed Guard service.

The social divisions are obvious despite ideology. Wealthy students live in better dormitory rooms (single occupancy, larger space, better furnishings), eat better food (can afford supplements to standard Academy meals), wear nicer clothes (fashion rather than function), have money for books and supplies and occasional luxuries (tavern visits, new clothing, gifts for romantic interests). Scholarship students work part-time jobs in Port District or for merchant kilits, share cramped rooms, wear simpler clothing, study harder because failure means losing everything including money their kilit invested in them.

But the Academy forces interaction. Classroom seating is assigned randomly. Dormitory assignments deliberately mix wealth levels. Group projects require cooperation across class lines. The intent is creating professional class that shares Republican identity beyond economic position. Partly it works—friendships form despite backgrounds, shared Academy experience creates bonds that persist after graduation, some wealthy students marry scholarship students (scandalizing families but happening anyway). Partly it doesn't—wealth creates advantages everyone notices, and pretending otherwise insults everyone's intelligence.

Social life happens in the central plaza and surrounding taverns—establishments that exist specifically to extract dinars from students who have them and extend credit to students who don't (with interest, naturally, but at rates the Academy negotiates down through its institutional weight). Students gather evenings to drink (grain alcohol, tea, occasionally smuggled Imperial spirits), argue politics, debate theology, gossip, form romantic relationships, study collectively, and generally navigate the complicated social dynamics of bringing together people from radically different backgrounds and expecting them to become colleagues.

The Academy's own generating capacity—turbines on the stream running through the complex—powers not just Academy buildings but several blocks of surrounding structures. This power is provided at cost, and the Academy publishes its distribution accounts monthly: zero markup, because educational institutions don't profiteer. The merchant kilits do something similar but frame it as philanthropy. The Academy frames it as institutional responsibility. The difference is subtle but ideologically significant—one is gift, the other is duty.

## Specific Locations

### The Taho Pass Road

The road through Taho Pass is pre-Interdict engineering that makes Taho possible. Granite blocks fitted so precisely that joints remain tight after millennia. The road climbs from 6,200 feet to 7,000 feet over twelve miles, switchbacking up the western slope. The grade is consistent—never too steep for laden wagons, designed for efficiency that modern engineers can't match but can maintain.

At night the road is lit by streetlights every quarter-mile on the Taho side—provided by various merchant kilits competing to have their lights reach furthest toward the pass, demonstrating their commitment to commerce and safety. The lights are maintained obsessively because when the pass is open, wagons move day and night to maximize trading season. A caravan arriving after dark sees Taho glowing below and the road lit like invitation: come trade, we have infrastructure that makes commerce safe.

Maintaining this road is continuous work. The pass closes winter but snow and ice still damage surface. Spring requires clearing rockfall, repairing drainage, checking for structural problems. The Republican Guard maintains garrison at the pass specifically for road maintenance—engineering corps that keeps commerce flowing. The Guard also provides the labor that installs and maintains the electrical infrastructure extending toward the pass—poles, wire, lights, all requiring constant attention at altitude where weather is brutal.

At the highest point, there's a marker: granite obelisk 15 feet tall, carved with text in script nobody can read. The marker was there when humans returned to this region. It marks something important to whoever built the road. Maybe it commemorates construction. Maybe it warns of something. Nobody knows. Everyone walks past it daily during trade season. Somebody—identity unknown—wraps electric wire around it during winter, creating makeshift streetlight at the pass summit. The light stays on all winter despite nobody officially maintaining it. Some mysteries are practical rather than philosophical.

### Factor's Row

A district in the Port District where Imperial factors operate from warehouses: timber buildings on granite foundations, large doors facing docks, offices on second floors, electric lights blazing from every window to demonstrate prosperity and reliability. The factors live here seasonally—arrive when pass opens, leave before winter, maintain year-round staff for warehouse security and off-season preparation.

Factor's Row has its own character: half Imperial, half Republican, entirely commercial. Imperial factors dress in geometric-cut robes marking Bureau affiliation or Aureate employment. They speak Imperial Common. They maintain customs that seem foreign: afternoon tea, formal business protocols, hierarchical deference patterns. But they've adapted to Taho: they negotiate with kilit representatives as equals (because kilits won't tolerate being treated as subordinates), they tolerate Republican political discussions in their presence (carefully saying nothing that might imply opinion), they understand that commerce here requires accommodation to local norms.

The warehouses themselves are vast: storage for months of goods, careful organization systems, electric lighting throughout (powered by merchant kilit subsidies the factors pay for but don't control). Security is provided by the Dockers' Fraternal—another accommodation, because factors initially tried hiring private security from Imperial sources, discovered that created immediate hostility, and accepted that warehouse protection in Taho means using Republican labor. The arrangement works because everyone profits from goods staying secure.

### The Copper Market

A plaza on the Second Terrace where artisans work and sell copper goods: vessels, jewelry, tools, decorative items. Copper comes from Ganati mines, is worked in Taho shops, sells to both Republican and Imperial markets. The plaza is maybe 100 feet square, surrounded by workshops with forge fires visible through open doors—and now, with electric lights supplementing natural illumination, work continues into evening during trading season.

The artisans are organized as their own kilit—the Copper-wrights' Collective. About 120 craftspeople and their families, specializing in different aspects of copper work. Some forge blanks. Some do decorative hammering. Some specialize in jewelry. Some make tools. They work cooperatively, share equipment, train apprentices collectively, and maintain some of the most elaborate workshop lighting in the city—a point of pride that they fund themselves through collective treasury rather than accepting merchant subsidy.

The copper work itself is distinctive: geometric patterns, organic curves, techniques refined over generations. A vessel might have patterns hammered into the surface creating texture and decoration. Jewelry might combine copper with turquoise or other stones in settings that require days of careful work. Tools are functional first but many have decorative elements showing craftsmanship pride and artisan identity.

The market operates daily, though more intensely during trading season. Artisans work in their shops (forge fires and electric lights making interior spaces visible from plaza, hammering creating continuous noise, smell of hot metal and quenching water), display finished goods in plaza, negotiate prices with buyers. Some goods sell locally. Some go to Imperial factors who pay premium prices for quality work—Ganati copper-craft has reputation in ya-Sattra for durability and aesthetic sophistication. The craftsmanship maintains Republican pride while generating Imperial currency—another Taho contradiction that nobody questions anymore.

The plaza's streetlights are maintained by the Collective itself, refusing subsidy from merchant kilits as point of artisan independence. The lights are kept obsessively functional, demonstrating that skilled labor can maintain its own infrastructure without depending on commercial patronage.

### The Great Stairs

Connecting the terraces are granite staircases, some pre-Interdict construction, some Republican addition. The greatest is the Central Stair: 800 steps from Port District to Upper City, rising 600 feet, wide enough for two people abreast, electric lights every twenty steps making night travel safe. The stairs are granite blocks, each step 8 inches high, edges slightly rounded from millennia of footsteps.

Climbing the stairs is daily experience for many Tahans. Port District workers living in lower terraces walk up to Upper City for deliveries or business. Students walk down to Port District for work or recreation. Merchants ascend to Senate sessions or religious services. The stairs aren't just transportation—they're social space, meeting ground, place where class and geography intersect visibly and people navigate that intersection daily.

The climb is exhausting at altitude. Visitors from lower elevations struggle with thin air and granite stairs that seem endless. Even natives pause periodically at landings every hundred steps—spaces widened deliberately to provide rest points and overlooks. From each landing you see more of the city: the port below, the terraced city climbing toward you, the lake stretching north and south, the mountains rising behind, the pass to the west where caravans arrive bringing contradiction.

At night the stairs become rivers of light—amber electric glow marking path from Port District to Upper City, maintained by different kilits at different sections but all competing to keep their section brightest and most reliably lit. You can tell when you cross from one kilit's territory to another by subtle differences in light spacing and intensity. The stairs at night are Taho's most dramatic demonstration of infrastructure—climbing through illumination that makes navigation safe and announces to anyone watching from surrounding darkness: we have power, we have coordination, we have the capacity to light 800 steps up a mountain face and maintain them obsessively because doing so demonstrates everything we claim to be.

## Relationship to the Republic

Taho is simultaneously essential and suspect. The city generates maybe 40% of Republican revenue through commerce and taxation. It trains most Republican officers and engineers. It provides manufactured goods the Republic needs. It demonstrates through its electric lights and coordinated infrastructure that Republican independence is economically and technically viable. Without Taho, the Republic would be poorer, less capable, more dependent on Imperial good will.

But Taho is also everything the revolution was supposed to oppose. Wealth concentration despite subsidies that make concentration palatable. Class formation despite universal electrification that obscures it. Dependence on Imperial trade despite rhetoric about independence. Sophistication that separates urban elite from frontier communities. Comfortable accommodation with the imprisoning Empire justified through theology that makes commerce into atonement. Every contradiction between revolutionary rhetoric and practical governance appears most clearly in Taho's electric-lit streets.

The eastern settlements view Taho as decadent compromisers who forgot what the revolution was about—they have electric lights while we have oil lamps, they trade with the Empire while we practice revolutionary purity, they accumulate wealth while claiming it serves collective capacity. The pastoral kilits view it as necessary evil—we need what they trade but we don't trust how they trade it, we need their rifles but we resent depending on their commerce, we vote Dusk partly because Taho's Dusk makes sense in ways Ganat proper's Dawn doesn't.

Ganat proper views Taho as problem requiring management through Dawn arch-curate and political oversight. Arch-Curate Tewodo exists to prevent complete ideological drift, to maintain connection between commercial practice and revolutionary principles, to ensure wealth serves collective capacity rather than becoming self-justifying. Whether he succeeds depends on who's judging. Taho claims he legitimizes their commerce. Ganat proper claims he constrains their excesses. Both are partly right.

The veterans view Taho as resource to be exploited for expansion—Taho's wealth funds eastern settlement through land grants and military stipends, its commerce enables military spending through taxation, its infrastructure provides model for what the Republic could build everywhere if resources permitted. They're not wrong, but they're also not acknowledging that Taho's wealth accumulates through mechanisms that would corrupt eastern settlements if replicated there. Taho works because it's the commercial gateway, unique position enabling unique compromises. Turning everywhere into Taho wouldn't spread prosperity—it would spread dependency on Imperial trade that only one gateway city can actually manage.

Taho views itself as realist. We understand what the Republic actually needs. We provide it through compromises that maintain revolutionary framework while accommodating practical necessity. We're building collective capacity through commerce that Dawn purists consider betrayal but can't provide alternatives for. We're sophisticated enough to navigate Imperial relations without being absorbed. We're successful in ways that make others resentful but that everyone depends on. And most importantly: everyone has electric light and heat, the streets are safe at night because they're lit, the commerce flows, the infrastructure works, and nobody freezes in darkness. That's not betraying the revolution—that's making the revolution materially successful instead of ideologically pure and materially unsuccessful.

## The Future

Taho is growing. Population increases 5% yearly—immigrants from across the Republic seeking work and opportunity. Commerce expands as the Republic reverse-engineers more Imperial technology and produces goods that compete with Imperial manufactures. The merchant kilits accumulate more wealth and political influence despite subsidies that make accumulation acceptable. The Workingmens' Caucus organizes more effectively, demanding higher wages and greater political representation. The Academy expands enrollment to meet demand. The contradictions intensify: more wealth to justify through theology, more dependence on Imperial trade to explain away, more sophistication separating elite from the rest of the Republic, more infrastructure demonstrating success that depends on compromises.

The city could become what the revolution was supposed to prevent: wealthy urban center whose commercial elite uses revolutionary rhetoric and subsidized utilities to justify permanent advantages while working class remains permanently dependent despite organization and collective bargaining. Or it could become what Dawn idealists hope: temporary inequality generating resources that fund collective capacity until everyone achieves similar capability and Taho's advantages disappear because they've been shared universally.

The distinction depends on whether Taho's wealth remains temporary means to collective ends or becomes permanent class formation obscured by universal electrification and theological justification. Nobody knows which. The merchant families accumulate intergenerationally but also subsidize power and fund schools. The Workingmens' Caucus pushes for greater shares but also depends on commerce they're pushing against. The Academy trains professionals who might transcend class or might perpetuate it. The Arch-Curacy preaches reinvestment while acknowledging privately that the system probably preserves rather than eliminates inequality. The Senate votes. The contradictions persist. The lights stay on.

Taho remains what it has always been: gateway city where revolutionary Republic meets imprisoning Empire, where ideology meets necessity, where sophistication meets suspicion, where commerce makes independence possible and dependence inevitable, where universal electrification demonstrates revolutionary success while obscuring revolutionary failure, where 700,000 people navigate contradictions that define the Republic's actual existence beyond its revolutionary rhetoric.

The pass opens each spring. The caravans arrive. The commerce flows. The lights blaze. The streetlights mark every major thoroughfare. The buildings glow from within. The stairs rise lit through darkness. The city demonstrates what Republican commerce achieves: nobody freezes, nobody lives in darkness, infrastructure works reliably, and all of this depends on compromises with the Empire that the revolution was supposed to make unnecessary.

The contradictions deepen. And Taho continues being simultaneously the Republic's economic heart and its ideological embarrassment, prosperous and pragmatic and essential and deeply, deeply suspect. But brightly lit. Always brightly lit. That much is undeniable as you approach the city after dark and see it glowing on the mountainside like proof that something is working, even if nobody can quite agree what that something is or whether it's what the revolution intended.
