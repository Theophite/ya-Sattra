## **I. Phonology**

Akama phonology draws from Tibetan, Turkic, and archaic Mayan influences, producing a harsh, throat-focused sound that most Imperial and Ganati speakers find difficult to replicate without childhood training. The language lives deep in the throat—gutturals that rumble in the chest, vowels so dark they seem to emerge from the stomach.

### **Consonants**

| Type | Sounds | Notes |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Plain stops | p, t, k, b, d, g | Standard; k, t, d are prominent |
| Aspirated stops | kh, th, ph | Distinct phonemes; kh is most common |
| Affricates | ts, ch | ts- is very common word-initially |
| Aspirated affricates | tsh, chh | Rare; appear in formal/archaic contexts |
| Fricatives | s, sh, gh, h | gh is a voiced uvular fricative (like French "r") |
| Nasals | m, n, ng | \-ng is a common word-final |
| Liquids | r, l | r is a light tap, not heavily trilled |
| Glides | w, y | Less common than in other languages |
| Glottal stop | ' | Appears mid-word; written as apostrophe |
| Ejectives | k', t', ts', ch' | Archaic/ritual only; see Section VII |

**Consonant Clusters:**

Initial clusters are permitted and common, showing Tibetan influence:

* ts-, kh-, gh-, dh-, br-, dr-, kr-, thr-

These clusters may simplify in casual speech. The phrase "standing in the rain" (*ghol krom dhaal*) exemplifies typical Akama consonant clustering.

**Final Consonants:**

Word-final consonants are common:

* \-ng, \-n, \-m (nasals)  
* \-k, \-g (stops)  
* \-r, \-l (liquids)  
* \-kh, \-gh (fricatives, less common)

### **Vowels**

| Vowel | Notes |
| ----- | ----- |
| a | Open, dark; the most common vowel |
| o | Back, rounded; frequent |
| u | Back, rounded; frequent |
| e | Front; less common, marks certain patterns |
| i | Front; less common, marks certain patterns |

Akama vowels are described as "almost swallowed," emerging from deep in the throat with reduced articulation compared to the clear vowels of Imperial Common or Ganati.

### **Vowel Harmony**

Akama names exhibit vowel harmony, a Turkic influence. Names tend toward either back-vowel or front-vowel patterns:

**Back-vowel names (dominant pattern):**

* Use only a, o, u  
* The standard, unmarked Akama pattern  
* Examples: Tsoka, Ataru, Ghom, Buragh, Gorin, Khol, Som, Tam

**Front-vowel names (marked pattern):**

* Use only e, i  
* Associated with Kma-Dhol populations, certain calendar phases, or specific regions  
* Examples: Imek, Sevek, Kherzen, Tselker, Vey

**Mixed-vowel names:**

* Cross harmony boundaries  
* Usually indicate compound formation, borrowed names, or foreign origin  
* Rare in traditional Akama naming

### **Syllable Structure**

* **Preferred:** CV, CVC, CVCC  
* **Initial clusters:** Common (ts-, kh-, gh-, br-, etc.)  
* **Final clusters:** Rare; \-ng, \-kh are acceptable  
* **Name length:** Monosyllabic to trisyllabic; disyllabic most common

### **Stress**

Stress typically falls on the first syllable. In compound forms (epithets, astrological names), each element receives its own stress.

## **III. Astrological Names**

The Akama employ a complex phasal calendar combining phase counts (traditionally 7-13 days per phase) with cycles of named phases related to Malpais animals, plants, and natural phenomena. This calendar system generates two types of astrological names, both following the same structure.

### **Structure**

**Pattern:** \[Number\] \+ \[Phase Element\]

* Numbers range from One through Thirteen  
* Phase elements are drawn from Malpais ecology and natural phenomena  
* The combination carries astrological significance

### **Family Astrological Names (Public Surnames)**

These capture the astrological character of a family line. They function as publicly used surnames, particularly common in the Malpais and among Kma-Dhol populations.

**Canonical Examples:**

* Thirteen-Hawk (as in Khol Thirteen-Hawk)  
* Nine Birds (as in Vey Nine Birds)

**Structure:** \[Personal Name\] \+ \[Number\]-\[Phase Element\]

The hyphen connects number and phase element into a single surname unit.

**Cultural Context:**

The specific combination is believed to reflect the family's essential character and fortune. Certain combinations are considered particularly auspicious or inauspicious, influencing marriage negotiations and alliance formation. A family with an inauspicious astrological name may struggle to arrange advantageous marriages regardless of their actual wealth or standing.

### **Private Astrological Names**

Each individual receives a private astrological name reflecting the specific day on which they were named (not necessarily born). These names follow the same Number \+ Phase Element structure but are used exclusively among family members.

**Usage Rules:**

* Used only in private, familial contexts  
* Never employed in public address or formal settings  
* Maintained even by families who have adopted non-astrological public surnames  
* Persist among Akama-cultured populations in Ogon who otherwise use epithets or Imperial names

**Naming Timing:**

Children born on unlucky days are not named on their birth date. The family waits until the next day with favorable fortune, ensuring the child receives an auspicious private name regardless of their actual birth timing. This practice means a person's private astrological name may not correspond to their actual birthday.

**Cultural Significance:**

The persistence of this practice even among populations who have abandoned other traditional naming conventions demonstrates its deep cultural importance. An Ogon merchant with an Imperial name and no astrological surname still uses a private calendrical name with their children and siblings.

Certain combinations are considered fortunate or unfortunate:

* Numbers 7, 9, 13 are generally favorable

**Generally Inauspicious:**

**Regional Variation:**

What counts as auspicious varies between Malpais villages, Ogon urban culture, and Kma-Dhol communities. A family considered fortunate in one region might be viewed with suspicion in another based purely on their astrological name.

## **IV. Descriptive Epithets**

Epithets are earned or descriptive names common in Ogon and among warbands, often replacing or supplementing astrological surnames. They carry clear lexical meaning.

### **Structure**

**Pattern:** \[Personal Name\] \+ \[Epithet\]

The epithet follows the personal name, functioning similarly to a surname while remaining semantically transparent.

### **Canonical Examples**

* Tsoka Red Deer  
* Ataru Blackfish  
* Imek Stonefoot  
* Iron-Rain

### **Epithet Categories**

**Physical Characteristics:**

| Epithet | Meaning |
| ----- | ----- |
| Stonefoot | Steady, immovable |
| Red Deer | Coloring, perhaps scale patterns |
| Blackfish | Dark coloring, orca-like |
| Long-Arm | Reach, height |
| Ghost-Eye | Pale eyes, unsettling gaze |
| Scar-Back | Battle-marked |
| Smoke-Voice | Raspy speech |

**Achievements/Qualities:**

| Epithet | Meaning |
| ----- | ----- |
| Iron-Rain | Devastating attack pattern |
| Thunder-Caller | Artillery commander or loud presence |
| Bridge-Burner | Decisive, no retreat |
| Path-Finder | Scout, navigator |
| Debt-Settler | Resolves disputes, possibly violently |
| Bone-Counter | Accountant or death-dealer |
| Dawn-Watcher | Vigilant, early riser |

**Event References:**

| Epithet | Meaning |
| ----- | ----- |
| Flood-Walker | Survived specific disaster |
| Gate-Holder | Defended position |
| First-Over | Led assault |
| Last-Standing | Sole survivor |

### **Usage Patterns**

**Standard Form:**

The epithet follows the personal name: "Imek Stonefoot." This is the default public identification.

**Inherited Form:**

Epithets may pass through generations as family names. A child of Imek Stonefoot might be known as "Ghom Stonefoot" without having earned the epithet personally. Alternatively, families may replace epithets each generation with new descriptive terms, or drop them entirely if deemed no longer applicable.

**Institutional Form:**

Some epithets become so dominant they completely replace the personal name. The individual known as "Iron-Rain" possesses a personal name that is not used in practice—the epithet has become their sole identity. This pattern is particularly common among successful war leaders and prominent figures.

**Warband Identification:**

When additional identification is needed, the warband name may be appended:

* "Imek Stonefoot of the Cracked Shield Band"  
* "Ghom Thunder-Caller, Broken Spoke"

### **Misfortune Epithets**

Some of the most prestigious epithets in Akama culture appear insulting at first glance. This reflects the "standing in the rain" philosophy: endurance is virtue, and surviving misfortune commands more respect than never having faced it.

A warband captain who was captured and castrated might introduce himself as "the Eunuch." A warrior who lost her teeth to a mace blow becomes "Toothless." Someone who has been defeated repeatedly but keeps fighting is "Falls-Twice" or "Three-Times-Broken."

**The logic:**

Taking a shameful mark as your name transforms it. You are not hiding what happened to you. You survived something that should have destroyed you, and you're still here, still dangerous, still commanding warriors. The epithet dares anyone to mock you for something you've already claimed.

To an Imperial, this would be incomprehensible—why advertise weakness? To a Ganati, it might seem performatively humble. But to an Akama, it demonstrates the core cultural value: you endured. You are still standing in the rain.

**Canonical pattern:** The more devastating the misfortune, the more prestigious the epithet becomes *if* the bearer continues to function at high level despite it. A nobody called "the Eunuch" is just pitiful. A Junta member called "the Eunuch" is terrifying—what kind of person loses that and still climbs to the top?

**Misfortune Epithet Examples:**

| Epithet | Implication |
| ----- | ----- |
| The Eunuch | Castrated, still commands respect |
| Toothless | Facial disfigurement, still fights |
| Falls-Twice | Repeatedly defeated, keeps returning |
| Three-Times-Broken | Severe injuries, survived all |
| Half-Hand | Lost fingers, still deadly |
| Limps-Far | Crippled leg, still travels/fights |
| Ghum | Untranslated Akama slur for cognitive impairment; roughly "retard." Taking this as a name is an act of profound defiance. |
| Kindling | Burn-scarred, still present |
| Left-Behind | Abandoned by warband, survived alone |
| Widow-Maker | Lost multiple spouses (dark irony—is it their fault?) |
| The Debtor | Lost everything financially, rebuilt |
| Ransomed Cheap | Captors valued them little; they proved the captors wrong |

**Social dynamics:**

Using someone's misfortune epithet *respectfully* acknowledges their endurance. Using it mockingly is a grave insult—you're denying that they transformed their suffering into strength. The distinction is entirely in tone and context, which outsiders often misread.

An Imperial diplomat addressing "Ghom the Eunuch" with visible discomfort has already lost the negotiation. A fellow Akama saying "Eunuch" with a nod of recognition honors a survivor.

### **Prestigious Names as Mockery**

The system cuts both ways. Just as a shameful epithet can become prestigious through endurance, a prestigious epithet can become shameful when given to someone who hasn't suffered.

The worst warrior in a warband might actually kill an enemy captain once—a genuine achievement. His companions give him the name "Captain-Killer." He cannot refuse it; he technically earned it. But every time someone calls him "Captain-Killer," the tone makes clear what they really mean: *you did one thing, and you're still useless. You have faced no real hardship. You got lucky once.*

**The logic:**

Achievement without suffering is suspect. A warrior called "Storm-Breaker" who has never been wounded, never lost a fight, never been captured or betrayed—what does he actually know? His prestigious name highlights his *lack* of scars. He hasn't stood in the rain. He's been lucky, and luck runs out.

Meanwhile, "Toothless" has been broken and kept fighting. "The Eunuch" lost everything that supposedly makes a man and still commands warriors. These names prove something. "Storm-Breaker" proves nothing except that misfortune hasn't found him yet.

**How this works in practice:**

A warband might deliberately give their weakest member an impressive-sounding name that technically fits something he did:

| Given Name | What He Did | What It Means |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| King-Slayer | Killed an enemy chief (by accident, in a rout) | You got lucky once and you're still worthless |
| First-Strike | Landed the opening blow in a skirmish | You've never had to fight from behind |
| Gate-Breaker | Broke through a wooden gate | One moment of glory, nothing since |
| Giant-Feller | Killed a large enemy | Everyone knows it was a group effort |

The victim cannot escape the name because it's technically accurate. Protesting would only deepen the shame. His only options are to either earn the name through subsequent endurance—suffer greatly and keep fighting until the mockery transforms into respect—or leave the warband entirely.

**Outsider confusion:**

Imperials and Ganatis consistently misread these dynamics. They hear "Captain-Killer" and assume respect. They hear "Toothless" and assume contempt. Getting it backwards marks you as someone who doesn't understand Akama culture at all—which is itself information the Akama will use against you.

Epithets should be:

* Semantically transparent (meaning is clear)  
* Two elements, often hyphenated  
* Descriptive of appearance, achievement, or character  
* Plausible as earned through specific events or characteristics

## **V. Imperial Names in Akama Society**

Members of the Akama cultural complex who are not genetically Akama or Malpais-descended—typically near-baseline humans or other castes who have integrated into Akama society—retain Imperial naming patterns for public identification while participating fully in Akama cultural life.

### **Structure**

**Pattern:** \[Given Name\] \+ \[Family Name\] (Imperial style)

### **Canonical Example**

**Bal Tama** — The Tama family of Ogon exemplifies this pattern. Despite maintaining Imperial names across generations and employing strategies characteristic of Imperial merchant families, they are fully integrated into Akama political structures, with family members holding Junta tokens and participating in Akama governance.

### **Private Astrological Names**

Culturally Akama individuals with Imperial public names still maintain private astrological names for family use. This dual system persists across generations:

* Public sphere: "Bal Tama" (Imperial pattern)  
* Private sphere: \[Number\]-\[Phase Element\] used only among family

An Ogon merchant with an Imperial name and no astrological surname still uses a private calendrical name with their children and siblings. The practice is nearly universal among Akama-cultured populations regardless of their public naming conventions.

### **Social Status**

The retention of Imperial names does not indicate lesser integration or status within Akama society. It reflects genetic origin while acknowledging cultural affiliation. The Tamas' Imperial names don't diminish their position in Ogon's power structure—they hold Junta tokens and shape regional politics as effectively as any Akama-named family.

### **Generating Imperial Names for Akama-Integrated Characters**

Use standard Imperial naming conventions (see Imperial Naming Guide) with the understanding that:

* The character participates fully in Akama cultural life  
* They maintain a private astrological name used only with family  
* Their Imperial name signals genetic heritage, not cultural allegiance  
* No status penalty applies within Akama society

## **VI. Regional Variations**

### **Malpais Populations**

**Naming Pattern:** Personal Name \+ Astrological Surname

Traditional naming remains strongest in Malpais villages. Families maintain detailed calendrical records and consult them for major decisions. The astrological surname is standard; epithets are less common except for achieved individuals.

**Example:** Khol Thirteen-Hawk

**Phonological Notes:** Back-vowel harmony dominant. Archaic forms with ejectives may appear in very traditional families or ritual contexts.

### **Ogon Populations**

**Naming Pattern:** Personal Name \+ Epithet (most common) or Personal Name alone

Urban Ogon populations are more likely to use epithets as surnames or go by personal names alone. However, private astrological names remain nearly universal in family contexts.

**Examples:**

* Tsoka Red Deer (epithet)  
* Ataru Blackfish (epithet)  
* Som (personal name only, in casual address)

**Phonological Notes:** Standard patterns; more tolerance for mixed-vowel names due to cosmopolitan contact.

### **Kma-Dhol Populations**

**Naming Pattern:** Personal Name \+ Astrological Surname

The Kma-Dhol consistently use astrological surnames even when integrated into Ogon society. The technical specialist community maintains traditional naming as a cultural marker distinguishing them from surrounding populations.

**Example:** Vey Nine Birds

**Phonological Notes:** Front-vowel names are more common among Kma-Dhol populations than elsewhere. Names like Vey, Imek, Kherzen, Dherim appear with higher frequency.

### **Warband-Affiliated Populations**

**Naming Pattern:** Epithet alone (institutional) or Personal Name \+ Epithet

Epithets are most common in warband contexts, often becoming so institutionalized that personal names are forgotten. The epithet becomes the identity.

**Example:** Iron-Rain (personal name unknown/unused)

**Phonological Notes:** Epithets are in Imperial Common or trade-tongue, not Akama phonology. Personal names follow standard Akama patterns.

## **VII. Archaic and Ritual Forms**

In sacred contexts—calendar recitations, naming ceremonies, old place names, mythological narratives—archaic phonological features appear that are absent from everyday speech.

### **Ejectives**

Ejective consonants (k', t', ts', ch') appear in:

* Ancient place names  
* Calendar terminology  
* Ritual epithets  
* Mythological names  
* Very old family astrological names that have fossilized

**Examples:**

* Ts'olam (ancient site)  
* K'orum (calendar term)  
* T'akha (ritual epithet, meaning uncertain)  
* Ch'arak (mythological figure)

**Writing Convention:** Apostrophe immediately after the consonant.

### **Usage Restrictions**

Ejectives are never used in:

* Personal names for living people  
* Newly formed epithets  
* Casual speech

A character might reference "the stones of Ts'olam" or "the K'orum cycle" but would not name their child with an ejective-initial name. These forms mark language as ancient, sacred, or mythological.

### **Ritual Calendar Recitation**

When reciting the full calendar cycle in ceremonial contexts, phase elements may take archaic pronunciations that differ from everyday forms. This specialized knowledge is maintained by calendar-keepers in Malpais villages and some Kma-Dhol communities.

## **VIII. Social Context**

### **Public vs. Private Names**

Akama naming operates on two parallel tracks:

**Public sphere:**

* Personal names  
* Astrological surnames (when used)  
* Epithets  
* Imperial names (for non-Akama integrated populations)

**Private sphere:**

* Astrological personal names used exclusively among family

This dual system persists even among populations who have largely abandoned traditional practices in other areas. The private astrological name is nearly universal regardless of what public naming convention a family uses.

### **Naming and Status**

Unlike Imperial or Ganati naming conventions, Akama names do not systematically encode social status or lineage prestige. The system values different qualities:

**Astrological names** indicate connection to traditional calendar systems and Malpais cultural roots, but don't necessarily confer higher status than epithets or Imperial names.

**Epithets** may indicate achievement and recognition within warrior culture, but inherited epithets carry no more weight than newly earned ones.

**Imperial names** among culturally Akama populations indicate genetic heritage but not subordinate status.

What matters is not the type of name but the individual's demonstrated capability, fortune, and adherence to Akama cultural values of endurance and direct action.

### **Translation and Meaning**

**Personal names:** Not considered to have lexical meaning outside their function as names. Though they derive from Akama words historically, they are treated as proper nouns without translatable content.

**Astrological names:** Explicitly carry lexical meaning. The phase elements (Reed, Hawk, Stone, etc.) are translated into whatever language is being spoken. "Khol Thirteen-Hawk" uses an untranslated personal name with a translated astrological surname.

**Epithets:** Retain clear lexical meaning. "Stonefoot" means exactly what it says. They are always in the language of the speaker, not rendered in Akama.

This creates dynamics where some elements of a person's full name are semantically transparent (the epithet "Stonefoot") while others are opaque (the personal name "Imek").

### **Process**

1. **Determine cultural context:** Malpais traditional? Ogon urban? Kma-Dhol? Warband? Integrated non-Akama?  
2. **Select name type:** Astrological surname, epithet, or Imperial pattern  
3. **Choose personal name:** Check vowel harmony; back-vowel for standard, front-vowel for Kma-Dhol  
4. **If astrological:** Select number (1-13) and phase element; consider auspiciousness  
5. **If epithet:** Create descriptive compound reflecting character's appearance, achievement, or nature  
6. **Consider private name:** All Akama-cultured characters have a private astrological name used only with family
