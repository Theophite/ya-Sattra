# Clan Benru: The Trusted Brokers

> **Location:** Three-story structure near Vetch-Rise, Iron Yards
> **Status:** Only registered sewer-fisher clan ("Technician Mixed-Caste")
> **Function:** Economic intermediaries for all sewer-fisher commerce
> **Commission:** 3-5% on brokered transactions

The Benrus operate the documentary fiction that allows sewer-fisher economic activity to exist. Every formal transaction technically flows through their ledgers.

## Important Members

**Patriarch Korel Benru** (Age 67): The clan's elder statesman, maintaining the delicate balance between legal registration and clan solidarity. His slender frame and pronounced sewer-fisher features—webbed fingers, blade-ears, void-black eyes—mark him as pure-line despite his formal robes. Manages the clan's relationship with the Salvage Guild and Bureau clerks.

**Chief Broker Nyma Benru** (Age 41): Korel's niece and likely successor. Handles day-to-day negotiations, particularly the complex documentation system which transforms illegal sewer-fisher contract labor into legally acceptable (and taxable) documentation. Known for her ability to calculate exchange rates between water-credits, salvage chits, and Imperial obols without written notes.

**Jossa Loak-Benru** (Age 29): Marriage-bridge between the Benru and Loak clans. Failed Companion training but retained enough social polish to interface with surface bureaucrats. Manages the clan's "translation services"—converting technical reports from other clans into formal, Bureau-approved language.

**Tessik Benru** (Age 19): Korel's grandson, currently receiving tutored education in Imperial law and accounting. The clan's hope for producing another Bureau candidate, though he shows more aptitude for practical accounting work than theoretical examination.

**"Aunt" Vel Benru** (Age 74): Korel's elder sister, keeper of the clan shrine and family library. Maintains the ancestral treatises on mathematics that the clan no longer directly uses but preserves as cultural heritage. Only living clan member who remembers Tatius before his Bureau appointment.

**Young Mirin Benru** (Age 11): Shows exceptional mathematical intuition even by sewer-fisher standards. The clan debates whether to invest in formal tutoring or preserve her talents for practical brokerage work.

## Family Business and Relationships

The Benrus operate from a three-story structure just outside Vetch-Rise, in the Iron Yards—close enough to the Salvage Guildhall for daily business but within traditional sewer-fisher territory. The building's basement floods during high tides. The middle floor houses the brokerage proper, where Nyma maintains constant negotiations. The upper floor contains family quarters and the clan shrine.

Their business model depends on laundering illegal labor into documented work. When the Loaks need to be paid in obols rather than power- or water-tokens, Benru ledgers show the work performed by "Benru-contracted labor." When the Sarns bring rare deep salvage, Benrus forge ownership chains that satisfy Guild requirements. They charge modest percentages—typically 3-5%—enough to maintain wealth without creating resentment.

The clan maintains careful neutrality in conflicts. When Pipefitters clash with the Salvage Guild, Benrus mediate without taking sides. When Chain-Men demand increased tolls, Benrus negotiate payment schedules that prevent violence. This neutrality is pragmatic rather than philosophical—taking sides would destroy their intermediary value.

Their marriage connections create information networks. Through the Chos, they understand Knot politics. Through the Loaks, they track salvage flows through Lift-3. These connections are deliberately maintained through arranged marriages that might produce sterile offspring but create permanent alliance bonds.

The relationship with the Bureau of the Coin remains complex. Their registration grants privileges but also scrutiny. Bureau inspectors regularly audit their ledgers, searching for evidence of the shadow economy everyone knows exists. The Benrus maintain two sets of books—one real, one for inspection—using mathematical techniques passed through the maternal line to ensure the false books remain internally consistent.

## Living Situation and Idiosyncrasies

The clan occupies a former pumping station, its machinery long ago sold for scrap but the robust architecture remaining. Iron fixtures bear the green patina of constant moisture, while walls show the high-water marks of historical floods. The building's original valve wheels have been repurposed as decorative elements, a reminder of their ancestral profession.

The brokerage floor features an unusual arrangement: two separate entrances, one for sewer-fishers, one for surface dwellers. Visitors never directly interact with anyone other than Benrus; Nyma or her assistants shuttle between spaces, carrying information but preventing direct contact that might upset either party. The sewer-fisher entrance opens onto a dock on the canals which cut between the Midden tenements, where small boats can arrive discreetly. The surface entrance faces a respectable commercial street.

The clan maintains obsessive documentary habits. Every conversation gets recorded, every agreement copied in triplicate. Children learn to write before they can properly walk, practicing letters on slate boards that can be quickly erased. The family library contains not just technical treatises but forty years of reverse-engineered Bureau of the Coin investigations of their fraud operation, slowly reconstructed from public documents.

Daily routine revolves around tide schedules. Morning low tide brings sewer-fisher petitioners arriving by tunnel. Afternoon high tide sees surface merchants arriving by street. Evening brings the reconciliation, where the day's real transactions get transformed into acceptable documentation.

The clan shrine holds particular significance. Beyond the standard Tatius Benru icon, they maintain his actual Bureau registration certificate—one of only three official documents acknowledging sewer-fisher ancestry. Families bring children to see it, proof that transcendence is theoretically possible. The certificate is water-stained and barely legible, but its Bureau seal remains visible.

The Benrus practice a peculiar form of honesty: they never lie about numbers. They might create elaborate fictions about who performed what labor, but the quantities—tons of salvage, gallons of water, counts of workers—remain absolutely accurate. This numerical integrity, inherited from thirty generations, makes them trustworthy even to those who know they're facilitating illegal activity.

Their wealth manifests subtly. They eat better than most sewer-fishers—fresh vegetables rather than algae starch—but never conspicuously. They maintain emergency water reserves that could sustain the clan for months. Most significantly, they can afford to keep elderly clan members who no longer work, a luxury most sewer-fisher families cannot manage.
