# **Imperial Media and Entertainment**

*A Setting Guide*

# **Executive Summary**

The Empire's entertainment culture reflects two centuries of slow transformation following the City-of-Glass Compact. What appears to be exhausted censorship is actually contested terrain, where reformist and conservative factions fight over the boundaries of acceptable expression. The Bureau of the Lens maintains formal authority over information and performance, but that authority operates through negotiation rather than absolute control. Censors fine productions they attend and enjoy; condemnations function as endorsements; the distance between legal prohibition and practical tolerance creates space for cultural production that would be impossible under genuine suppression.

Imperial entertainment divides sharply by caste and access. Without Ganat's democratic broadcast infrastructure, the Empire relies on live performance, licensed print, physical spectacle, and Church observances for communal experience. Lectors are work tools rather than entertainment devicesâ€”too expensive and too controlled for casual use. The biological diversity of the caste system creates performance traditions impossible elsewhere: Ironbone feats of strength, Springheel acrobatics, Highborn polyphonic vocalization. Foreign mediaâ€”particularly Ganati films and radioâ€”penetrates through smuggling and high antennas, offering audiences evidence that alternatives to Imperial exhaustion exist.

The cultural production emerging from this environment serves reformist purposes whether or not reformists coordinate it. Audiences learn to read between lines, to interpret silence and omission, to understand that official reality and actual reality operate on different tracks. The entertainment is not revolution, but it is the soil in which revolution might grow.

# **The Censorship Apparatus**

## **Exhaustion as Policy**

The Bureau of the Lens maintains formal authority over all information dissemination and public performance within the Empire. In theory, nothing may be published, performed, or displayed without Lens approval. In practice, the apparatus is as exhausted as every other Imperial institution. Inspectors lack the resources for comprehensive enforcement. Fines generate revenue that the Bureau needs. Suppression creates martyrs and draws attention to ideas that might otherwise remain obscure.

The result is a system of negotiated tolerance. A Lens censor might attend a pantomime performance, laugh throughout, and then deliver his fine as part of the show. The formal condemnationâ€”posted in the theater's lobbyâ€”functions as endorsement. "The Bureau of the Lens finds this production CONTRARY TO PUBLIC ORDER" tells audiences the production is worth seeing. The elaborateness of the condemnation signals quality: a perfunctory notice suggests forgettable work, while three paragraphs of formal denunciation suggests something exceptional.

Performers calibrate to this dynamic. The skill is finding the edge where condemnation functions as praise without crossing into genuine suppression. The best productions employ someone whose job is understanding exactly where the current censor draws his practical lineâ€”which is never where the formal law places it.

## **The Real Limits**

Genuine limits exist. No production mocks the Emperor directly. No exhibition questions the Interdict's justice. No performance suggests that humanity deserved what happened or that the Empire has already ended. These are the lines that even exhausted censors will kill to defend. The fiction of Imperial continuity must be maintained even by those who know it is fiction.

The playwright Kerem Tal-Vostra crossed this line with The First Dawn in AsovoÃ«. His play did not explicitly mention the City-of-Glass Compact, the Aureates, or any element of current Imperial governance. The allegory operated through displacement. But the final speech collapsed the displacement, asking the audience directly whether the Compact's answer had been sufficient. The Lens did not fine the production. They disappeared the playwright. The distinction matters: mockery of dysfunction assumes dysfunction is permanent; questioning whether audiences have the capacity to decide treats the current order as contingent.

## **Factional Dynamics**

The Lens is not monolithic. Reformist and conservative factions compete within the Bureau as they compete throughout Imperial governance. A production that a conservative inspector would suppress might pass without comment under a reformist's review. The same material plays differently depending on which faction controls local enforcement.

This creates inconsistency that performers exploit. A pantomime company might schedule its most provocative material for districts where reformist inspectors hold authority, saving bland work for conservative territories. The geography of tolerance becomes common knowledge among those who need to know it.

# **Print Culture**

## **The White Sheet**

The Institute of the Echo's White Sheet functions as the Empire's only authorized news sourceâ€”a digest that determines what every other publication may print. What the Sheet permits becomes truth; what it forbids becomes unspeakable. Licensed reprinters distribute it throughout the Empire, and the content is deliberately bland: agricultural reports, infrastructure updates, bureaucratic announcements, sanitized versions of events that frame decline as strategic patience.

For most Imperial subjects, the White Sheet is print culture. Reading it aloud in taverns or workplaces creates shared knowledge of official reality. The monotony is intentionalâ€”excitement suggests instability, and the Empire cannot afford to acknowledge instability.

But the Sheet is more interesting than it appears. Redactors compete to approach truth without censure. Sophisticated readers learn to decode careful phrasings, conspicuous omissions, the placement of stories that seem unrelated until you notice their juxtaposition. "Unusual bandit concentrations" means Ganati military movements. "Maritime traders operating under unusual protocols" means the Westerners. "Administrative reorganization in the eastern provinces" means territory has been lost. The redactors cannot say these things directly, but they can arrange their bland reports so attentive readers assemble the picture themselves.

## **The Grey Sheet**

Classified intelligence that deliberately leaks through controlled channels. The Grey Sheet reaches contractors who sell redacted excerpts to local powers, creating an intelligence gray market the Institute of Domestic Intelligence monitors and manipulates. Recipients believe they have inside information but actually receive what the IDI wants them to know.

The constant leaking serves multiple purposes. It provides pressure release for information that would be more dangerous if completely suppressed. It allows the Lens to shape what "secret" knowledge circulates. And it generates revenue for intermediaries like the Galen family's Ridge-Line Scriptoria, who maintain careful relationships with both the Bureau and their customers.

## **Other Print**

Beyond the Sheets, licensed publications exist for specialized audiences: legal commentaries, Bureau procedural manuals, theological treatises, technical documentation. These are not entertainment, but the legal booksellers who stock them occasionally acquire old novels that sit unsold for decades. Fiction exists but occupies no prestige position. The Empire has no equivalent to Ganat's dozen competing newspapers or its partisan press ecology.

Unperformable scripts circulate through channels the Lens monitors but cannot suppressâ€”plays written for the page rather than the stage, designed to be read aloud in private gatherings or passed hand-to-hand in copied manuscripts. These works address subjects no licensed production could touch: Bureau examinations as absurdist dialogue, hereditary patents as multigenerational traps, audiences with the Emperor as journeys into temporal alienation. The Lens maintains files on these scripts but takes no action; suppressing them would require acknowledging their contents.

# **Theater and Performance**

## **Pantomime Tradition**

Popular theater operates in designated venues in each Whorl, with content varying by audience wealth. Fourth Whorl theaters stage polished productions with professional performers and elaborate costumes. Iron Yards venues are rougherâ€”converted warehouse spaces, amateur players, audiences drinking throughout.

Stock character types recur across productions. The Tsatsan Mystic Swindler speaks in exaggerated palatalization, claims secret knowledge, picks pockets. The Provincial Barbarianâ€”an Akama or Thousand Kingdoms caricatureâ€”looms huge and simple, constantly confused by civilization. The Pompous Bureaucrat quotes regulations endlessly while accomplishing nothing. The Clever Sewer-Fisher solves problems through wit that larger characters miss. The Suffering Penitent offers melodramatic piety played for pathos or comedy depending on the production.

Plots recycle familiar scenarios: inheritance disputes resolved through bureaucratic absurdity, lovers separated by caste complications, rogues outwitting corrupt officials. The humor is physical, the morals conventional, the political content carefully within White Sheet parametersâ€”at least on the surface.

## **Interpretive Divergence**

The same script can produce radically different experiences depending on staging choices. The Last Dusk in AsovoÃ«â€”a canonical tragedy about Highborn lovers dying as ya-Tsovez falls to slave revoltâ€”demonstrates the principle. Traditional productions use every theatrical tool to create the emotional experience the text seems to demand: beautiful costumes emphasizing Highborn modifications as aesthetic achievements, lighting that suggests impossible architectural delicacy, an aria sung with Highborn emotional manipulation techniques that make audiences weep.

A reformist production of the identical script makes different choices. The slavesâ€”who have no linesâ€”become visible, maintaining the gardens and serving the dinners in the background, their presence increasingly hard to ignore as the play progresses. The Highborn costumes emphasize modifications as modifications, joints bending at angles that read as wrong rather than graceful. The aria is sung without emotional manipulation, leaving audiences to hear what the words actually say. The ending holds on the survivors rather than the dying lovers.

Neither production lies. Both interpret. The Lens condemns the reformist staging for "distorting the playwright's evident intentions," and the theater posts the condemnation as advertisement. Audiences attend both versions, sometimes comparing notes, arguing about which is true.

## **The Feast of Reflection**

An annual Church observance that has become the closest thing to licensed misrule. Tradition holds that congregations may perform "dramatic readings" of religious texts with "interpretive elements." The tradition's origins are unclearâ€”possibly genuine liturgical history, possibly legal fiction established to permit entertainment that would otherwise be prohibited.

In practice, the Feast is the one night when theatrical performance occurs in parish halls rather than licensed theaters, subject to Church rather than Lens oversight. The Church's standards differ from the Bureau's. Material that would be fined in a Fourth Whorl theater passes without comment in a dockside chapel. The clergy who review performances tend to focus on theological content rather than political implication, and many clergy are themselves sympathetic to reform.

# **Physical Spectacle**

The caste system's biological diversity creates performance traditions impossible elsewhere. These entertainments exist because humanity has been fragmented into specialized populations with capabilities baseline humans lack.

## **Ironbone Feats**

Strongman shows draw on Labor Caste variants bred for mining and heavy constructionâ€”people whose bones are dense enough to anchor muscles that can lift weights no baseline human could manage. The performances are straightforward: bending metal bars, supporting architectural loads, competitive lifting between performers from different lineages.

The audiences include both genuine appreciation and something darker. Workers watching Ironbone performers might feel pride in what their caste can achieve. Aureates watching the same show might see confirmation that the caste system produces useful specialization. The performer occupies both positions simultaneouslyâ€”artist and specimen, athlete and product.

## **Springheel Acrobatics**

Almost impossible to watch without vertigo. Castes engineered for work in high placesâ€”rigging, tower maintenance, cliff-face constructionâ€”have modified proprioception, altered inner ears, muscle fiber distributions favoring explosive movement. What they do casually as labor becomes breathtaking as performance.

A Springheel troupe might perform on structures that would terrify baseline audiences just to look atâ€”narrow beams at height, swinging platforms, vertical surfaces they traverse like level ground. The entertainment comes partly from the physical feats and partly from the audience's awareness that they themselves could never do this, that they're watching bodies shaped for a different relationship with gravity.

Some troupes develop choreography that references the alienness of their capabilitiesâ€”and some include moments that reference the falls that kill their cousins on construction sites. If the subversion stays aesthetic rather than explicit, the Lens condemnations remain decorative.

## **Anthropological Exhibitions**

The most ethically fraught entertainments, and probably the most popular. Rare castes displayed for examinationâ€”not performing, just existing while audiences observe them. The framing as "anthropological" or "educational" provides cover for what is essentially a human zoo.

Exhibitions might include Sickly individuals under Companion supervision, their rose-gold lattices glowing in dim display lighting; Mutterers demonstrating their computational vocalizations; representatives of frontier variants most Imperial citizens have never seen. The tone is carefully managedâ€”placards describe each caste's function, their engineering history, their place in the Imperial order. Everything emphasizes that these are fellow subjects, valued contributors, not curiosities to gawk at. The gawking happens anyway.

Some exhibitions feature castes that no longer serve their original purposeâ€”colonial variants engineered for worlds humanity can no longer reach, specialists whose functions became obsolete, biological dead ends maintained because the Creche protocols that created them remain technically valid. These carry particular melancholy: bodies shaped for contexts that no longer exist.

## **Highborn Vocalists**

The prestige end of caste-based performance. Highborn modifications that allow simultaneous production of multiple vocal tones transform singing from melody into architecture. A single performer can produce harmonies that would require a choir of baseline humansâ€”and harmonies no baseline choir could produce, intervals and resonances outside normal human vocal range.

The arias written for these performers are genuinely sophisticated art. Composers study what specific Highborn can doâ€”each performer's modifications are slightly differentâ€”and write pieces exploiting their particular capabilities. A famous vocalist might have works composed specifically for their unique throat architecture, pieces no other performer could sing.

The audiences are primarily Aureate, the ticket prices prohibitive for most workers. But the performances also serve social functionsâ€”families demonstrating cultivation by attending, business negotiations in private boxes during intermissions. The Highborn performer is simultaneously artist and status marker.

# **Music Beyond the Stage**

## **Church Hymns**

The primary musical experience for most Imperial subjects. Congregations sing the Plea and associated hymns weekly, the "harmonic resonances" and "mathematical relationships between the notes" carrying theological significance. Wealthy congregations debate these relationships abstractly while poor congregations sing them in difficult conditionsâ€”ankle-deep in salt water, in converted industrial spaces, surrounded by Mutterer vocalizations.

Major observances bring diverse choirs together. The Tenth Observance, for instance, assembles fifteen voices from each participating congregation, "five sections of three representing mathematical perfection." For communities like Valve-Saint Parishâ€”sewer-fishers, dock workers, the forgottenâ€”an invitation to sing at the Cathedral is an opportunity to be seen, to exist publicly in ways their daily lives do not permit.

## **Ya-Tsatsan Quiet Days**

Twice annually, the entire population of ya-Tsatsa gathers on the crater slopes and sings for hours. The sound is discordantâ€”multiple tones layered over each other, rising and falling without resolving, the same droning quality as the city's ceramic fall whistles. No visible conductor coordinates the performance, but everyone knows when to shift pitch, when to sustain, when to pause.

When asked what the singing is for, Tsatsans say it's traditional observance. Quiet Day hymns. Sung for the Oracles. Why those particular tones? That's how they're sung. The archives might have documentation. The archives describe Quiet Days as periods of rest and Oracle veneration. Nothing about why the hymns sound like whistles.

## **Factory-Song**

In the Thousand Kingdoms, tribal groups treat autofactories as spontaneously generative gods. Their singing is elaborate, communal, desperate. Entire villages produce harmonies they believe specify desired outputsâ€”low drones for structural materials, high keening for metals, polyrhythmic chants for specific items. Songs have words in languages no one speaks, passed down phonetically. When production occurs, even randomly, it validates everything.

This practice does not exist within the Empire proper, but knowledge of it circulatesâ€”another reminder that Imperial ways are not the only ways, that other populations have developed other relationships with the Technical Caste inheritance.

# **Visual Arts and Exhibition**

## **The Uninterpreted Past**

Private museums display pre-Interdict materials under strict prohibition against interpretation. Holograms of orbital habitats, photographs of worlds whose names have been forgotten, music recorded when humanity spanned light-years. No placard explains what viewers see. No guide contextualizes the images. Visitors wander through rooms of inexplicable beauty and must construct their own meanings.

This prohibition serves obvious purposesâ€”interpretation would require acknowledging what was lost, and the Empire cannot officially acknowledge loss. But it also creates something unexpected: unmediated encounter with the past. The music means what the listener feels it means. The hologram shows what the viewer imagines it shows.

The museums attract devoted visitors who return repeatedly, building private interpretations shared in whispered conversations. Tavern discussions about "what the Third Gallery shows" are technically illegalâ€”interpretationâ€”but practically tolerated. A subculture of pre-Interdict enthusiasts develops its own vocabulary, its own theories, its own aesthetic preferences. The Lens cannot police every conversation.

Some materials are genuinely beautiful in ways modern production cannot match. Music recorded with technologies no longer functional. Images captured by instruments that no longer exist. The contrast between what the past could make and what the present can make is obvious to anyone paying attention, even without interpretation making it explicit.

## **Contemporary Production**

Imperial visual art follows available materials and sanctioned forms. Carved geometric patterns on building facades demonstrate wealth through craftsmanship depth. Welded sculpture from salvage metal transforms failure into aestheticsâ€”alloys that resist conventional working become decorative elements whose strange properties are features rather than flaws.

Luxury artisans produce pieces for Aureate consumption: mourning crystals whose nested spheres align differently based on the viewer's grief, textiles incorporating data-thread that shifts pattern with temperature, jewelry whose authentication protocols make it simultaneously ornament and encrypted key. These works exist at the intersection of craft and Technical Caste inheritanceâ€”objects whose functions blur the line between art and archaeotech.

# **Processional Spectacle**

Public processions punctuate ordinary life with visual and emotional experiences. The Sickly processionsâ€”citizens lining paths, offering flowers and whispered blessingsâ€”represent public spectacle as civic participation. Watching a Sickly carried through the streets in a palanquin, vesicles glowing rose-gold, attended by Companionsâ€”this is awe and melancholy combined, "inspiring poetry and song."

Other processional events include funeral processions for significant figures, Bureau ceremonial movements, Aureate family displays during calendar observances. Ya-Tsatsa celebrates mysterious events with Mutterers garlanded in wildflowers, carried to buildings whose bronze doors open without mechanism and close without sound. The Empire maintains older ritual forms that serve functions similar to Ganat's revolutionary paradesâ€”collective witness, shared experience, the rhythm of public life.

# **Foreign Media**

## **Ganati Cinema**

Films reach Imperial audiences through smuggled reels and hidden projectors. What arrives is often last year's releases, prints worn from legitimate circulation, sometimes films that failed commercially but found second lives in foreign markets. An Imperial viewer watching Bride of the Republic for the first time experiences something Ganati audiences consider dated propaganda. But it's still more alive than anything the Empire produces.

Viewing happens in private spacesâ€”a warehouse with chairs arranged and a sheet hung for projection, a wealthy home where the host has acquired equipment through channels best not examined, sometimes a commercial establishment that runs a second program after the Lens inspector has collected his payment and departed.

The films show a world where collective action produces results, where workers have political power, where the future might be better than the past. This is propaganda, obviously. But it's propaganda for a society that actually functions, which makes it more subversive than any explicit critique could be.

## **Ganati Radio**

Broadcast requires only reception. A good antenna positioned high enoughâ€”on the Ridge, on upper-Whorl rooftopsâ€”can pull Ganati transmissions when atmospheric conditions permit. The signal fades and strengthens with weather, time of day, factors no one fully understands. But it comes through.

What you hear depends on when you listen. Ministry of Information broadcasts sound bizarre to Imperial earsâ€”a government actually telling citizens about crop yields, about dam maintenance, about mundane governance. Party programming is more obviously ideological, but even Dawn's revolutionary rhetoric carries information the Empire would never publish.

The music hooks most listeners. Frontier ballads from eastern settlements. Working songs from textile kilits. Revolutionary anthems with genuine melody rather than the droning hymns Imperial workers grew up with. The music sounds alive in ways Imperial approved content does not.

## **The Cultural Gap**

The contrast damages Imperial self-image in ways the Lens cannot address. Ganati entertainment assumes its audience is intelligent, politically engaged, capable of handling complexity. Imperial entertainment assumes its audience needs protection from information, from challenge, from anything that might disturb the elaborate fictions maintaining Imperial legitimacy.

An Imperial worker who consumes Ganati media experiences a society that takes people like her seriously. The underlying assumption is that citizens matter, that their opinions should be shaped because their opinions have consequences. This is more dangerous than explicit anti-Imperial content would be. The Lens can suppress direct attacks. It cannot suppress the quiet realization that other ways of organizing society exist.

# **Gambling and Social Spaces**

## **Tavern Culture**

Without Ganat's hookah-den salon system, Imperial drinking establishments serve simpler functions: places to drink after shifts, spaces for negotiation between factions, neutral ground where formal hierarchy loosens slightly. Iron Yards taverns serve "industrial cuisine" with credit arrangements tied to Guild pay cycles. Wealthier establishments in the Fourth Whorl offer better alcohol and more private rooms, but the same basic function.

The taverns are where the White Sheet gets read aloud, where Ganati radio gets discussed, where the interpretations forbidden in print circulate through conversation. They are not political salonsâ€”the Empire lacks the infrastructure for thatâ€”but they are the closest equivalent.

## **Gambling**

Operates everywhere, with sophistication varying by clientele. Simple venues offer straightforward games: dice, cards, betting on fights or races. The ya-Tsatsan establishments represent the high endâ€”seven-sided dice, Testament-suited cards, betting structures incorporating seven-fold branchingâ€”where gambling doubles as religious observance and encrypted communication.

The gambling has religious dimensions even when players don't recognize them. The Oracle Cult's emphasis on stochastic processes as divine revelation means random events carry theological weight. A lucky streak might indicate favor; a losing run might suggest correction. The superstitions are genuine even when the theology is half-understood.

## **The Seventeen Hypotheticals**

A game played in certain back rooms, particularly those with ya-Tsatsan management. Players bet on outcomes to "hypothetical situations" posed by a moderator. The hypotheticals begin simplyâ€”a merchant discovers cheating, what does he do?â€”but become more pointed as the evening progresses. A Bureau inspector discovers corruption in his own office. A worker learns her labor supports something she finds immoral.

The betting patterns carry encoded meanings regular players understand. Certain sequences signal political affiliations. Particular hypotheticals test whether new players can be trusted with more direct conversation. The game operates as entertainment, ideological sorting mechanism, and communication network for people who cannot speak openly. A Lens inspector sitting through an evening would hear nothing actionableâ€”everything is hypothetical, all outcomes determined by chance. The actual communication happens in gaps the inspector cannot perceive.

# **The Political Terrain**

## **Two Centuries of Slow Revolution**

The City-of-Glass Charter ended Creche breeding authority, opened Bureau examinations to all castes, removed automatic Aureate succession. At the time, conservatives called it temporary accommodationâ€”a necessary concession to veterans who had saved the Empire and refused to disarm. The reforms would be rolled back once stability returned.

Two hundred and seven years later, stability never returned, and the reforms became load-bearing. A generation of bureaucrats who entered through examination rather than inheritance now holds real power. Lowborn officials sit on the Satara itself. The conservatives still hold significant positions, but they're fighting a rearguard action against demographic reality.

## **Entertainment as Battleground**

The exhausted censorship is not merely declineâ€”it is contested terrain. The Lens censor who delivers his fine as part of the performance is making a choice about what kind of Empire he wants to live in. The competition among redactors to approach truth without censure reflects genuine ideological division about whether citizens should be informed or protected.

The Ganati media penetration serves reformist interests whether or not reformists facilitate it. Every Imperial subject who watches Ganati films or listens to Ganati radio encounters evidence that the Charter's direction leads somewhere functional. The Republic is proof of conceptâ€”a society that abandoned caste hierarchy and survived.

The museums displaying pre-Interdict materials without interpretation create space for dangerous questions. If the past produced such wonders, what went wrong? The prohibition on interpretation prevents official answers, but it also prevents official lies. Visitors draw their own conclusions.

## **The New Audiences**

The Charter generationâ€”those born after the reforms, who entered adulthood in an Empire where examination success could lead anywhereâ€”consume entertainment differently than their grandparents. They expect engagement rather than numbing. They seek out condemned pantomimes, Ganati broadcasts, museum exhibits that raise questions. They discuss what they've seen in taverns and workplaces, developing interpretations the Lens cannot control.

These audiences are disproportionately represented in the examining classesâ€”bureaucrats, clerks, technical specialists, skilled workers who passed tests to reach their positions. They proved capability through merit. They have less patience for entertainment that assumes they need protection from complexity.

## **The Long Game**

The reformists on the Satara do not commission this entertainment or coordinate its messages. They don't need to. The entertainment emerges from the same social forces that produced reformist politicsâ€”the Charter generation's expectations, the demographic shift away from hereditary privilege, the Ganati example proving alternatives exist. Cultural production and political production reinforce each other without central direction.

The conservatives see this happening and cannot stop it. They can fine individual productions but cannot fine the interpretive habits audiences bring to everything they see. They can suppress individual scripts but cannot suppress the questions the scripts ask. They can maintain the legal fiction that the Empire has not changed, but they cannot prevent people from experiencing how much it has.

The entertainment is not revolution. It is the soil in which revolution growsâ€”slowly, patiently, measured in generations. For the first time in recorded memory, the future might be different from the past.

# **Appendix: Sample Productions**

## **The Redactor's Dilemma**

A comedy in three acts, currently playing in the Fourth Whorl's Threshold Theater. The Lens condemnation calls it "a work of DELIBERATE CONFUSION regarding the proper functions of Imperial information management" and runs to four paragraphs. Sri Redactor Kemmen discovers that telling truth and telling lies produce identical outcomes because no one reads his work carefully. The comedy escalates as he attempts to satisfy impossible requirements, eventually producing a White Sheet edition consisting entirely of punctuation marks, which his readers praise as "the clearest issue in years."

## **The Verenis Troupe**

A Springheel acrobatic company operating out of the Iron Yards. Their signature piece, "The Scaffold," recreates a construction accident in slow motion. Performers build an actual scaffold onstage, then climb it while the structure progressively fails. The piece ends with collapse, the performers having transferred to ropes that were not provided by any employer. The program notes describe it as "celebration of Labor Caste capability." The actual performance communicates something else entirely.

Visitors stand before it for hours, sometimes weeping. The family has refused offers to purchase it at prices that would make them among the wealthiest in the Empire.

The es-Poel patents are too valuable.
