## Accounting

The tide was coming in. Aram es-Thabit could hear it through the grated floor sections of the parish hall—that particular susurration of water finding its level against corroded metal. In three hours, the congregants at evening service would stand ankle-deep in brine while the great wheel altar collected another day's worth of carved prayers. For now, though, the overflow chamber held only his four deacons and the persistent mathematical muttering from the Mutterer nests above.

They'd arranged themselves around the salvaged table that served for these meetings, its surface scarred by decades of different purposes. Mer Hasket had his ledger open, thick fingers tracing columns of numbers he'd checked four times already. The grain dole accounts. Sela Loak sat opposite, arms crossed, watching him with an expression that managed to be both patient and withering. Kesh Ritt hunched over his own notes—medical supplies, serum allocations, the names of the sick written in his careful hand. Brann Olt stood by the door, because Brann always stood by the door, his drag-hook hanging loose at his side.

"Twenty-three sacks," Mer said finally. "That's what we have. Twenty-three sacks of grain after this morning's distribution."

Sela leaned forward. "And how many days does that buy us?"

Mer's face tightened. He hated these calculations, the way they forced him to reduce human need to arithmetic. "Four days at current ration. Maybe five if we cut portions."

"We're already at half-portions," Kesh said quietly. "Any less and people won't have strength to work their shifts. The dock crews are already stumbling."

Above them, the Mutterers' vocalization shifted pitch—some optimization cascade beginning and ending in the space of a breath. Aram waited for it to settle before speaking.

"What about the vat-kitchen supplies?"

Sela's expression darkened. "The potato starch is holding, but we're down to dregs on the miso. The mussel beds are producing, but the Bannick clan's raised their prices. Again." She paused, calculating. "We can keep the kitchen running for a week, maybe ten days if we substitute more algae paste. People won't like it."

"People don't have to like it," Brann said from the door. "They just have to eat it."

It was the kind of practical brutality that made Brann useful, even if it grated against everything Aram tried to build here. The young man—part Warborn, built like a loading crane—understood survival in ways that Aram, despite his renunciations, still struggled to grasp fully.

"The medical supplies are the real problem," Kesh said, pulling them back to the immediate crisis. "We're burning through the antiseptic reserves. The fever cases need constant cleaning, and the Companions are restricting serum access." He looked up at Aram. "They say my provisional status doesn't authorize emergency dispensation rates."

"How many fever cases now?" Aram asked.

"Forty-three confirmed. Maybe another twenty showing early symptoms." Kesh's voice carried the exhaustion of someone who'd been counting the sick for too many days. "The Mutterers are helping with basic care, but they keep trying to 'improve' the bedframes. I've had to station someone just to ring the disruption chimes every hour."

Mer had been scratching figures while Kesh talked, his arithmetic laborious but determined. "If we redirect funds from the building maintenance pool—"

"No." Sela's interruption was sharp. "The pumps need their quarterly service, or we flood. That's not negotiable."

"The Valvehands could—" Mer began.

"The Valvehands already take their percentage for keeping the Chain-Men from setting up toll points at our door," Sela said. "We can't ask for more without offering something in return, and we have nothing to offer."

The mathematical muttering above peaked again, then subsided. One of the Mutterers had probably found a more efficient arrangement for its nest materials. They did that sometimes—small optimizations that served no purpose except the optimization itself.

Aram studied his deacons. Each carried their piece of the parish's burden, shaped by what they'd been before they came here. Mer, who'd spent his youth loading cargo, understood weight and volume but struggled when those quantities became abstractions. Sela, raised in the salvage trades, saw everything as a system of inputs and outputs that could be rebalanced if you were clever enough. Kesh, trained by the Companions, thought in terms of biological processes that could be managed but never truly controlled. And Brann—Brann thought in terms of force and counter-force, protection and threat.

"The capitation tax is due next week," Aram said. It was the detail none of them wanted to acknowledge. The Empire's levy on registered Penitents, designed to discourage exactly what they were doing here.

"How much?" Sela asked.

"Three hundred obols. For the quarter."

Silence. Even the Mutterers seemed to pause in their eternal calculations.

"We don't have it," Mer said unnecessarily.

"The wealthier parishes—" Kesh began.

"Are supporting seventeen other congregations in the Midden Tenements alone," Sela finished. "The well's run dry there."

Brann shifted by the door. "Luka Fen would—"

"No." Aram's voice was quiet but firm. "We don't take money from the Knot."

"Even if it means closing the clinic?" Brann's question held no challenge, just pragmatic assessment.

"Especially then."

The tide was rising faster now. Soon, the first wavelets would push through the grates, bringing the harbor's smell of rust and rot and life persisting despite everything. The parish would smell of salt for hours afterward, that particular mineral benediction that reminded everyone what they were built on—an old machine that barely worked, maintained by faith as much as engineering.

"There's another option," Sela said slowly. "The electronics salvage from Lantern-Heaps. My nephew's crew pulled three crates of capacitor boards last week. Pre-Interdict manufacture. The Ridge families would pay well for those."

"That's clan property," Mer said. "Not parish."

"It's my nephew," Sela repeated. "I can arrange something. A loan against future recovery. Interest, but manageable."

Aram watched her face. Sela Loak had spent twenty years navigating the informal economies of the Middens. She understood obligation and reciprocity in ways that had nothing to do with Bureau documentation or Imperial law. This loan would create debts, not just financial but social, that would ripple through the clan networks for months.

"What rate?" he asked.

"Twenty percent. Monthly."

Kesh whistled low. "That's—"

"Fair, given the circumstances," Sela said. "And it keeps us operating."

The Mutterers above began a new optimization cycle, their vocalizations overlapping in harmonics that almost resembled song. Aram had learned to find it soothing, that sound of minds that couldn't help but solve problems, even problems that didn't exist.

"Do it," he said finally. "But document everything. Every obol, every transaction."

Sela nodded. She understood. The parish survived on transparency, on the fact that anyone—Bureau inspector, Knot enforcer, suspicious congregant—could examine their books and find nothing but careful poverty carefully managed.

"What about the grain?" Mer asked, still staring at his ledger.

"We cut portions," Aram said. "Quarter-rations for anyone who can supplement elsewhere. Full rations for the sick and those working the pump maintenance."

"People will complain," Mer warned.

"Let them. Better complaints than corpses."

It was harsher than he usually spoke, but the mathematics were inexorable. They could maintain the fiction of abundance for another few days, or they could stretch their resources to cover the crisis's duration. The choice was hardly a choice at all.

"I can probably get another shipment of antiseptic," Kesh offered. "There's a Companion journeyman who owes me a favor. It won't be official Guild supply, but it'll work."

"Do it," Aram said. Then, to Brann: "What's the situation with the Chain-Men?"

"Tense. Jarek's crews are pushing harder on the toll collections. They know we're stretched." Brann's partial Warborn heritage showed in the way he assessed threat—constant, automatic, precise. "They won't move against the parish directly, not yet. But they're watching."

"Let them watch."

The water was definitely coming in now. Aram could see the first dark patches spreading across the floor where the seawater seeped through. In an hour, the patches would be puddles. In two hours, a shallow lake that turned the parish into an island.

"Anything else?" he asked.

His deacons looked at each other, reading the complex emotional mathematics of shared burden. They were tired, all of them. Tired of making impossible decisions, of stretching nothing into barely something, of maintaining hope in a world that seemed designed to extinguish it.

"The evening service," Sela said finally. "What's the sermon?"

Aram hadn't decided yet. He could speak about endurance, about the virtue of suffering that the Penitent Church preached. He could talk about the saint for whom their parish was named, who'd turned a wheel that couldn't possibly have worked but worked anyway. He could recite from the old texts about humanity's culpability and the mercy that came from accepting judgment.

But looking at his deacons—at Mer's laborious pride in his columns of numbers, at Sela's fierce pragmatism, at Kesh's exhausted compassion, at Brann's protective vigilance—he knew what he'd actually say.

"I'll talk about the logistics. How you organize distribution for five thousand. How you prevent riots when there isn't enough. How you make sure everyone gets something, even if it's not enough." He managed a tired smile. "I'll make it practical."

Sela actually laughed—a short, sharp sound that cut through the Mutterers' mathematics. "The Bureau would have your head for that interpretation."

"The Bureau doesn't attend evening service in the Midden Tenements."

They dispersed after that, each to their particular labor. Mer to recount his figures one more time. Sela to negotiate with her nephew. Kesh to his rounds among the sick. Brann to his watch at the door.

Aram remained, listening to the water rise, thinking about resources and their distribution. Above, the Mutterers continued their optimizations, solving problems that existed only in the abstract space of pure mathematics. Below, the ancient pumps continued their work, moving water from one place to another according to principles that barely anyone understood anymore.

The parish existed between these extremes—between the incomprehensible and the mechanical, between transcendence and subsistence. They distributed what they had according to need, or as close to need as they could calculate. They maintained their accounts with obsessive precision because precision was the only thing standing between them and dissolution.

The wheel altar stood in its place, accumulating prayers that would never turn it, that would never activate pumps it had never controlled. But people needed to believe that their actions mattered, that somewhere in the chain of cause and effect, human will could still make a difference.
