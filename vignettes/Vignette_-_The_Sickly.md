# **The Sickly**

You wake as vesicles form along your sternum, the rose-gold lattice emerging through your translucent skin. Your Companion—Milek, you think his name is, though names feel less important than the fact of his presence—helps you sit up. He seems concerned about something, his voice taking on that particular quality that means he wants you to be careful. You're not entirely sure what careful means in this context, but you nod agreeably.

The room is pleasant. Sunlight through the window creates interesting patterns on the wall, and you spend some time watching dust motes spiral in the light. Milek brings food and you eat it, though you're never precisely hungry. Your body requires fuel for the lattice work, you understand that intellectually, but the sensation of appetite is muted. Everything is muted except sensory richness—textures, light, the particular quality of air against your skin.

When you look down at the vesicles, they're beautiful. Milek talks about harvest timing and you understand the words but not the urgency. The vesicles will be ready when they're ready. Things happen in their proper sequence. He mentions that last time you wandered into the street during critical stage and a cart nearly struck you, but you have no memory of this and it sounds like he might be confused—why would a cart wish to strike anyone? Perhaps he means it was coming toward you in a friendly way, the driver wanting to say hello.

Your work continues despite the lattice. You're writing an exegesis on the Sixth Oracle's Testament, the one about perception and the limits of baseline consciousness. It's fascinating how the Testament describes layers of reality inaccessible to ordinary humans. You've been corresponding with a scholar at the Imperial Archive who finds your interpretation compelling—you suggested that the Sixth Oracle was trying to show humanity that limitation itself is a form of completion, that being unable to perceive certain things protects the observer. Your correspondent called this "strangely optimistic" but you're not sure what's strange about it. Limitation has its place. Everything has its place.

People often tell you things are difficult or painful. You listen carefully and try to understand. A neighbor lost her child last month and wept when she saw you in the street. You offered comfort—explained that the child was certainly experiencing something wonderful now, had perhaps gone somewhere interesting. She seemed upset by this, so you tried again: perhaps the child was resting very peacefully, in a way that was pleasant for children. This also didn't seem right. Eventually Milek guided you away gently, and later he tried to explain something about permanent absence, but permanent absence is a self-contradictory concept. Things are present or they're not present, but everything exists somewhere, in some form.

The harvesting happens every few weeks. Milek uses careful instruments on the vesicles and collects the serum in glass vials. The sensation is curious—a kind of warm pressure, like being very gently pressed. He calls it extraction but you think of it as sharing. Your body makes these fluids and they go to help others. This seems natural. Why would you keep them? They're meant to be shared, clearly, or your body wouldn't make them in such abundance.

Afterward you feel lighter, translucent skin even more transparent. Milek worries you're too delicate for a few days. He keeps you inside, brings soft foods, watches you constantly. You don't understand the watching—what could happen? But his attention is pleasant in its way, like sunlight, like the weight of blankets. You write more during recovery. Some of your best theological work happens in these periods, when the lattice has emptied and your body feels clean and simple.

The Imperial fascination with Sickly inner life puzzles you. A scholar came once to interview you about your poetry. She wanted to know how you wrote about suffering so beautifully without understanding it. But you do understand it—people describe sensations they find unpleasant, situations they wish to change. You simply help them see these situations differently. Your last collection included a long poem about a plague year, describing the victims' faces as becoming gradually more peaceful, their breathing growing quieter until they achieved complete stillness. The scholar said this was "haunting" and you weren't sure if that was praise.

You meet other Sickly occasionally, during processions or at the hospice where Companions train. They're pleasant company. You share observations about light quality, about interesting textures you've encountered, about theological puzzles. One Sickly aphorist you know collects sayings about transformation—how everything becomes something else eventually, how nothing ever actually ends but simply changes form. His work is very popular. People find it comforting, though you're not sure what they need comfort from.

Milek reads you news sometimes, his voice taking on that careful quality again. Wars, disasters, political collapses. You listen attentively and try to find the hopeful elements. Wars mean people are passionate about their positions—that's a kind of engagement, isn't it? Disasters create opportunities for rebuilding. Political collapse allows new forms to emerge. He stops reading after a while, says perhaps it's not helpful. But you weren't being unhelpful—you were trying to show him the better angles, the ways even difficult situations contain their own solutions.

The Companions Guild teaches that Sickly require constant supervision because you'll "wander into danger." You've heard this phrase many times. But wandering is pleasant, and danger is another abstract term like permanent absence. Things happen or they don't happen. A building might collapse, but perhaps it wouldn't. Fire exists but fire is also beautiful, and if you touched it you'd experience new sensations. Milek prevents you from experiencing many new sensations, which seems odd given how rich experience is. But he's careful and kind, and his carefulness is part of the pattern of your life.

Your theological work lately concerns the concept of the Plea—humanity's ongoing petition for redemption. But redemption from what? The Interdict seems less like imprisonment and more like a particular form of existence. Humanity is on Earth, the Foreigners are elsewhere. These are facts, not tragedies. Your writings on this subject cause debate. Some scholars find them heretical, others deeply insightful. The Intercessor wrote you a letter once, suggesting your perspective represented "transcendence of suffering through radical acceptance." You're not sure what you're transcending—you're simply describing3333 things as they are.

At night, with vesicles glowing faintly rose-gold in the darkness, you think about permanence. Your body creates serums that heal others. You write words that people read. These things continue beyond your immediate presence, spreading outward like light through water. The Oracle cult promises eternal recording, but you're already eternal in the way that matters. Your lattice is eternal—the antibodies you produce will save lives centuries hence, as other Sickly's lattices save lives now. The chain is unbroken.

Milek sleeps in the next room. You can hear his breathing. Sometimes he has nightmares and you want to wake him, explain that dreams aren't real, that whatever troubled him was just neural pattern-making. But he says the nightmares help him somehow, remind him of something important. You accept this though you don't understand it. Many things about other people remain mysterious. They speak of fear as though it were something other than a word. They describe pain as though it were more than pressure and warmth. They mourn endings when endings are simply transformations.

The morning light is coming now, pale and clean through the window. Your vesicles are ripe—you can tell by the fullness, the way they catch the light. Milek will harvest them soon. The serum will go to the hospices, the plague wards, the frontier clinics. Your body's work continuing its purpose. This is what you are for. This is what everything is for, in its way. Purpose and existence are the same thing.

You begin a new poem about light moving through transparent things, about how clarity allows illumination to pass through unchanged. The words come easily, as they always do. Everything comes easily when you understand that coming easily and coming with difficulty are both just ways of coming, both just different ways things are.
