**Vey, Trip to Port Ahn**

The lamp swayed with the ship's roll, throwing shadows across the forward hatch where the guards had spread a worn canvas. Vey sat cross-legged, trying to match their posture despite his knees protesting the unfamiliar position. The dice—Kherzen's weighted set—sat between them in a leather cup.

"Engineer drinks first," Ghom announced, producing a clay bottle from somewhere. "Guest rule."

The liquid hit Vey's throat like industrial solvent mixed with winter forest. Spruce and mint fought with something that tasted like lamp fuel, while coriander provided an aftertaste that somehow made everything worse. His eyes watered. The guards watched, amused.

"Good, yes?" Tselkor asked innocently.

"It's..." Vey coughed. "What is this?"

"Whatever grain we could buy," Buragh said, taking the bottle and drinking deeply without reaction. "Plus what grows. Spruce from the mountains. Mint from someone's garden. Makes it medicine, not just drink."

Kherzen picked up the dice, rolling them between his fingers. "You know dice?"

"I understand probability."

They laughed—not mockingly, just amused. "Probability," Ghom repeated. "Engineer word. Here's the game: roll three dice. Count the dots. Closest to thirteen without going over wins the pot. You go over, you drink and add twice your bet."

Simple enough. Except Vey could feel the weight distribution in the dice when Kherzen handed them over. Minutely off-center, probably lead drops embedded in the resin. He could calculate the bias, but that would require multiple rolls to map the probability space, and—

"Stop thinking," Kherzen said. "Your fingers know. Just roll."

Vey rolled. Eleven. Safe, not winning.

"See?" Tselkor threw and got fourteen. He drank without complaint, added coins to the pot. "Iron-Rain got seventeen once. Drank half a bottle, then punched through a door because it was faster than opening it."

"Door was locked," Ghom corrected. "That's different."

"Door was decorative," Buragh added. "Not meant for opening."

They passed the bottle around. The liquor got marginally better on the second drink, or Vey's throat had gone numb. The ship creaked. Somewhere above, the Ganati crew was singing their revolutionary hymns.

"Crossed the line two hours ago," Kherzen mentioned. "Imperial waters now."

There was a shift in the air. Not quite tension, but acknowledgment. North of here, Ghom could walk into any warband camp and find cousins. South of here, they were all foreigners.

"You been south before?" Vey asked.

"Twice," Ghom said. "Escort work. Imperial merchants pay well for guards who don't ask questions." He rolled the dice. Twelve. "They kept checking if I could read. Like that mattered for hitting things."

"Can you?"

"Numbers. Enough to know when they're shorting payment." He studied Vey. "Your people—Kma-Dhol. That true what they say?"

Vey tensed slightly. "Depends what they say."

"That you've got clever souls. Two souls, maybe. One for living, one for..." Buragh gestured vaguely. "Making things work wrong."

"Or work right," Kherzen added. "Depending."

In Ogon, this conversation would carry different weight. The superstitions ran deep—don't let Kma-Dhol handle your weapons, they'll jam. Watch them with money, their clever souls make numbers dance. But here, in the lamplight, with Imperial water under the keel, it felt more like curiosity than accusation.

"We're just good with patterns," Vey said carefully.

"Pattern like these dice?" Kherzen held them up. "You knew they were weighted. Your fingers went tense."

"The weight distribution is off."

"But you rolled anyway."

"Guest rule," Vey said.

They found that funny. Tselkor rolled, got exactly thirteen, and whooped victory. "See? Luck beats patterns."

"Tell the pig story," Buragh suggested.

"Which pig story?" Ghom asked.

"Iron-Rain and the war-pig."

They all started laughing before the story even began. Tselkor tried to tell it through gasps: "So there's this destrier breeder who thinks he can make war-pigs. Like destriers but pigs. Armored pigs. For some reason. And he's showing them to es-Valent—the Colonel down south—trying to sell them."

"They're ridiculous," Kherzen interjected. "Size of cattle but shaped wrong. Squealing."

"And one breaks loose," Tselkor continued. "Charging through the camp. Everyone's running, climbing walls. But Iron-Rain just stands there. Thing hits him full speed—bam\!" He slapped his hands together. "Iron-Rain doesn't move. Pig bounces off, lands on its back, legs kicking air."

"Iron-Rain looks down," Ghom picked up the story, "and says 'Your pig is broken.' Then walks away. Just leaves it there, upside down, squealing."

"Es-Valent didn't buy the pigs," Buragh added unnecessarily.

Vey found himself laughing despite the image of a weaponized pig being somehow horrifying. The bottle came around again. The liquor had evolved from punishment to something almost warming.

"He really doesn't feel pain?" Vey asked.

"Feels it," Ghom corrected. "Doesn't care. We were fighting the Torek band two winters back. Arrow goes through his shoulder. Clean through. He pulls it out the front, looks at it, says 'Good arrow,' and keeps fighting."

"Uses it to stab someone," Tselkor added.

"That was a different fight."

"Right, right. The arrow thing was the Sevak raid."

They spent ten minutes reconstructing which battle involved which impalement, treating grievous wounds like weather—notable but not particularly important. The dice went around. Vey won a hand, lost three, started to understand the weight bias well enough to play with intent rather than calculation.

"Why ya-Sattra?" Kherzen asked eventually. "Long way for family."

"Distant family," Vey said. The liquor made the half-truth easier. "Lost connections. Want to reestablish them."

"Must be important family."

"Maybe. Or maybe they're fishmongers who won't acknowledge me."

"Then you come back," Ghom said simply. "Knowing is better than wondering."

The ship rolled harder, and Tselkor went slightly green. "Going to feed the fish," he announced, stumbling toward the rail.

"Young," Ghom said apologetically.

"We're all young here," Buragh observed. "South of the line, everyone's young. Even the Empire. You know they claim ten thousand years? But the records go confused after five hundred. Everything older might be stories."

"The Kma-Dhol remember longer," Kherzen said, then glanced at Vey. "Truth?"

Vey considered. "We remember patterns. Technical patterns. How to maintain things, how to fix things. But who built them, why—that's mostly gone."

"Clever souls for clever memories," Ghom said, not unkindly.

The lamp oil was running low, making the shadows deeper. Tselkor returned, looking better, and immediately lost another hand. The bottle was nearly empty. Vey's throat had completely surrendered to the spruce-and-fuel taste.

"We dock at Port Ahn in five days," Ghom mentioned. "Imperial checkpoint. They'll want to know everyone's business."

"What do we tell them?"

"Truth. Guards escorting an engineer to ya-Sattra. They'll check the charter, tax us for existing, let us through." He paused. "They might test you. Make sure you're actually Kma-Dhol, not someone pretending."

"Test how?"

"Bring something broken. See if you can fix it. Or sing to something, see if it responds." Ghom shrugged. "Imperials are suspicious of their own shadows."

"Can you?" Tselkor asked. "Sing to machines?"

Every Akama superstition about factory-singing rolled through that question. Vey weighed his answer. "Sometimes. If the harmonics are right. If the machine is listening."

They accepted this as perfectly reasonable. The dice made a final round. Buragh won the pot, scooping coins with satisfaction. The lamp finally guttered out, leaving only moonlight through the hatch.

"Sleep," Ghom announced. "Tomorrow the waves get worse."

They scattered to their bedrolls. Vey made his way back to his spot by the stern, head spinning slightly from the liquor. The coast was invisible in the darkness. Somewhere ahead, Port Ahn waited with Imperial checkpoints and suspicious officials. Beyond that, ya-Sattra and whatever answers the fishers might provide.

But tonight he'd played weighted dice with Akama warriors, drunk their horrific liquor, laughed at stories about invulnerable men fighting armored pigs. They'd called him Engineer, not with mockery but with the same tone they'd say Warrior or Merchant. A recognition of function.

Guest rule, Ghom had said. South of the line, they were all guests in the Empire's unwelcoming house. That was its own kind of belonging.
