**The Black Box Inheritance**

Sri Custodian Ema es-Tel had developed, over seventeen years, a perfect routine for handling the Requests. When the pneumatic tube thundered—always between three and four in the morning—she would rise from bed, walk to the sealed chamber that constituted the entirety of the es-Yatani patent holdings, and perform the Procedure exactly as her mother had taught her, exactly as her grandmother had taught her mother, reaching back eleven generations to when the first es-Yatani had accepted a patent they weren't allowed to read.

Tonight's Request arrived at 3:21 AM. The brass tube shrieked its arrival, waking the entire estate. Pell's husband groaned but didn't stir—he'd learned to sleep through it. Her daughter Kess, sixteen and beginning her training, met her at the chamber door.

"Observe carefully," Pell said, though she knew Kess had been observing for years. "Every step exactly as prescribed."

The chamber itself was a cube of pre-Interdict metal, seamless except for the door and various ports. Inside sat the Apparatus—a black rectangular solid the size of a coffin, covered in panels that displayed symbols in languages the family had never seen elsewhere. They weren't even sure which end was the top.

Pell opened the Request, which came on the special paper that dissolved if you tried to copy it. As always, it was largely incomprehensible:

*PRIORITY YELLOW-SEVEN-SEVEN*  
 *AUTHENTICATION: \[string of numbers that hurt to look at\]*  
 *IMPLEMENT PATTERN: Ascending Meridian with Variant Nine*  
 *TIMELINE: Immediate*  
 *COMPENSATION: Standard Rate Plus Urgency Modifier*

"Ascending Meridian with Variant Nine," Pell repeated to Kess, who wrote it in the family's operational log. They had no idea what any of the Patterns meant, but they'd developed their own interpretations over the generations.

Pell approached the Device with the ancient key—a crystalline rod that was the actual patent, the only thing that could activate whatever this was. She inserted it into what they called Port Three (though the ports weren't numbered), then began the Ascending Meridian pattern: Port Three to Port Seven, Port Seven to Port One, Port One to Port Five. The Variant Nine meant she had to hold each connection for nine heartbeats instead of the standard six.

The Device hummed. Not audibly—the sound happened inside their bones, made their teeth ache. The displays flickered through symbols faster than eyes could track. Somewhere in the First Whorl, something was happening that required whatever the Device did.

"Why don't we ever see what comes out?" Kess asked, the question every es-Yatani asked eventually.

"Because nothing comes out. Nothing goes in. It just... processes."

The compensation notice arrived before the Device even finished its cycle. Eight hundred thousand obols, deposited directly to family accounts. For five minutes of following a procedure they didn't understand, implementing patterns they couldn't comprehend, on a Device whose purpose was so classified that even its operators couldn't know it.

The Device's hum ceased. The displays returned to their resting configuration—symbols that might have been numbers or might have been warnings or might have just been decoration.

"Mother," Kess said carefully, "what if we're doing it wrong?"

Pell had wondered the same thing for seventeen years. "Then presumably they'd stop paying us."

But that was the terrifying thing—how would anyone know? The First Whorl clerks who sent the Requests probably didn't understand the Device either. They were likely following their own inherited procedures, sending Requests based on criteria established so long ago that the original purpose had been buried under centuries of classification stamps.

Three nights later, another Request arrived. This one was different—marked with red seals Pell had only seen twice before.

*PRIORITY APEX*  
*AUTHENTICATION: BLACKDOORBLACKDOORBLACKDOORBLACKDOOR*

*IMPLEMENT PATTERN: Complete Reset Protocol*  
*TIMELINE: WITHIN TWENTY MINUTES*  
*COMPENSATION: Annual Income Equivalent*

Pell's hands shook. The Complete Reset Protocol was the family nightmare—twenty-seven steps that had to be performed in exact sequence with no margin for error. Her great-aunt had attempted it forty years ago and gotten step nineteen wrong. The Device had screamed—actually screamed, like something alive and suffering—for six hours. The family had not received another Request for two years after that. They'd nearly gone bankrupt.

"Get the full manual," she told Kess.

The manual was three hundred pages of procedures in their family's handwriting, accumulated notes and observations about patterns, timings, and sequences. None of it explained what the Device did. Page one had a single typed line in ancient script: "ONLY PRECISION IS REQUIRED."

They began the reset. Port to port, key turned at specific angles, patterns within patterns. Step fifteen required both of them—one to hold connections while the other adjusted something they called the "frequency dial" though it probably wasn't. Step twenty-three made the lights in the entire district flicker. Step twenty-six created a smell like ozone and burnt hair, and the things which happened became vague. Peculiar.

On step twenty-seven, Ema recalled that she inserted the key into all seven ports simultaneously—an impossibility, she knew. The Device shuddered, went silent, then resumed its quiet humming.

The compensation arrived instantly: 1.2 million obols. More than the family usually saw in a year.

"What did we just do?" Kess whispered.

"I don't know. Your grandmother didn't know. Her grandmother didn't know." Emalocked the chamber, hands still trembling. "Someone in the First Whorl needed it done."

They returned to bed, leaving the Device to its incomprehensible purpose. Tomorrow, Emawould review the procedures with Kess again, adding notes about tonight's reset to the manual. In a few days, another Request would arrive. They would perform it exactly as required, collect their payment, and continue their guardianship of something that might be critical to Imperial function or might be a bureaucratic echo of a system no one remembered creating.

The next morning, Ema noticed something she'd never seen before. The morning light through her window seemed wrong—not darker or brighter, just wrong in a way she couldn't articulate. It lasted only a moment, then everything was normal again.

She didn't add this to the manual. Some observations were too strange even for a family that lived on incomprehensible procedures. But she wondered, as she always wondered, what patterns they were really implementing, what processes they were enabling, what consequences rippled out from their midnight manipulations of the Device.

The Empire paid them not to know. So they didn't ask.

But sometimes, in the bone-deep hum of the Device's operation, Ema thought she could almost hear something—the heartbeat of something vast and patient. She taught Kess the procedures exactly as they'd been taught to her. Only precision was required.
