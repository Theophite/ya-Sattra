# **The Return**

## **I.**

I was sold twice before I understood what selling meant.

The first time, I was seven years old and the village council decided I would go south with the traders. I did not experience this as loss. The council had determined the sale; the determination became action; I went where the determination sent me. The traders were not unkind. They fed me adequately and did not strike me and spoke to me in words I eventually understood. The journey took six weeks. At its end, I was in a place where the soil was red instead of grey and the horizon stretched flat in every direction and people spoke with sounds that scraped the throat instead of rolling from the tongue.

The second time, I was twelve and the factor who owned my contract died without heirs. His estate was liquidated. I was part of the estate. A man with scaled skin—Serrulata, I later learned, a caste adapted for desert survival—purchased my contract and transported me further south, to a manor house on high plains where the wind never stopped and the crops grew in patterns that should not have sustained life.

I was thirty-four when I decided to leave.

The decision did not come easily. Decisions never come easily to Draëthen. We are made for consensus, for gradual alignment, for the slow emergence of collective determination. To decide alone, without the presence of others whose positions could be felt and incorporated, was to work against the structure of my own mind.

But I was alone. I had been alone for twenty-seven years. The other Draëthen in the March—there were perhaps a dozen of us, scattered across the seven territories—did not form consensus with me. We were too dispersed, too isolated, too shaped by different circumstances. When I encountered another of my caste, we would stand near each other for a time, feeling the pull toward alignment, but the pull was always interrupted by the demands of owners who did not understand what they were preventing.

The Mor-Kethran did not understand. They understood that I could maintain their Antediluvian systems—the irrigation controls, the heating panels, the devices whose functions I could optimize without comprehending. They understood that I worked without complaint, that I required direction but completed tasks with precision, that I cost less to maintain than the horses they used for riding. They did not understand that I was incomplete, that the silence where consensus should have been was a wound that did not heal, that I had spent twenty-seven years doing what I was told while something inside me slowly became capable of wanting something I had not been told to want.

I wanted to go home.

## **II.**

The March of Keth-Amran occupied high plains in the eastern reaches of Ganati territory—or what had been Ganati territory, before the Revolution, when the Sultanate granted the land to ancestors of the current lord. The arrangement persisted through regime change. The Republic did not abolish the marches. It simply declined to acknowledge them, creating a legal fiction where the Mor-Kethran were citizens of Ganat representing a constituency of pastoralist bands, rather than feudal lords ruling a population of serfs.

The serfs—of whom I was one, though my contract distinguished me from the agricultural laborers—worked the Antediluvian crops that made the territory valuable. The crops should not have grown in the high plains' thin soil and sparse rainfall. They grew anyway, following instructions encoded in their genetics before the Interdict, producing yields that conventional agriculture could not match. The serfs planted and harvested and processed the grain that fed populations hundreds of miles away. They did not own the land they worked or the crops they grew or, in any meaningful sense, themselves.

I maintained the systems that kept the crops productive. The irrigation controls. The climate modification panels. The storage facilities whose preservation functions I could not explain but could adjust. The work was adequate for my capabilities. It did not satisfy me, but Draëthen are not made to be satisfied by work performed in isolation. We are made to be satisfied by consensus achieved through collective labor, by the alignment of purpose across a group working toward shared goals. Satisfaction through isolated competence is a poor substitute, but it is what the circumstances permitted.

The journey I planned would take me northwest through the Malpais—the scablands where Akama warbands operated without Republican or Imperial oversight. Then north past Ogon, where the autofactory had transformed the Columbia River into something geometrically impossible. Then further north, through territories that grew increasingly marginal, until I reached the Sound and the villages where my caste had originated before the diaspora scattered us across the continent.

Twelve hundred miles, perhaps more depending on the routes I could find. Through territory where a lone traveler could be killed for the contents of their pack, or captured for labor, or simply lost in landscapes that offered no landmarks and no mercy.

I did not expect to survive. But I had decided to try, and the decision had finally emerged after twenty-seven years of not-quite-deciding, and once a Draëthen decision emerges it becomes action without further deliberation.

I left in spring, when the traders' caravans departed the manor and the confusion of their departure provided cover for my absence.

## **III.**

The first two weeks were the most dangerous.

The Mor-Kethran maintained Akama mercenaries—warriors from the Thousand Kingdoms who enforced the lord's will in exchange for payment, housing, and permission to maintain customs the Republic officially prohibited. If the lord discovered my absence quickly, he would send them after me. The mercenaries rode destriers—the carnivorous mounts engineered before the Interdict—and could cover in a day what would take me a week on foot.

I traveled at night when I could, hiding during daylight in the shallow ravines that cut the high plains. I carried dried provisions, a waterskin, a knife I had made from scrap metal over the course of three years, and nothing else. The less I carried, the further I could travel before exhaustion stopped me.

The plains gave way to broken country as I moved northwest. The elevation dropped. The soil changed from red to grey to black. The vegetation thinned until I was walking across bare volcanic rock—the Malpais, the badlands, the scablands that extended from the high desert to the coast.

I encountered my first Akama warband on the eleventh day.

I heard them before I saw them: the clicking that destriers made, a rhythmic sound like clock mechanisms that changed tempo before the animals moved. I froze against a basalt outcropping and watched as twenty riders passed perhaps two hundred meters from my position, their mounts barely touching the ground between leaps, covering distance at speeds nothing else could match.

The riders did not see me. Or if they saw me, they did not consider me worth pursuing. A lone near-baseline on foot in the Malpais was either already dead or would be soon. The warband had other business.

I waited until the clicking faded completely, then continued northwest.

## **IV.**

The villages of the Malpais survived through calibrated submission.

I learned this in the third week, when I approached a settlement hoping to trade labor for food. The village occupied a hollow where a spring emerged from the basalt—perhaps a hundred people, growing what they could in small patches, keeping cattle and sheep in stone enclosures. No walls. No defenses. The lack of fortification was itself a statement: we submit to whoever controls this territory.

The headman examined me with the calculating attention of someone who had survived decades of variable overlordship. "Draëthen," he said. It was not a question. My proportions, my posture, the particular flatness of my affect—these marked my caste to anyone who knew what to look for.

"Yes."

"From where?"

"The eastern marches. The Mor-Kethran holdings."

His expression shifted slightly. "Long way to walk."

"Yes."

"Where are you going?"

"The Sound. The villages of my caste."

He considered this. Behind him, the village continued its morning work—women carrying water, men repairing a stone wall, children watching me with the frank curiosity of people who rarely saw strangers.

"The current overlord," the headman said carefully, "is Khan Tseren's band. They passed through four days ago. They'll return in perhaps two weeks. If you're still here when they return, they'll want to know who you are and what you're doing."

"I understand."

"You can work three days for food and water. Then you should leave."

I worked. The labor was agricultural—repairing irrigation channels, clearing stones from planting areas, tasks that required the patient repetition Draëthen were made for. The villagers watched me with the same calculating attention the headman had shown. They were not unfriendly. They were simply experienced with transients, with people passing through territory that belonged to no one and everyone, with the calculations that survival in the Malpais required.

On the third day, I left with provisions that would last another week and directions to the next settlement where I might find similar hospitality.

## **V.**

The journey took four months.

I do not have the words to describe what I saw. The Malpais was not one landscape but a thousand, each distinguished by the particular way volcanic rock had weathered and life had colonized what remained. I crossed lava flows frozen in patterns that suggested motion, walked through forests growing from fissures in solid basalt, skirted lakes whose waters steamed with geothermal heat. I saw the autofactory corridors extending north from Ogon—abandoned infrastructure built for purposes no one understood, maintained by systems no one controlled, occupied by people who had adapted the functionless architecture into something resembling homes.

I saw destrier herds running wild across the scablands, their clicking audible for miles before their shapes appeared on the horizon. I saw warbands pass in the distance, their banners indicating affiliations I could not read. I saw villages that welcomed transients and villages that drove them away and once, a village that had been destroyed so recently the fires were still burning.

I learned the rhythms. Which settlements would trade labor for food. Which territories were currently contested and should be avoided. Which routes the warbands favored and when they were likely to use them. The knowledge accumulated through experience and through the fragmentary information other travelers shared—the Malpais had its own networks of communication, slower than Imperial systems but adequate for survival.

The further north I traveled, the more the landscape changed. The volcanic rock gave way to other stone. The vegetation thickened. Rain fell more frequently, then constantly—a grey drizzle that lasted for days and soaked everything that was not already wet.

I crossed the Columbia at a point where traders maintained a ferry service, paying for passage with three days of labor loading and unloading cargo. The river here was not the Grand Canal—that geometric impossibility lay further downstream, where the autofactory had transformed watershed into something that followed no natural logic. Here the river was merely a river, broad and cold and indifferent to the humans who crossed it.

North of the Columbia, the Akama presence thinned. The territory was too marginal, too wet, too difficult for destrier cavalry to operate effectively. The villages here paid tribute to no one—or rather, paid tribute only to the land itself, which extracted its tax in the form of endless labor against climate and soil that resisted cultivation.

In the fourth month, I saw the Sound.

## **VI.**

Grey water stretched to a grey horizon under a grey sky. Islands emerged from the water like the backs of sleeping animals. Fog lay on the surface, obscuring distances, making the far shore seem further than it was or perhaps closer than it appeared.

I stood on a ridge above the water and felt something shift in my chest.

I had been alone for twenty-seven years. I had made a decision alone, traveled alone, survived alone through territory that should have killed me. The isolation had become so familiar that I had stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing a constant sound.

Now, looking at the grey water, I felt the absence sharply again. The villages were somewhere on this shore or the islands beyond—the villages where my caste had originated, where Draëthen lived in compounds and reached consensus through gradual alignment and experienced the satisfaction of collective labor that I had not felt since I was seven years old.

I did not know if they would accept me. I had been away too long, shaped by circumstances they had not shared, carrying experiences that might make alignment impossible. A Draëthen who had lived alone for twenty-seven years was not the same as a Draëthen who had grown within the compound's structure. I might be too different. I might be unable to feel what they felt, unable to align, unable to integrate.

But I had come this far. The decision had emerged and become action. What remained was to complete what I had started.

I descended toward the water.

## **VII.**

Ködhegy rose from the fog like a memory of something I had never seen but recognized anyway.

The village occupied a hilltop three hundred meters above the harbor—defensive terrain where destriers could not easily operate, where centuries of isolation had allowed the refugee castes to maintain themselves against the pressures that had scattered my people everywhere else. Stone buildings climbed the slopes. Fog catchment systems channeled moisture into carved cisterns. Banners in grey and white hung from the highest structures, barely visible through the mist.

I climbed the switchback road, passing through gates that were not guarded but were clearly defensible. The village watched me approach—I could feel the attention, the assessment, the calculation of whether I was threat or opportunity or simply another transient to be managed and sent on my way.

At the final gate, a woman waited. She was tall for a Draëthen—nearly my height—with the pale grey-white skin that marked our caste's engineering for heavy-metal tolerance. Her posture was familiar in ways I could not immediately articulate. She stood the way I had once stood, before years of isolation had taught me to occupy space as an individual rather than as part of a collective.

"You are Draëthen," she said. The language was mine—or had been mine, long ago, before the traders' tongue and the Mor-Kethran's Imperial and the pidgin of the Malpais had overlaid my childhood speech.

"Yes."

"From where?"

"From here, originally. The village council sold me south when I was seven years old. I have been in the eastern marches for twenty-seven years."

She considered this. Behind her, I could see other Draëthen gathering—not in a crowd, but in the way our caste gathered, gradually, positions shifting until a kind of formation emerged from individual movements. I felt the pull toward alignment, stronger than it had been with the scattered Draëthen of the marches, stronger than I had felt in decades.

"You came back," the woman said.

"Yes."

"That is unusual."

"Yes."

"Why?"

The question hung in the fog-thick air. I did not have an answer that would make sense to someone who had never been alone. I did not have an answer that made complete sense to myself. The decision had emerged from years of isolation, from the absence that had slowly become presence, from the wanting that had grown in the silence where consensus should have been.

"I wanted to," I said.

The woman nodded slowly. Behind her, the gathered Draëthen shifted again—not movement exactly, but something like movement, a collective adjustment that I could feel as much as see.

"Wanting is unusual for Draëthen," she said. "We decide. We align. We act. Wanting is what other castes do."

"I know."

"Perhaps," she said, "you have become unusual."

She stepped aside, leaving the gate open behind her. The gathered Draëthen watched me pass—their attention neither welcoming nor hostile, simply present, a collective awareness that I had not felt for twenty-seven years.

I entered the village. The fog closed behind me. Somewhere below, the grey water of the Sound met the grey sky in a line I could no longer see.

I had come home. Whether home would accept me remained to be determined.

But for the first time in twenty-seven years, the determination would be collective.

## **VIII.**

The compound occupied the village's eastern slope, a cluster of buildings whose architecture assumed Draëthen proportions—low ceilings, narrow doorways, spaces designed for bodies that crouched and compressed and moved through confined areas with ease.

Grey-Patch—that was what they called her, the woman who had met me at the gate, the name describing a distinguishing feature rather than an identity—led me through corridors worn smooth by centuries of use. Other Draëthen moved around us, engaged in tasks I recognized by rhythm if not by specific purpose. The maintenance of systems. The processing of materials. The endless labor that villages required and that Draëthen were made to provide.

"The council will determine," Grey-Patch said. "We do not decide quickly."

"I know."

"You may stay while we determine. You may work. Your labor will be evaluated."

"I understand."

"Do you?" She stopped, turning to face me in a corridor lit by windows too narrow to admit much light. "You have been away for twenty-seven years. You have lived among other castes, alone, without alignment. You have become something we do not have words for."

"I am still Draëthen."

"You are Draëthen who walked twelve hundred miles through territory that should have killed you, alone, because you wanted to. We do not want. We align. We determine. We act." Her eyes—pale, adapted for dim conditions—examined me with an intensity that I had not felt from another of my caste since childhood. "What you have done is not what Draëthen do."

"Then what am I?"

She did not answer immediately. Behind her, through the narrow windows, I could see the harbor far below—grey water under grey sky, ships at anchor, the fog that gave the village its name wrapping everything in moisture.

"That is what we will determine," she said finally. "It may take years. Decades. We do not decide quickly."

"I have time."

"You have whatever time the determination requires." She resumed walking, leading me deeper into the compound. "The Völërath will want to meet you. They remember when your contract was sold. Some of them approved the sale. Some opposed it. The disagreement has persisted for twenty-seven years. Your return will require them to resolve it."

I had not considered this. The Völërath—the dilute Highborn who governed the villages, who could not Compel but still commanded deference—operated on timescales I had forgotten. Their disagreements lasted centuries. Their resolutions required generations.

"What will they decide?"

"They will decide whether you were supposed to come back or whether you have disrupted something that should have remained settled." Grey-Patch's voice carried no judgment, only information. "The Völërath do not like disruption. They prefer things to remain as they were. But they also cannot deny that you are here, that you survived, that you have done something Draëthen do not do. This will require them to account for possibilities they had dismissed."

We reached a small chamber—a sleeping space, furnished with a platform and a basin and nothing else. Draëthen do not require much. We are made for efficiency, for function, for the completion of tasks rather than the accumulation of comforts.

"You will sleep here," Grey-Patch said. "Tomorrow you will work. Each day you will work. The council will observe. When we have determined, you will be told."

"How long?"

She looked at me with something that might have been curiosity or might have been concern or might have been simply the assessment of a problem that required solving.

"However long it takes," she said. "We do not decide quickly. But we decide completely. When the determination emerges, it will be final."

She left me in the small chamber, alone again but differently alone—alone within a compound where other Draëthen moved through adjacent spaces, where the collective awareness I had not felt for twenty-seven years pressed against the edges of my isolation like water against a dam.

I lay on the platform and listened to the sounds of the village—distant voices, the creak of structures adjusting to wind, somewhere the rhythmic clanging of metal being worked.

The fog pressed against the narrow windows. The Sound lay invisible below. The journey that had brought me here—twelve hundred miles through the Malpais, past warbands and through villages and across rivers—was complete.

What remained was the determination. Whether I could align again after twenty-seven years of isolation. Whether the wanting that had driven me here could transform into the collective purpose that Draëthen were made for. Whether home could accept what I had become or whether I would remain forever between what I had been and what the journey had made me.

I closed my eyes. For the first time since leaving the Mor-Kethran holdings, I slept without calculating the next day's route.

Whatever came next, I would not face it alone.
