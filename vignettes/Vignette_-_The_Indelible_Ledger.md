**The Indelible Ledger**

"The Empire demands its due tithe," Gavit announced to the assembled townspeople, who watched with the patient confusion of cattle. "As established by the Covenant of Subsidiary Provinces, ratified in the fourth year of the Seventh Oracle's Manifestation."

"The Empire," Gavit said carefully, "is the supreme authority over all human-inhabited space." The words sounded particularly hollow here, where the jungle pressed against buildings that were half archaeotech salvage, half bamboo, and entirely ramshackle.

"Space," the mayor repeated, tasting the word. "Right. Well, we don't have any of that. We've got copper, though. And these." He held up what appeared to be a large beetle. "Good eating. Cures joint-ache, too."

Behind Gavit, Targe made an interested noise. His joint-ache was visible from twenty meters.

Gavit consulted his ledger, a leather-bound tome that contained wildly optimistic revenue projections for the Steaming Sky Province based on surveys conducted before the Interdict, when this had been a thriving mineral extraction zone. Now it was seven villages, maybe eight, connected by roads that disappeared each monsoon season, populated by people who might be third-generation fugitives or might be indigenous or might be the descendants of a crashed orbital platform. The genetic markers were so scrambled even the Bureau of the Creche had given up trying to assign castes.

"Your annual assessment," Gavit declared, "is seventeen thousand obols, or equivalent value in extracted resources."

But tell you whatâ€”" He gestured to a building that might once have been a fusion plant, now converted into what looked like a brewery. "You stay for the feast tonight. We tell the story of the Empire. You tell us if we've got it right. Then tomorrow, we work out what we can actually pay."

Gavit wanted to refuse. He had six more villages to visit, each one probably more remote and less profitable than this one. But Yent was already lowering herself onto a bench, her breathing labored, and Kessik was eyeing the beetle with genuine interest.

"The feast," the mayor continued, "is for the Sky Burning Festival. Celebrates when the sky caught fire and the ancestors fell down. Good eating. Dancing. Might even find something worth taking back to your Empire."

The sky catching fire. The Interdict, probably, or maybe just a particularly impressive thunderstorm. These people had mythologized everything and understood nothing.

"One night," Gavit conceded.

The feast was, against all expectation, elaborate. The entire village contributed dishes made from things Gavit couldn't identify. The beetle, roasted with local peppers, was genuinely delicious. Targe ate seventeen of them and claimed his spine felt better, though that might have been the cloudy alcohol they kept serving in hollowed gourds.

Then came the story.

The village's storyteller was ancient, her skin marked with scars in patterns that might have been ritual or might have been radiation exposure. She told of the time before, when the ancestors lived in the sky and traveled between stars in great ships. She told of the Burning Day, when the ancestors fell to earth in fire. She told of the Empire Beyond the Mountains, which sent tax collectors every few decades to collect things the village didn't have, in exchange for protection they didn't need, from threats that didn't exist.

"But we pay anyway," she concluded, "because the tax collectors bring news. And medicine, sometimes. And because the big onesâ€”" she gestured at the Warborn, "â€”they fix things. The old machines. They know the old words."

Gavit looked at his enforcers. Kessik had disassembled someone's water purifier and was rebuilding it while drunk, his massive fingers moving with forgotten precision. Yent was teaching children a marching song that was older than the Empire's presence on this continent. Targe was arm-wrestling local men, deliberately losing to each one, laughing at their delight.

"So," the storyteller asked Gavit, "did we get it right? The Empire story?"

Gavit thought about the truthâ€”the Bureaus, the castes, the Aureate families extracting wealth from provinces that barely existed, the Interdict that had trapped humanity on this single world while the infrastructure of interstellar civilization slowly collapsed into myth and salvage.

"Close enough," he said.

In the morning, bleary from drink and exhausted from dancing he hadn't meant to participate in, Gavit accepted the village's payment: three hundred obols in mixed ancient coins, two tons of copper ore, a cart full of the medicinal beetles, and a promise to pay more next year if the rains were good.

His ledger demanded seventeen thousand. The Bureau would expect at least ten. He'd report three thousand collected, keep enough to maintain his Warborn and his transport, and hope the Bureau didn't audit too closely. They never did. The Steaming Sky Province was a rounding error in Imperial calculations, a territory that existed more in ledgers than reality.

Next year, if his Warborn survived that long, he'd return. The village would pay what it could. He'd record what he needed to. The Empire would continue believing it ruled territories that barely knew it existed.

The system worked, after a fashion. Even if no one could explain exactly why.
