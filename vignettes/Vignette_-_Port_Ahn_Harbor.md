**Port Ahn, Harbor**

Port Ahn rose from the sea like a broken tooth—limestone cliffs sheering up three hundred feet from the bay, topped with dark shale that crumbled at the edges. The citadel perched on the promontory's highest point, its gauss cannon emplacements tracking the Flame of the Revolution as she entered the harbor. Each barrel could punch through a ship's hull at two kilometers. They stayed centered on the waterline as the Ganati crew dropped anchor.

The town itself clung to the cliff face in terraces carved from living rock. Wooden structures sprouted from limestone shelves, connected by stairs cut so steep they were nearly ladders. Everything faced the harbor—the only approach that mattered. Behind the town, scrubland stretched south toward ya-Tsatsa, north toward nowhere in particular.

"Imperial checkpoint," Ghom announced unnecessarily. They could all see the banners—the Empire's seven-pointed star hanging limp in the still morning air.

The harbormaster's launch reached them first. A bored-looking clerk in Scale Bureau grey checked their charter, noted the Ganati registration, charged them forty obols for "harbor maintenance" that clearly hadn't happened in years. Then he mentioned, almost as afterthought, that all passengers would need clearance from the revenue house.

The dock smelled of rotting kelp and tar. Ganati crew unloaded cargo under the watch of armed stevedores who made it clear that nothing moved without proper documentation. The guards formed up around Vey, Ghom taking point, the others arranging themselves to look casual while maintaining sight lines. Professional habits.

The revenue house squatted halfway up the cliff—a cube of imported stone that didn't match the local limestone, probably hauled from the Imperial core at absurd expense to make a point about permanent authority. Bureau of Coin's brass scales were worked into the doors, tarnished green from sea air.

Inside, everything was forms. Walls of pigeonholes held different documents for different purposes, color-coded by incomprehensible logic. Three queues led to three clerks, each handling specific categories of business. A sign in Imperial proclaimed "Order is the Foundation of Revenue." Someone had carved "Revenue is Theft" in Akama underneath, and someone else had tried unsuccessfully to sand it off.

They waited an hour. The clerk who finally called them forward was young, perhaps twenty-five, with the soft look of someone who'd never missed a meal or faced violence. His uniform was crisp—freshly issued, not yet worn into comfort.

"Purpose of visit?"

"Transit to ya-Sattra," Ghom answered. "Engineering consultation."

The clerk's eyes catalogued them—five obvious Akama warriors, one small grey figure. He'd reached for the standard transit documentation, then stopped, staring at Vey.

"What caste is that?"

"Kma-Dhol," Vey said.

The clerk's expression went blank. He clearly had no idea what that meant. "Wait here."

He disappeared into a back room. They heard voices, pages turning, someone saying "Look in the appendix, the supplemental classifications."

Ten minutes passed. Tselkor started humming. Buragh cleaned his nails with a knife until another clerk glared at him.

The young clerk returned with an older supervisor and a massive leather-bound registry. They set it on the counter, flipping through pages that looked like they'd been updated by different hands across decades. Finally, the supervisor's finger stopped on an entry.

"Here. Kma-Dhol. Classification: Mutterer-derivative, subcategory hybrid, limited sentience presumed." The supervisor looked up. "You'll need special documentation."

Vey felt heat rise in his throat. Mutterer-derivative. As if the Kma-Dhol were some degraded offshoot of neurologically crippled Technical Caste rather than—what? He didn't actually know what they were, but they weren't that.

"The fee is higher," the supervisor continued, pulling out a different form. "Eighty obols for semi-sentient transit, plus twenty for the handler registration."

"Handler?" Ghom's voice carried an edge.

"Someone has to be legally responsible for semi-sentient cargo." The supervisor gestured at the form. "Standard procedure for Mutterers, kalbats, trained animals."

The guards had gone very still. Vey touched Ghom's arm slightly—let it go. This was useful. Insulting, but useful. If the Empire classified him as semi-sentient, he avoided Creche attention. Technical Caste derivatives would be the Bureau of the Creche's business, but Mutterer-hybrids were just another category of property.

"I'll register as his handler," Ghom said flatly.

The forms took another twenty minutes. Description of the semi-sentient (height: 1.4 meters, coloration: grey, distinguishing features: enlarged cranium). Purpose of transit (technical assessment—the clerk had to look up whether semi-sentients could perform technical work, discovered they could if supervised). Duration of custody. Liability waivers.

Finally stamped, sealed, and officially documented as cargo, Vey followed the others out.

The market spread across the largest limestone terrace, a hundred stalls under canvas and salvaged polymer. This was Port Ahn's real purpose—the last place northbound traders could buy Imperial goods, the first place southbound Akama could sell frontier products.

The mix was jarring. Imperial merchants in geometric-cut robes sold ammunition beside Akama women in orange and rust-colored wool. Technical manuals from ya-Sattra stacked next to destrier harness. A Coin Bureau assessor weighed gold using scales that probably predated the Interdict while her bodyguard, obviously Akama by his build, watched the crowd for threats.

"Three days until the next ship south," Ghom noted, checking the posted schedules. "We need supplies."

They split up. Vey wandered the technical quarter, examining components that might have been manufactured last week or salvaged from orbit. A merchant tried to sell him "genuine Technical Caste tools"—obvious fakes, wrong metallurgy, but someone would buy them believing they held special properties.

At a bookstall, he found something unexpected: a Jargon text. Not a complete document, just fragments bound with other technical papers, but real. The mathematical proofs hurt to look at, concepts that folded back on themselves. He bought it for twelve obols, knowing he'd never understand it but unable to leave it behind.

"Engineer\!" Tselkor's voice carried across the market. "Come see\!"

The young guard stood before a weapon merchant's display, pointing at something that shouldn't exist—a plasma lance, civilian model, probably three hundred years old but still sealed in its original case.

"How much?" Vey asked the merchant, a scarred woman with Colonel-Hereditary bearing.

"Not for sale. Display only." She studied him. "You know what it is?"

"Neural-locked plasma accelerator. Pre-Interdict manufacture. Probably keyed to someone who's been dead for centuries."

She smiled slightly. "My great-great-grandfather. Still in the family, but the authentication died with him. Pretty paperweight now."

This was Port Ahn—the last edge of Empire where broken weapons were displayed as heritage, where Kma-Dhol were classified as property, where Imperial order met frontier chaos and both pretended the arrangement made sense.

Three days here. Then further south, further into the Empire's documentary fiction, carrying papers declaring him semi-sentient cargo under Ghom's supervision. It was absurd and practical, insulting and protective.

The market's noise washed over him—haggling in three languages, hammering from a smith's booth, someone playing a stringed instrument badly. At the edge of the terrace, where the limestone dropped to the harbor, he could see the Flame of the Revolution at anchor, waiting to carry them deeper into Imperial territory.

Behind them, north, the Thousand Kingdoms continued without documentation or classification. Ahead, south, everything would require forms, stamps, proper categorization.

He was cargo now, officially. It would make everything easier.
