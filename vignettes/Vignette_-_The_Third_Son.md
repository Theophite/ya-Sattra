*The Kel-Tseren claim the eastern valley by right of their fathers,* Ataru wrote. *But their fathers took it from ours in the Record of Bitter Wind, and our fathers took it back in the year of the red comet, and they have no right that we do not have also. What does the law say?*

There was no law. That was what Ataru did not understand, or understood and refused to accept. The Republic's writ did not run in that part of the Malpais. The Thousand Kingdoms had no law, only custom and violence. The grazing dispute would be settled the way grazing disputes had always been settled: by negotiation if the bands were evenly matched, by blood if they were not.

Khas-Sekh would write back with something gentle and evasive. Perhaps a verse about the foolishness of fighting over grass when the herds were fat and the winter was mild. Khan Softhands was not an arbiter; he was an entertainer, a gossip, a voice that came from nowhere and spoke to everyone. Amatu would be disappointed. He would also, probably, resolve the dispute on his own, as his fathers had, as his sons would.

The thought was not entirely comforting.

Crude. Effective. Khan Softhands did not trade in subtlety.

The next verse was about Dhal-Orom, and here Khas-Sekh allowed himself a small indulgence. He had met Valdis Dhal-Orom once, years ago, at a gathering of old families that had somehow survived the Revolution's first decade. Dhal-Orom had been drunk, maudlin, full of talk about the marches and the old ways and the eastern trade that was going to restore his family's fortunes. Khas-Sekh had found him pathetic and faintly admirable, a man playing at being something his ancestors had actually been.

The verse he wrote was not kind. It touched on the cousin-bride, the debt-bondage allegations, the suspicious provenance of goods flowing through Dhal-Orom's territory. Khan Softhands had no loyalty to marcher lords, whatever Khas-Sekh might feel in private. The readers wanted blood, and blood they would get.

But he softened it, just slightly. A line about Dhal-Orom standing alone against the Republic's inspectors. A suggestion that whatever his flaws, the man was holding something that mattered. The Akama would catch the undertone. They always did.

He finished the draft, set it aside for revision, and sat for a while watching the evening light fade over Taho.

His brothers had died eighteen years ago. Kashet at the Far Wall of Ganat, leading a sortie that achieved nothing. Yem in the reprisals after, dragged from hiding and shot in the street while their father watched. Their father had died a week later, of something the physicians called heart failure and Khas-Sekh understood to be simpler than that.

He had been in Taho when the news came. Teaching a seminar on Akama phonology to students who would soon be fighting or dead. He had finished the seminar, dismissed the students, walked back to his apartment, and sat in the dark until morning.

The Republic had killed his family. Not personallyâ€”no one had signed an order with his brothers' namesâ€”but systemically, inevitably, the way a river kills a village built too close to its banks. He did not hate the Republic for this. Hate required energy he had spent long ago. He simply did not believe in it: in its institutions, its rhetoric, its careful negotiations between factions that would tear each other apart if the balance ever shifted.

The Akama believed in things. That was what drew him to them, what had drawn him since he first read a Malpais war-song in translation as a boy of twelve. They believed in honor, in lineage, in the duty of the strong to rule and the weak to serve. They believed that a man's worth was measured in what he would die for. They were brutal and capricious and magnificent, and when he sat in his apartment surrounded by their artifacts, he felt closer to something real than he ever felt in faculty meetings or Senate hearings or consultations with the Ministry of War.

But the knife had outlasted the smith, the forge, the city, the civilization that produced it. Something had worked.

A knock at the door. He set the knife down and rose to answer it.

The courier was young, near-baseline, wearing the discreet insignia of the Ministry of Internal Affairs. "Professor Akkhali? Priority assessment request."

Khas-Sekh took the sealed envelope, signed the receipt, and closed the door. The courier's footsteps receded down the stairs.

Inside the envelope: a summary of intercepted correspondence, a preliminary analysis by some junior officer, a request for expert evaluation. The subject was a voice called Khan Softhands, whose newspaper was circulating among Akama populations of uncertain loyalty. The junior officer had flagged several passages as potentially seditious. He sat down at his writing desk, moved a pottery fragment aside, and began his assessment. He did not, of course, deny being Khan Softhands. Others sometimes wrote under that name; he could find this deniable. But this one he wrote.

Khas-Sekh poured himself a glass of the imported wine he could not quite afford, raised it to no one in particular, and drank.
