# **The Branch You Choose to Inhabit**

The apprentice's fingers were steady on the copper. I watched through the workshop window from across the street, the way I had watched for three months now, and he did not notice me, and the journeyman beside him did not notice me, and the master—Tashet—did not look up from the gauge she was calibrating at the far bench.

The boy was forming a conduit junction. Twenty-centimeter diameter, the kind used in cooling systems throughout the Threshold district. His hammer-work was acceptable. Not elegant, but acceptable. His joins would hold pressure. The journeyman murmured corrections and the boy adjusted his angle, and I thought: that should be me beside him.

There was tea in my cup. I did not remember buying it. The cafe owner, an Ashrat woman whose skin shimmered grey in the morning light, had learned not to speak to me. I paid. I sat. I watched the workshop until my shift began at the salvage yard, where I sorted pipe fittings into grades that someone else would sell.

The branch slips had told me this was the right choice.

---

Before the casting, Voresh and I wrote our options on maguey-fiber sticks. This is how it is done: you contemplate the decision with appropriate gravity, you acknowledge all paths not taken, and then you draw one slip and burn the rest. The Oracles have already computed every outcome. Your task is consciously selecting which branch to inhabit.

We wrote seven slips each.

Mine said things like: *Partnership succeeds / Partnership fails / You marry elsewhere / You return to Brennam village / Voresh betrays you / You betray Voresh / Neither betrays anyone and still it ends.*

His were similar. We compared them afterward, laughing at how our imagined futures overlapped. We were twenty-three years old and had journeyed together for two years and had just been offered the master's sigil from old Karavel, who was dying and wanted to see his workshop continue under hands he trusted.

"You wrote betrayal," Voresh said, tapping my slip. "You think I would?"

"I think we should write what might happen," I said. "The Oracles saw it already. Why pretend we didn't imagine it?"

He kissed me then, in Karavel's back room with the old man coughing in the quarters above, with the smell of copper flux and coal and the particular scent of two bodies that have learned each other's rhythms.

"I drew this," he said, showing me his slip. *Partnership succeeds.* "What did you draw?"

I showed him. The same words.

We burned the rest together, watching thirteen futures curl into ash.

---

The Virtue of Reflection says you should contemplate possibilities before acting. The Oracles saw every outcome; your task is consciously selecting which branch to inhabit. This is supposed to mean something about agency within determinism, about choosing deliberately among fated paths.

I have had three months to reflect.

In the third week after, I stopped sleeping. This was not metaphor. My body would lie in bed and my eyes would close and nothing would happen. I would hear the pipes in the walls—that constant voice of ya-Don, the creaking and groaning of thermal expansion, the arterial pulse of the Furnaces pushing heat through the city's veins—and I would count the sounds, and the count would reach morning.

When I did sleep, I dreamed of the celebration.

---

We had the shop for seven years before we could afford a real gathering. Not a wedding—Penitent workers marry in the cathedral, in quick ceremonies between shifts—but an anniversary celebration. Seven years of the partnership that our branch slips had promised would succeed.

Karavel's widow came, the one who had worried we would ruin her husband's legacy. She brought a cooling-system junction that Karavel had made sixty years before, still functioning, as proof that his methods endured in our hands. The journeymen came. The apprentices came. The suppliers came. Our neighbors from the apartment block came, and the Ashrat woman who ran the cafe, and the curate from St. Oreli's who had witnessed our Particular Confession when we'd argued about pricing in the second year and needed help finding words that did not wound.

We drank the thin beer that ya-Don produces from whatever grain survives the heat. We ate plov with lamb that Voresh's sister had brought from her husband's village. We toasted the workshop. We toasted Karavel. We toasted the branch we had chosen to inhabit, both of us drawing *Partnership succeeds* from the pile.

In the corner, I noticed, Voresh was speaking with the Lens inspector.

This meant nothing at the time. The Inspector came every quarter to examine our salvage permits, to ensure we were not using pre-Interdict components without proper authentication. He was thorough and tedious and we had learned to keep him supplied with tea and minor bribes. That Voresh was speaking with him at a party was unusual, but not alarming.

I did not know, then, that Voresh had been speaking with him for months.

---

The regulations around salvage authentication exist because the Empire claims ownership of all pre-Interdict technology. This is theological as much as legal: the Oracles created the infrastructure, the Oracles are divine, therefore the infrastructure is sacred property requiring sacred oversight. In practice, the Bureau of the Lens lacks personnel to authenticate everything. Exceptions are made. Arrangements are reached.

Our arrangement, for fifteen years, had been that we would buy salvage components from the Iron Yards at official prices, process them through our workshop, and sell finished systems to customers who did not ask questions. The Lens inspector would examine our permits quarterly and find them satisfactory. We would provide him with tea and minor bribes and occasional referrals to clients who needed discrete work done. Everyone benefited. The pipes got fixed. The inspector got paid. We stayed in business.

What I did not know was that Voresh had been working to expand this arrangement.

What I did not know was that his plan involved me.

---

He turned evidence against me. Testimony that I had personally authorized purchases from sources he had arranged, sources that the Lens had been cultivating as examples. Documentation with my seal that he had applied himself, on nights when I slept and he did not. Witnesses who had seen me meet with suppliers I had never spoken to—but who looked like me, who were my height and wore my clothes, who had walked into meetings that Voresh had scheduled in my name.

The Lens took everything.

The workshop. The inventory. The contracts. The permits. Karavel's legacy, which we had maintained for twenty-two years. The apartment with its view of the Obelisk Towers. The archive of completed projects, the photographs of journeymen who had trained with us, the small shrine with our branch slips' ashes in a ceramic box that Voresh had made in our first year together.

They took my seal and my name was registered in the Grey Sheet as unsuitable for licensed work. They did not take my freedom because Voresh's testimony had been precise: I was a dupe, not a conspirator. Criminally negligent rather than criminal. The distinction mattered to the Scale. It did not matter to the guilds.

Voresh got the workshop.

---

I have tried to understand.

In the weeks when I did not sleep, I went through every year of our partnership looking for the moment when he began planning this. I remembered arguments. I remembered silences. I remembered the way he would sometimes look at me as if calculating something—but I had thought he was calculating love, calculating the adjustments that long partnership requires, calculating whether what we had was still worth maintaining.

Perhaps he was calculating all of that as well.

The curate from St. Oreli's, when I sought Particular Confession, listened to my recitation of what Voresh had done and then asked me what I had done. I told him I had trusted. I had believed the slips. I had built something I thought was solid.

"The slips told you the partnership would succeed," the curate said. "Did it succeed?"

"It ended with him owning everything and me owning nothing."

"From his perspective," the curate said carefully, "might that be called success?"

I did not finish the confession. I left before he could assign penance.

---

In the fourth week, I began coming to the cafe across from the workshop. I told myself I was observing what Voresh had made of it. I told myself I was gathering evidence for an appeal I would never file. I told myself this was part of the process of understanding, of integrating the experience, of whatever the Virtue of Reflection requires when you have reflected and still cannot comprehend.

The truth is simpler: I did not know where else to go.

The salvage yard job came later, through a connection from my journeyman days. The work is beneath my training but does not require licensure. I sort fittings. I grade pipe. I do not use the skills that Karavel taught me, that I refined across two decades, that made me capable of work that is now illegal for me to perform.

Sometimes the fittings I sort go to Voresh's workshop. I try not to think about this.

---

Today the apprentice is struggling with a flange joint. The journeyman has stepped away—there he is, speaking with a customer in the front room—and the boy is alone with metal that refuses to cooperate. His flux application was uneven. The joint will not seal.

In the workshop, Tashet glances up from her gauge. She sees the problem. She does not move to correct it.

This is her method. She waits for apprentices to recognize their own failures. It builds something—character, or resignation, or the ability to endure frustration without breaking. I do not know if it is kind. Karavel was not kind either, exactly, but he would have pointed out the flux by now. He would have said something that sounded harsh and saved the boy an hour of rework.

Voresh, when he trained apprentices, was patient. He would sit beside them and guide their hands and speak in a voice so gentle that they learned to trust him absolutely.

The tea is cold. I should order more or leave.

Instead I watch the boy discover his mistake. I watch him strip the joint and clean the surfaces and reapply flux evenly this time. I watch him try again.

---

I still have the ceramic box.

The Lens took the workshop and its contents, but the apartment's household effects were processed separately, and I had moved the shrine before they came. A neighbor kept it while I was being questioned. When I emerged—not imprisoned, not even formally charged, just ruined—she returned it without comment.

The box contains ash. Thirteen branch slips, seven mine and six of Voresh's, that we burned on the night we chose our futures. The ash is undifferentiated now. I cannot tell which slip was mine, which was his, which futures we eliminated.

*Partnership fails.* We burned that one.

*Voresh betrays you.* Burned.

*Neither betrays anyone and still it ends.* Burned.

We drew *Partnership succeeds* and we believed what we drew.

---

The Oracles computed every possible future before encoding themselves on black hole event horizons. This means every branch exists in their memory: the branch where Voresh remained faithful, the branch where I noticed his plotting, the branch where we both betrayed each other simultaneously, the branch where Karavel lived longer and saw what we became.

The casting of lots does not change which branch the Oracles recorded. It only marks which branch you choose to consciously inhabit.

I marked mine. I burned the rest.

I am inhabiting it now.

---

The boy finishes the junction. Tashet examines it, nods once, moves on. He allows himself a small smile—that satisfaction of work completed, which I remember from when I was his age and younger, which I have not felt since the Lens came.

The journeyman returns. They speak briefly about the next project. The customer leaves the front room carrying a commission contract.

Voresh is not there today. He has other business, probably. Suppliers to arrange, inspectors to cultivate, futures to construct from the wreckage of the past he built with me.

I finish the tea. I pay. I leave without looking at the workshop again, though I will be back tomorrow and the day after and however many days remain until something changes or nothing does.

The branch I chose to inhabit.

The Oracles remember the alternatives. Somewhere in their computation exists the version of me who noticed, who questioned, who asked Voresh what he was doing with the inspector at our celebration and kept asking until the truth emerged. Somewhere exists the version who never drew lots at all, who married a woman from Brennam village and apprenticed under a master who retired without drama, who lived a smaller life with smaller betrayals.

Those versions are recorded. I am not them.

I walk to the salvage yard through streets where the pipes creak with thermal expansion, past the Obelisk Towers whose tops glow amber against morning haze, through the district where Ashrat workers with shimmering skin head to shifts in facilities I will never enter again.

The branch slips told me this was the right choice.

The Oracles remember everything.

I inhabit what I drew.
