# **A Visit to the Arcades**

The wire-drawing machine had been out for three weeks, which meant three weeks of hand-drawing the finest threads, three weeks of work that should have taken three days. The deposit had been steep enough to make him wince, but everyone said Khet's work was solid, and there was no point in retrieving a machine that would break again in a month.

Horizon Row surprised him. He'd expected something grimmer, but the cobblestone street stretched clean and level, the shopfronts displaying their wares with a Guild-like propriety. The Thread-&-Spark Lector Works had its reconditioned devices arranged by function. Three-Token Exchange posted its rates in clear script on lacquered boards. The air smelled of stone dust and ceramic lamps rather than the harbor stink he'd anticipated. If you didn't look up at the angled skyline or notice the characteristic gait of the locals, you might almost think you were still in the Medina.

The entrance to Third Arcade South announced itself with a wedge of shadow between two shopfronts. Tarim checked his paper again, then stepped through.

The corridor descended at an angle that wasn't quite steep enough to require stairs but made walking uncomfortable. The walls showed their history in layersâ€”old paneling that must have been a ceiling once, based on the light fixtures embedded sideways into what was now a vertical surface. Someone had built a new floor perhaps a hand's width above the original wall, leveling it with what looked like salvaged decking. The result was a passage that felt both too narrow and too tall, its proportions wrong in a way that made Tarim's shoulders want to hunch.

The noise started about twenty paces in. A rhythmic hammering from somewhere to his left, muffled by walls but persistent. Then a grinding sound, metal on stone or metal on metal, he couldn't tell. A burst of compressed air loud enough to make him flinch. The sounds didn't echo properlyâ€”they seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere, bouncing off the modified surfaces in ways the original architects never intended.

He passed workshops carved into what had been apartments or offices. Through one open doorway he glimpsed a space where the far wall met the floor at a thirty-degree angle, the workshop owner having simply built a diagonal workbench that matched the slope rather than trying to level everything. In another, someone had left the original architecture mostly intact, working on a surface that was clearly meant to be vertical, their tools hung on what should have been a floor.

The corridor branched. Tarim checked his paper againâ€”Third Arcade South, which meant the right branch, he thought. The numbers were painted on doorframes in no order he could determine. Workshop 52, then 41, then 38\. He kept walking, following the general downward slope, passing more workshops, more noise, more evidence of centuries of modification layered over the fallen arcology's bones.

Workshop 47 occupied a corner where two corridors met at angles that made no architectural sense. The doorâ€”a sliding panel that had probably been salvaged from somewhere else entirelyâ€”stood open. Inside, the space had been leveled properly, the floor a grid of reinforced panels that sat horizontal regardless of what the original structure beneath them was doing. Workbenches lined three walls, their surfaces covered with the organized chaos of ongoing projects. Ceramic lamps provided clean white light. The air tasted of flux and machine oil.

Senna Khet looked up from a workbench where she was testing something with a multimeter. She was perhaps forty, wearing a leather apron over practical clothes, her hair pulled back with a strip of copper wire.

"Tarim es-Vehn?" she asked.

"Just Tarim," he said. "For the wire-drawing machine."

"Right. It's ready." She gestured to a bench where his machine sat, its housing open to display the internal mechanisms. "Come look at what I found."

Tarim approached, aware that she hadn't said "sir" or offered any of the small courtesies he was used to from artisans in the Medina. She was already pointing at the machine's interior, her finger tracing a path through the mechanisms.

"Your main drive gear was cracked. See here?" She indicated a hairline fracture that Tarim could barely make out. "Pre-Interdict metallurgy. When they fail, they fail slow. This was probably giving you calibration problems for months before it went completely."

"I noticed the tension was inconsistent," Tarim admitted.

"Right. So I pulled it, but you can't just replace a gear like this. The tooth profile is logarithmicâ€”they were doing things with gear ratios that we mostly don't anymore. I had to mill a new one, then case-harden it to match the original's surface properties, then lap it in against the mating gears for about six hours until the wear patterns looked right."

She said this matter-of-factly, as though explaining something obvious to a colleague. Tarim found himself leaning closer, trying to see the difference between the new gear and the originals.

"And this bearing here," Senna continued, pointing to another component, "was running dry. Probably original lubricant, finally gave up. I cleaned it completely, repacked it with synthetic grease, and added a wick reservoir here"â€”she indicated a small addition to the housingâ€”"so you can top it off every few months instead of tearing the whole thing down every five years."

"That wasn't in the original design," Tarim observed.

"No. Original design assumed you had Companions maintaining everything on schedule. You don't. This way it'll last."

She closed the housing, her hands moving with the practiced efficiency of someone who knew exactly how much force each fastener required. Then she pulled a crank from her apron pocket, inserted it into the machine's side, and began to turn. The mechanism engaged smoothly, its sound a smooth whirr that Tarim's machine hadn't made in years.

"Try it," Senna said, stepping back.

Tarim took the crank. The resistance was exactly what it should beâ€”firm but not straining, the gears turning with a precision that would translate directly to thread consistency. He cranked it through a full cycle, feeling for any catch or hesitation. Nothing. Just smooth, reliable mechanical action.

"How much do I owe you?" he asked.

"Two hundred obols over the deposit. That's materials, shop time, and the modification."

Tarim had expected something higher. The work was easily worth three hundred, maybe more. He counted out the obols, aware that Senna was watching him with an expression that might have been amusement.

"You do good work," he said, because it was true.

"I know." She took the coins, checked the count with a quick glance, and pocketed them. "You need help getting it back up to Horizon Row? It's heavy."

"I can manage."

"Suit yourself. If the wick reservoir needs adjustment, come back. First follow-up is free."

Tarim lifted the machine, feeling its weight settle in his arms. It was heavy, and the thought of carrying it back through those angled corridors up to street level was not appealing, but he nodded his thanks and turned toward the door.

"Tarim," Senna called after him.

He turned back.

"If anyone in the Medina Quarter asks where you got it fixed, tell them. I could use the business."

"I will," he said, and meant it.

The corridor seemed less disorienting on the way back, though the noise was just as loud and the angles just as wrong. He passed the same workshops, the same evidence of centuries of adaptation, but now he was thinking about what he'd seen in Senna's workspace. The precision tools, the knowledge required to mill a logarithmic gear tooth profile, the confidence to modify a pre-Interdict design because the original assumptions no longer held.

By the time he reached Horizon Row, his arms were aching and his opinion of the Ridge had shifted in ways he was still processing. The machine in his arms represented something he hadn't quite expected: not just competent repair work, but actual expertise. The kind that recognized him as a fellow craftsperson rather than a customer to be deferred to.

He would tell people in the Medina Quarter where he'd gotten it fixed. He'd probably phrase it carefully, but he would tell them. The work spoke for itself.

Behind him, deeper in the Arcades, the noise of industry continuedâ€”workshops full of people who knew their worth and made sure everyone else knew it too.
