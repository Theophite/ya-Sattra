**The Feast, Malpais**

The warband came back at sunset, their destriers making no sound across the basalt until they were practically on top of the village. Mirya looked up from the dumplings she was shaping and saw them silhouetted against the red sky \- maybe thirty riders, the destriers' impossible legs folding and extending like mantis limbs as they slowed from their running gait to something almost reasonable.

The lead rider was Tsokh himself, recognizable even at this distance by the way he sat his mount. He raised one long arm \- the Akama all had those elongated arms, somewhere between human and something else \- and the warband peeled off toward the gathering ground.

"They won," Mirya's grandmother said, not looking up from the kavdak she was rehydrating. "They're back too soon to have lost."

The village had been preparing anyway. When the warband left three weeks ago to meet Kelam's people at the border, everyone knew there'd be a feast when they returned. Winner or loser, there'd be a feast. If they'd lost, it would be Kelam's warband eating with them tonight instead of Tsokh's.

By the time full dark came, the fires were going and the food was ready. The warband had contributed two deer and a destrier calf that had broken its leg \- the meat was already spitted and roasting. The village provided everything else: dumplings by the hundred, plov cooked in enormous pots, sour cream in clay bowls, pickled vegetables, flatbreads, koumis in leather bags.

Tsokh dismounted with that characteristic Akama grace, hit the ground already moving. He was shorter than a Warborn but built stranger, all length and leverage. His face had that simian cast they all shared \- broad, expressive, the bones slightly wrong. He walked straight to Mirya's grandmother.

"Mirkit," he said. "We held the ford."

"Then you're still collecting tribute," her grandmother said.

"We are."

"The usual amount?"

"The usual amount."

Mirya's grandmother nodded and went back to her kavdak. The conversation was over. That was the entire political transition.

Old Gan was already sitting by the fire \- not the young Gan who rode with Tsokh, but the other Gan, seventy years old, who'd ridden with Orlam's band before his knees gave out. He'd settled in the village fifteen years ago, choosing acceptance over dying on a destrier. Slightly disgraceful, everyone understood, but reasonable. He lived in a small house near the edge of the village and made arrows, which he sold to whoever held the territory.

Young Gan \- his grand-niece \- dropped down next to him as the feast started. "Uncle."

"You held the ford."

"We did. Kelam's people tried the crossing at dawn. We were waiting."

"Good." Old Gan took a dumpling, dipped it in kavdak, then sour cream. "Your form is still sloppy."

"My form won the ford."

"Winning proves strength. It doesn't prove technique."

Young Gan grinned, showing teeth that were slightly too large, and took a bowl of plov.

Mirya filled her own bowl \- barley cooked with shredded smoked beef, grated carrots, dill seed, cumin \- and took three dumplings. She dipped into the kavdak her grandmother had made, then into the sour cream with fresh dill.

The kavdak was perfect. Smoky and rich, tart from blackberries, the marjoram adding complexity underneath. The dumplings were hot and substantial \- pounded beef, carrot, spices. The sour cream cut the richness.

The warband was relaxing now, the tension of three weeks on the road visibly draining away. This was their village. Their people. They could let down their guard here in a way they couldn't at the ford or out on the scablands. Tsokh was talking with the headman, not standing over him. Young Gan was joking with her uncle, not watching her back. The destriers were grazing, their impossible legs folded at rest.

Across the fire, Tsokh was confirming the tribute schedule with the headman. Not arguing \- just confirming. Three destriers per year, twenty measures of grain, whatever forage the village could spare.

The headman nodded. "The usual amount."

"The usual amount," Tsokh agreed.

Everyone understood what wasn't being said. If Tsokh tried to take more \- if he came back next month demanding extra grain, or four destriers instead of three \- the headman could tell him directly that Kelam's people would be welcome the following week. No conspiracy, no secret plot. Just: you've proven weak by over-reaching, we're choosing another master. And Tsokh would have to accept that as legitimate.

But Tsokh took the usual amount. So the headman confirmed their arrangement. And everyone ate.

Old Gan was telling a story now about a raid that had gone wrong thirty years ago, when he'd ridden with Shet's band. Young Gan was listening, correcting details. Mirya's grandmother refilled the kavdak bowl. One of the villagers started singing \- one of the old Akama songs about a destrier that refused to be broken. The warband joined in on the chorus, their voices rougher but enthusiastic.

Mirya had been fourteen when the last transition happened. Tsokh's warband had fought Orlam's people in the hills north of the village, and Tsokh had won. Orlam's survivors had ridden off to try their fortune elsewhere. Old Gan had stayed \- he'd already settled by then, already chosen acceptance. The village had served Tsokh a feast that night too, and her grandmother had made kavdak, and everything had continued.

Before Orlam it had been Shet. Before Shet it had been someone whose name Mirya couldn't remember. It didn't matter. The village remained. The basalt remained. The thin soil and the harsh winters and the destriers running across the scablands at seventy miles per hour \- all of it remained.

This was brutal. Tsokh could die tomorrow and they'd serve the next warband the same feast. The tribute was heavy. The violence was real \- young Gan had a healing wound across her shoulder, visible when she reached for more plov. Three riders hadn't come back from the ford at all.

But there was no deception. When Tsokh said the tribute amount, he meant that amount. When the headman confirmed it, he was also confirming he wouldn't betray \- and both men knew that confirmation could be withdrawn if the terms changed. When you lost, you lost, and everyone understood what that meant. When you won, you won, and the victory tasted like smoked meat and kavdak and koumis shared around a fire.

In the Empire, Mirya would be \- what? A clerk somewhere, maybe, if she passed the examinations. Eating light grain bowls alone in a Fourth Whorl apartment with no kitchen, signaling refinement she didn't feel, navigating bureaucratic rules designed to delay and obscure. Or more likely she'd be in the Middens, eating whatever the Hanged Men's extortion left her able to afford, watching bureaucrats pretend the system worked.

Here she made dumplings and her grandmother made kavdak and when the warband came back they all ate together. When Tsokh won, everyone knew he won. When someone else won, everyone would know that too. The headman could announce a betrayal to Tsokh's face and that would be legitimate. Old Gan could sit with his grand-niece who rode against his former band and everyone understood: he struggled while he could, now he accepts.

The singing continued. Young Gan was explaining something about the fight at the ford, demonstrating with her hands. The destriers grazed nearby, their insectile legs folding at impossible angles. The fire crackled. The stars came out above the basalt scablands.

Mirya took another dumpling. Dipped it in the kavdak, then the sour cream. The smoke, the tartness, the cooling dill. The meat and spices inside the dumpling, hot and substantial.

This was why. Not because it was kind \- it wasn't kind. Because it was clear. Because when you struggled you knew what you were struggling against, and when you accepted you knew what you were accepting. Because the headman could look Tsokh in the eye and say "Kelam's people are coming" and that would be honorable. Because old Gan sat with young Gan and nobody pretended the sides mattered more than the meal.

Fate is justice. Everyone ate.
