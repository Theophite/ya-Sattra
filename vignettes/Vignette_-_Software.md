**Software**

Seven chartered software corporations in ya-Tsatsa produce most lector software used throughout the Empire, exporting pristine memory cores that integrate flawlessly with Imperial computational infrastructure. These family businesses operate under charters predating the current Record, guaranteeing operational privacy so absolute that even the Bureau of the Lens cannot conduct physical inspections. The city imports no lectors or memory cores in meaningful quantity, yet the corporations claim to maintain adequate testing facilities within their compounds. The software functions perfectly. How it is produced remains deliberately, legally unknowable.

---

The Gan-Derem family has operated from the same compound for six Records. You can see the building from Horizon Streetâ€”three stories of white-gray stone, shuttered windows, a brass nameplate polished daily by servants who arrive before dawn and depart after sunset. The nameplate reads "Gan-Derem Computational Scriptorium, Est. Record of the Broken Chain, Imperial Charter 4-7-Kappa."

That charter number means more than most people understand. It references a class of warrants issued during the expansion era, when the Empire still believed it might need to negotiate with whatever it found among the stars. The charters guaranteed operational autonomy in exchange for essential services, protected their holders from all Imperial oversight except documentation review. Most such charters became irrelevant after the Interdict. A handful persisted for practical reasonsâ€”the es-Tsay lector monopoly, certain Creche breeding programs, the Ezh Dhol mathematical proofs. Seven persisted in ya-Tsatsa for reasons the Empire has decided not to examine.

I know this because my cousin married into the Gan-Derem line. Not the main branchâ€”she's the wife of a nephew who handles export documentation, which means she knows what leaves the compound but not what happens inside it. She told me once, over tea in the Akama district where we could speak freely, that her husband doesn't talk about his work. Not because it's forbidden. Because he genuinely has nothing to say about it. He processes shipping manifests, coordinates with factor-houses in ya-Sattra and ya-Don, arranges payment transfers through Coin Aureate intermediaries. The memory cores arrive from deeper in the compound in sealed transport crates. He logs them, certifies them, dispatches them. The cores function perfectly. Every single one.

"Where do they come from?" I asked her.

"The production halls."

"What's in the production halls?"

"I don't know. He's never been inside them."

Her husband was born into the Gan-Derem family, has worked there for twenty-three years, and has never seen where the actual work happens.

The Bureau of the Lens tried to investigate, of course. This was during my father's time, when the Institute of Domestic Intelligence was pursuing theories about Testament Society influence on software production. The Gan-Derem compound would be an obvious targetâ€”isolate the mathematicians, apply Compulsion, extract whatever cryptographic methodologies they're using. Standard procedure for any operation that might involve Oracle mysticism bleeding into Imperial infrastructure.

The IDI assembled a warrant. They presented it to the Gan-Derem patriarch, a seventy-year-old man named Ozen who my cousin describes as having the conversational warmth of a fish. He read the warrant carefully, excused himself, and returned ten minutes later with the family's charter documentation.

Charter 4-7-Kappa guarantees operational privacy. Not "operational discretion" or "operational autonomy"â€”privacy, which in the legal vocabulary of the expansion era meant something specific. It meant the Empire acknowledged that some functions required isolation from oversight not because they were suspect but because inspection would compromise their operation. The astronomers who mapped relativistic navigation routes operated under privacy chartersâ€”watching them work would introduce observer effects that corrupted the mathematics. The psychological conditioning programs that prepared colonists for centuries-long voyages required privacy because external examination would undermine the conditioning itself.

The IDI argued that modern interpretation should supersede obsolete protections. Ozen Gan-Derem pointed to clause seven, which specified that charter terms were perpetual and self-enforcing unless amended by the grantor. The grantor was a First Whorl administrator who experienced a five-year voyage that on Earth lasted eighty years. That administrator is still alive in the First Whorl, experiencing perhaps a decade of subjective time for every century that passes outside. He has not amended the charter. He will likely never amend the charter. He may not remember it exists.

The Institute of Imperial Record reviewed the case and confirmed Gan-Derem's interpretation. The charter stands. The IDI withdrew their warrant. The Institute for the Suppression of Knowledge lodged a formal protest, citing concerns about unmonitored technological development. The protest was noted and archived.

That was forty years ago. The investigation has not been repeated.

My cousin's husband processes fifty to seventy memory cores per week. Each core contains software updates, authentication protocols, or specialized applications for lector systems throughout the Empire. The cores arrive in his office in sealed transport crates, accompanied by documentation specifying destination, application type, and verification checksums. He's never seen one fail verification. In twenty-three years, not a single core has been defective.

"How is that possible?" I asked my cousin.

"They test thoroughly."

"With what lectors? The city doesn't import any."

"They have testing facilities in the compound."

"Have you seen them?"

She looked at me the way Tsatsans look at outsiders who ask questions that redirect to nowhere. "The facilities are in the production halls."

There are seven such companies in ya-Tsatsa. Gan-Derem, Mir-Olos, Tsan-Gavvi, Dhem-Kheret, Oron-Qal, Tep-Navim, and the largest, Khet-Moros. Together they employ perhaps three hundred people, though this number is difficult to verify because only those handling external operations are visible. The production staffâ€”the mathematicians, the programmers, whoever or whatever actually writes the codeâ€”work inside the compounds and are never seen in the city proper.

Khet-Moros exports two hundred cores weekly. If each core requires testing, they must possess at least a dozen lectors to maintain that output. But no lectors appear in port records. No memory cores are imported for testing purposes. The Bureau of the Lens attempted to cross-reference power consumption patterns to identify computational equipment, but the compounds' energy usage matches residential expectations. Either their lectors run on negligible powerâ€”which would be unprecedentedâ€”or they're using something else entirely.

The families themselves are... peculiar. Not in visible ways. They dress like other Tsatsans, attend Quiet Days, maintain household shrines. But they marry almost exclusively within the seven families, creating an endogamous network that's lasted at least six Records. The current generation of Gan-Derems includes marriages to Mir-Olos, Oron-Qal, and Khet-Moros lines. My cousin is an exception, brought in from a merchant family because the nephew she married couldn't find a suitable match within the network.

She says the family gatherings are strange. Everyone is polite, conversations remain pleasant, but there are topics that simply don't arise. No one discusses their work beyond logistics. No one mentions the production halls. When her husband's uncleâ€”one of the actual mathematiciansâ€”visits for dinner, he talks about the weather, the quality of autumn whistles, whether the cattle herds look healthy this year. Never mathematics. Never software. Never anything about what happens inside the white-gray buildings with their shuttered windows.

My cousin asked once, early in her marriage, what her husband's uncle actually did. Her husband said he worked in computational scripture. What kind? The kind that maintains lector functionality. Using what methodology? Traditional approaches. Could she see the work? The production halls aren't open to visitors. Why not? They're not set up for visitors.

She learned to stop asking.

The Institute of the Lattice accepts this situation with what I can only describe as aggressive indifference. They know the software works. They know it integrates cleanly with their cryptographic infrastructure. They've examined the code and found it technically sound, occasionally even elegant. The Lattice mathematicians who handle integration sometimes mutter about solution approaches they wouldn't have considered, mathematical shortcuts that suggest whoever wrote the code thinks about problems from unusual angles. But the code functions. That's sufficient.

What disturbs meâ€”what should disturb everyoneâ€”is that the Institute for the Suppression of Knowledge has publicly documented its concerns and been overruled. The ISK exists to prevent exactly this situation: opaque operations producing critical infrastructure without oversight. They maintain classification systems specifically to quarantine dangerous knowledge, run amnestic protocols to scrub contaminated information from human minds. Yet seven family businesses in ya-Tsatsa operate with complete immunity from the Empire's primary memetic security apparatus.

The official justification is that the charters predate the ISK's authority and cannot be retroactively modified. But charters have been amended before when security required it. The es-Norrel family's weapons patents were restricted after the Malpais War despite their original charter guaranteeing absolute production autonomy. The Creche breeding programs operate under constant Bureau oversight despite privacy protections. The Empire bends its own rules when necessary.

It chooses not to bend them here.

I think about the memory cores arriving in sealed crates, perfect every time, produced by methodologies no one will explain. And I think about the Bureau of the Lensâ€”the Empire's intelligence apparatus, the guardians of classified truth, the institution that knows secrets even the First Whorl has forgottenâ€”quietly withdrawing their investigation forty years ago and never returning.

The software companies export their cores. Ships carry them to every major city. The cores integrate into Imperial systems, processing tax records and authentication protocols and all the small computational tasks that make the Empire function. The Lattice mathematicians accept them. The Institute of the Echo distributes them. And somewhere in white-gray compounds with shuttered windows, something happens that the Empire has decided is better left unknown.

My cousin's husband processes his manifests. The cores arrive, perfect as always. He logs them, certifies them, ships them. And every night he returns home to his wife who was born outside the seven families, who will never understand what her husband's relatives do in the halls she cannot enter, who has learned that some questions redirect to nowhere.

The whistles drone outside. Autumn wind through ceramic tubes, playing tones that mean something to someone. Traditional observance, they say. Perhaps the archives have documentation.

The archives don't.
