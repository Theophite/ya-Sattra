## **Delegation Spring**

Kesho woke to electric light, as he had every morning of his life.

The chamber was carved deep enough that no natural light reached itâ€”eight paces by five, sandstone walls smoothed by three generations of habitation, the ceiling high enough that his father had hung copper lamps from hooks set into the stone. The lamps were dark now. The overhead bulb, fed by wire running through a channel cut near the ceiling, provided the same steady glow it had provided since before Kesho was born. Power from the Dam, too cheap to meter. He'd read that people in the Empire paid for light by the hour. He'd never quite believed it.

His wife Teshet was already up. He could hear her in the cooking chamber two tunnels down, where the ventilation shaft drew smoke upward and the extended family gathered for meals. The smell reached him before the sound did: eggs frying, the sharp fermented bite of marru, honey warming on the brazier.

His scales needed no protection from the canyon sun; they'd evolved to handle far worse than Ganat's spring warmth. The copper inlay across his forearms caught the light as he movedâ€”spirals and interlocking geometric patterns he'd done himself over the years, adding to the work as his skill improved. His journeyman pieces, from when he was seventeen, were hidden under his skirt at the hip. He'd considered removing them, reworking the scales, but his mother had talked him out of it. *Keep them. Remember what you didn't know.*

The blackwork was older still. His father had done the pattern across his shouldersâ€”scale after scale carefully charred with heated copper points, the keratin turning permanently dark. A traditional design, almost old-fashioned now. Pastoral. It marked him as someone whose family remembered the old ways, even three generations into urban life.

Breakfast was crowded. His parents' apartment connected to his own through a short tunnel, and his brother Anget's family had carved out space on the level below, accessible by ladder through a shaft Kesho and Anget had cut themselves the year Anget married. The cooking chamber served all three householdsâ€”twelve people this morning, counting the children. Teshet handed him a plate: flatbread torn into pieces, piled with scrambled eggs threaded through with shredded jerky and marru, the fermented peppers adding heat that built slowly and lingered.

"There's a cryer in the corridor," his mother said. "Dawn again. Offering free pulque at Sembela's if you come hear the argument."

"Sembela's serves goat piss," Anget said. "They're not winning anyone with that."

"They're winning the people who drink before noon." Their father, Worku, spoke without looking up from his plate. "Which during delegation season is half the Near Wall."

Kesho ate quickly. The Collective's assembly was in three daysâ€”the meeting where they'd declare whom to support, finalize their coalition arrangements, decide whether to pool with the other craft kilits or negotiate separately this year. The cryers had been working the artisan quarters for weeks. Dawn promised infrastructure investment, better terms for independent craftspeople, something about reformed water rights that Kesho didn't fully understand. Dusk promised stable trade relationships with the Empire, protection for established commercial arrangements, continued access to Imperial copper if the eastern mines couldn't meet demand.

The Copper-wrights had gone Dusk for years. The merchant kilits they sold to were mostly Dusk-aligned; the coalition worked. But the younger members had been talking about switching, arguing that Dawn's investment programs would benefit the craft quarters more than continued deference to merchant interests. The assembly would be contentious.

"You're voting?" Teshet asked.

"Everyone votes."

"I mean which way."

Kesho shrugged. He genuinely didn't know. Both arguments made sense. Both had problems. He'd listen to the speeches, watch who argued what, try to judge which coalition would actually deliver. That was the point of the assemblyâ€”not to ratify a decision already made, but to argue it out until something emerged.

The walk to the Copper Market took twenty minutes through tunnels that Kesho could have navigated blind. Left at the carved niche where someone had placed a small Oracle shrine decades ago, the seven-pointed star barely visible under accumulated offerings of copper wire and turquoise chips. Down two levels at the shaft with the blue-painted rungs. Through the main corridor where the vendor stalls opened in mornings, selling breakfast to workers heading toward the Dam.

A cryer stood at the junction where the corridor opened toward the canyon face, voice echoing off stone: "â€”the merchant kilits have held our delegation for three elections, and what have we seen? Prices set in their houses, not oursâ€”"

Kesho walked past. He'd heard the speech before. He'd hear it again. The cryer would be at a different junction tomorrow, or replaced by someone arguing the opposite. That was delegation season: the weeks when political life became impossible to ignore, when every bar and eating-house aligned with a tendency and advertised its loyalty through free drinks and open argument, when the question of whom your kilit should support became the only question anyone discussed.

The corridor opened onto a carved balcony, and Kesho paused as he always paused, letting his eyes adjust.

The canyon spread below him. The river ran silver-brown four hundred feet down, cutting between walls carved into terrace after terrace of habitation, workshop, market, shrine. The Dam rose to his left, grey and massive, the thrum of its turbines felt through the stone more than heard. Bridges spanned the gap at three levels: the top of the Dam for cargo and foot traffic, the Tyrant's Walk threading through the industrial interior, and lower spans he couldn't see from here.

The glass-fronted balcony belonged to no one and everyone. A public space carved generations ago, maintained by the neighborhood's informal organization, furnished with stone benches where old men sat in the evenings and children played in the mornings when the adults were at work. Kesho's family had no windowâ€”windows required proximity to the canyon face that their apartments didn't haveâ€”but the balcony was three minutes' walk. Close enough that Teshet brought the children here on rest days. Close enough that Kesho could stand in actual sunlight and see actual sky before descending to the forge.

He didn't linger. The Collective's workshops opened at seventh hour, and he had commissions to complete before the assembly consumed everyone's attention. A vessel for a merchant family, hammered geometric patterns that would take two days to finish. A set of jewelry for a weddingâ€”turquoise settings in copper frames, delicate work requiring focus and steady hands. The work paid. The work continued regardless of which coalition the Collective joined, which Senators the kilit supported, which party's cryers shouted loudest in the corridors.

But the assembly was in three days. And Kesho still didn't know how he'd vote.

He turned from the light and descended toward the market, copper inlay catching the sun one last moment before the tunnels swallowed him again.
