# **The Long Coast**

We left Orcas in the last week of trading season, when the factors were already calculating how many more runs we could make before the harbor iced. The *Covenant of Autumn* was heavy with Avouvar copper and bronze, low in the water, her crew of seventeen tired from three months of the northern run. I had signed on in Taho as a deckhand and found myself, by the journey's end, something between cook's assistant and unofficial translator for the captain's negotiations with people whose languages I did not speak but whose gestures I could read.

Captain Varo wanted one more run south before winter. The copper prices in ya-Don were twenty percent higher than in ya-Sattra, he said, and the overland route from the capital added costs that the rail monopoly extracted with mathematical precision. Better to sail the full length, unload at the Rift, take the river barges inland. It would add three weeks to the journey. It would also, he did not say but everyone understood, keep us employed through the season's end rather than paid off in the capital with winter wages that would not stretch to spring.

I had no objection. I had signed on to see the coast, and ya-Don was the coast's other end—the place where the goods we carried would become whatever the Furnaces produced, which would become the manufactured items we carried north, which would become the metals we carried south. The circle closed there, in the industrial heart of an Empire that claimed everything and controlled progressively less the further you traveled from its center.

---

We had left Asovoë three days before. I had not gone into the city—no one without Avouvar blood could survive the contamination—but I had seen it from Shaw Island during the quarantine inspection, and the sight had not left me.

The city glowed.

Not with the orange-amber of fire or the yellow of lamps, but with the aurora's colors: green and magenta and violet shifting across structures that rose against the grey sky like monuments to a civilization I could not comprehend. The buildings were black and gleaming, reflecting the colored light in patterns that seemed almost deliberate, as if the architecture had been designed to catch and scatter the aurora's display. Between the lit precincts, darkness stretched for what seemed like miles—empty districts where nothing moved, where the infrastructure maintained itself for a population that would never return.

The Avouvar factors who processed our cargo moved with a grace that made baseline humans look clumsy. They were nearly two meters tall, most of it leg, their skin the absolute black of ink, their clothing wrapped in scarves of aurora colors that shifted as they walked. I watched one of them count cargo manifests, her huge hands moving with precision that suggested centuries of practice. Later I learned she was two hundred and twelve years old, young by their standards, sent to the factorias because she had made enemies in precinct politics and needed decades away from the city to let grudges cool.

Two hundred and twelve. I was twenty-three.

The quarantine took two days. Factor-Quarantine Teszvelöran—I learned his name from the captain's complaints—examined everything: cargo holds, crew quarters, documentation, the captain's personal effects. He was looking for contamination, the captain said, but also for intelligence about the outside world that the Avouvar collected through their factors and spies and the endless patience of people who had centuries to accumulate information.

When we finally cleared inspection, I stood at the stern and watched the city recede. The aurora was active that night, greens and magentas dancing across the sky, and the city's black towers caught the light and held it. I thought: I will never see anything like this again. I thought: there are people in those precincts who remember events that happened before my great-great-grandmother was born.

I did not know, then, that I would be thinking about Asovoë for years afterward. That the sight of those glowing precincts against the ice-field backdrop would become the measure against which I judged everything else I saw.

---

The coast south of the San Juans belonged to the Thousand Kingdoms, which meant it belonged to whoever was strong enough to hold it.

We passed fishing villages where the banners changed between visits—this headman's colors flying where that headman's had flown six months before, the tribute arrangements renegotiated through violence or threat of violence, the villagers adapting to new masters with the resignation of people who had adapted many times before. We passed the mouths of rivers where Akama warbands watered their destriers, the carnivorous mounts clicking and steaming on the banks while their riders watched our ship with the calculating attention of people who respected guest-right but remembered that guest-right had limits.

At Ogon, where the Grand Canal met the sea, we took on fresh water and provisions. The autofactory city rose behind the harbor in geometries that made no sense—ziggurats and suspended corridors and towers that led nowhere, all of it maintained by systems that operated without human direction. The locals had built their lives into the interstices, timber structures clinging to concrete foundations, markets occupying plazas designed for purposes no one remembered.

I went ashore with the cook to buy fish sauce and dried salmon. The market smelled of wet concrete and wood smoke and something fermented that I could not identify. An Akama woman sold us provisions, her Serrulata scales decorated with copper wire that caught the grey coastal light. She spoke Imperial with an accent that flattened the vowels and hardened the consonants, and when I responded in kind—my ya-Don vowels flat as hers, my consonants shaped by a childhood among furnace workers—she looked at me with sudden interest.

"Furnace city," she said. It was not a question.

"My mother's family."

"Long way from home."

"Getting longer."

She added extra dried salmon to my purchase, a gift or a recognition or something I did not entirely understand. Akama commerce operated on principles that seemed arbitrary until you realized they were following rules you simply hadn't learned. I thanked her in the formal mode the captain had taught me, the mode that acknowledged hierarchy without specifying its direction, and she nodded once and turned to her next customer.

That night, anchored in the canal's geometric channel, I watched the autofactory's lights flicker through patterns that might have been communication or might have been malfunction. The locals maintained observation towers around the facility, watching for omens. Occasionally the autofactory produced something—extruded a structure that might stand for hours or might melt within minutes, vented gases whose composition the observers recorded without understanding. The whole city existed in relationship to this incomprehensible machine, drawing resources from its outputs while remaining unable to predict or direct what those outputs would be.

I thought about the Furnaces at ya-Don, which were also pre-Interdict facilities that operated beyond human comprehension. But the Furnaces produced things that humans used—lector components, illumination systems, the passive electronics that Imperial technology required. The autofactory at Ogon produced things that humans could not use, or could use only by accident, the way you might find a fallen branch useful for walking even though the tree had no intention of providing you with a cane.

The difference seemed important, though I could not have explained why.

---

South of Ogon, the coast became Imperial.

The change was gradual at first—more regular patrols, more standardized documentation requirements at the ports where we stopped for water and provisions. Then, perhaps two hundred miles north of ya-Sattra, the gradual became abrupt. A Bureau of the Lens cutter intercepted us, and uniformed officials came aboard to inspect cargo and credentials with an efficiency that the Akama warlords would never achieve.

They found nothing wrong. Our documentation was in order; the captain had been running this route for twenty years and knew exactly what the Bureaus required. But the inspection took four hours anyway, the officials examining everything twice, asking questions whose answers they already knew, demonstrating that Imperial authority existed and would be exercised regardless of whether the exercise served any purpose.

When they left, the captain spat over the rail and said nothing. The crew, who had been tense and silent during the inspection, resumed their work with the focused energy of people who wanted to be somewhere else.

I understood. I had grown up with the Bureaus. I knew how the weight of Imperial administration felt on the shoulders of people who had no choice but to bear it.

---

Ya-Sattra appeared at dawn, rising from the coast like something imagined rather than built.

The Inner City's spiral cone climbed into clouds that glowed amber and teal from within. The Antediluvian Quarter's arcologies reached heights that seemed impossible—cylinders and pyramids and structures I had no words for, some blazing with light, some dark and apparently dead, all of them built on a scale that made the ship beneath my feet feel temporary and small.

We did not dock at ya-Sattra. The captain had been clear about this: the capital's port fees would eat whatever margin the ya-Don copper prices provided. Instead we followed the coast south, past the Rift's mouth where the Iron Yards smoked on the southern cliffs, past fishing villages whose nets spread across waters that had been harvested for longer than anyone could remember.

The journey from ya-Sattra to the Rift's southern terminus took two days. Then we entered the river system that would carry us inland—narrow channels where the *Covenant of Autumn* barely fit, locks that raised us through elevation changes the coastal plain did not suggest, finally the broader waters of the valley's irrigation network where barges carried goods in both directions and the traffic required constant attention.

The climate changed as we traveled inland. The coastal fog gave way to clear skies. The temperatures rose. By the time we reached the junction where the river route met the rail lines from ya-Don, the air had become the dry heat I remembered from childhood—the heat that settles into your lungs and stays there, that makes you understand why the Ashrat evolved to run hot.

We transferred our cargo to river barges for the final leg. The *Covenant of Autumn* was too deep-drafted for the channels ahead; she would wait at the junction while Captain Varo negotiated the barge fees that would determine whether this run had been profitable. I had the option to wait with the ship or to continue with the cargo, and I chose to continue.

I told myself I wanted to see ya-Don. I told myself I was curious about the industrial city I had heard about throughout my childhood, the place where my mother's family had worked the Furnaces for three generations before my grandmother moved to Taho and married a Ganati merchant and produced children who belonged fully to neither world.

The truth was simpler: I did not want to wait. I had been moving for three months—north to the factorias, south to the capital, now inland to the Furnaces—and the momentum had become its own purpose. Stopping felt like something I could not afford, though I could not have said what would happen if I did.

---

The Furnaces appeared first as a glow on the horizon, visible for hours before we reached the city's edges.

It was nothing like Asovoë's aurora-colors. This was orange-amber, the color of heated metal, of exhaust vents, of processes that consumed and transformed. The glow did not shift or dance; it burned steadily, coloring the night sky above the Central Valley with a permanent artificial dawn.

As we approached, the shapes resolved: massive domes rising above the cityscape, their curves following geometries I could not quite describe; obelisk towers whose tops pulsed with expelled thermal energy; a three-dimensional maze of pipes—copper and bronze, oxidized to verdigris and rust—connecting everything in a network that seemed to have grown rather than been built.

The barge docked at a facility in what the pilot called the Freightyard district. It was nearly midnight, but the district was awake—shift changes at the Furnaces meant that ya-Don never fully slept, that there was always a tide of workers moving toward the gates or away from them, that the city's rhythms followed the production schedules rather than the sun.

I climbed the loading ramp and stood on ya-Don soil for the first time in my life.

The air tasted of ash. Not unpleasantly—a mineral tang that settled on the tongue and stayed there, that I would later learn came from the silicate compounds that Ashrat workers sweated out, that accumulated in the atmosphere and became part of the city's baseline reality. The pipes creaked and groaned overhead, thermal expansion producing sounds that the locals probably stopped hearing years ago. Somewhere nearby, a Mutterer choir was vocalizing—the mathematical droning of Technical Caste remnants whose broken cognition still produced outputs the Furnaces required.

An Ashrat woman walked past me, her grey skin shimmering in the ambient light, her work clothing marking her as a member of the Order of St. Akhmet's Furnace. She did not look at me. She was heading toward a gate where workers were queuing for the night shift, and her attention was on whatever task awaited her in the domes I could see rising above the rooftops.

I thought about Asovoë: the black towers catching aurora-light, the Avouvar factors moving with centuries of accumulated grace, the city maintaining itself for a population that would never return. I thought about the two hundred twelve-year-old factor who had seemed young to her people, sent south to escape precinct politics, counting cargo manifests with precision that baseline humans could not match.

Here, the oldest Ashrat would be lucky to reach sixty-five. Their metabolisms burned faster; their bodies consumed themselves in the service of heat tolerance that the Furnaces required. They measured time in shifts, not centuries. Their politics were practical, immediate, concerned with wage rates and guild elections and the endless negotiations between the Order and the Furnace Board.

I had traveled from the cold end of the coast to the hot end, from a city of radiation-extended lifespans to a city of accelerated mortality, from people who remembered the pre-Interdict era as recent history to people for whom three generations was deep time. The journey had taken three months. In Asovoë, three months was barely enough time to schedule a meeting.

The Ashrat woman disappeared into the queue at the gate. The pipes groaned. The Mutterer choir's vocalization shifted to a new harmonic, and somewhere in the Furnace complex, something responded—a change in the quality of the light, a subtle variation in the sound, a recognition that I probably imagined.

I picked up my bag and walked into the city my grandmother had left two generations before.

---

Dawn came slowly, filtered through the haze of particulates that ya-Don's industry produced. The sun did not so much rise as the sky brightened, orange giving way to pale blue at the horizon while the atmospheric haze caught the light and scattered it.

I found a tavern in the Threshold district—the one called the Burning Rest, though the name seemed ironic for a place whose customers were mostly too tired to care what they were drinking. The proprietor, an Ashrat woman named Jhen, asked no questions when I paid for a bed and a meal. I was clearly not local, my skin lacking the grey undertone that marked furnace exposure, my clothing cut wrong for bodies that ran hot and required heat dissipation. But I was also clearly labor caste, my hands calloused in the right patterns, my posture shaped by work that near-baseline bodies understood.

She gave me a corner bed and a bowl of something dense with calories—the kind of food that Ashrat metabolisms required, heavy with fat and protein, designed for bodies that would burn through it in hours. I ate because I was hungry, though the portions would have fed me for a day and were apparently intended as a single meal.

"From the coast?" she asked when I returned the bowl.

"From further than that."

"Ya-Sattra?"

"Asovoë."

She looked at me differently then. Not with suspicion, exactly, but with a recalibration of assumptions. "The far north. The contaminated city."

"I didn't go in. No one does, except the Avouvar. But I saw it from the islands."

"And?"

I thought about how to describe it: the black towers, the aurora-light, the precincts glowing against the glacier-backdrop, the absolute stillness of districts where nothing had moved in ten thousand years. I thought about the factor who was two hundred twelve years old and considered young.

"It makes you think about time differently," I said.

Jhen nodded slowly, as if this answer made sense to her even though I had not explained what I meant. "The Furnaces do that too," she said. "Different direction, though. They make you think about how little time there is."

She returned to her work, and I climbed to the corner bed, and I slept while the city around me changed shifts, while the pipes groaned and the Mutterers vocalized and the Furnaces burned with purposes that no one now living fully understood.

I dreamed of aurora-light falling on bronze towers, and when I woke the room was orange with reflected glow, and I could not immediately remember which city I was in.

---

I stayed in ya-Don for six weeks.

I had not planned to stay so long. I had planned to see the city, to understand something about the place my grandmother had left, and then to return to the *Covenant of Autumn* for the journey back north. But the captain sent word that repairs would delay departure until spring, and the barge company offered work to anyone willing to load and unload cargo, and I found myself becoming part of the city's rhythms in ways I had not anticipated.

The work was hard but not complicated: moving goods between barges and warehouses, learning which cargo required special handling, which destinations meant which routes through the maze of channels and rail lines that connected ya-Don to everywhere else. I worked with Ashrat and near-baselines and Redbacks whose strength made my contributions seem marginal, and I learned to navigate the guild politics that determined who got which shifts and which rates.

In the evenings, I explored.

The Threshold district bordered the Furnaces themselves, and I could see the gates where workers queued for entry, the checkpoints where credentials were verified, the constant flow of bodies that the industrial complex consumed and released. I visited the Cathedral of St. Akhmet, where the Penitent Church had merged with the labor guilds to produce something that was neither orthodox religion nor simple unionism but something stranger and more practical.

I walked through the Amber Quarter, where Serrulata merchants from Ganat maintained shops and warehouses that supplied goods the Empire did not officially import. I descended into the Depths of St. Yerren, where the Karst lived in tunnel networks that predated human memory, their tap-communication echoing through stone passages that I could not navigate without guides.

I ate Ashrat cuisine that made my mouth burn and my eyes water, prepared by a woman named Senna Korrad who was apparently writing a cookbook and wanted baseline opinions on recipes she was developing. The food was extraordinary—dense with flavor, calibrated for palates that could detect subtleties I had never known existed—and when I told her so, she wrote something in a notebook and asked if I would be willing to come back and try variations.

I visited the Pilgrim's Rest district, where the hermits lived—ancient near-baselines who had retreated from the world to contemplate the Virtues, who maintained small caves and cells in the foothills and emerged occasionally to offer cryptic wisdom that might or might not be useful. One of them, a woman who claimed to be one hundred and forty years old, asked me where I had come from. When I told her, she nodded and said: "The cold fire and the hot fire. You have seen both ends of the Empire now."

"Is that significant?"

"Everything is significant," she said. "The question is whether you notice."

---

I did not return to the *Covenant of Autumn*.

The captain sent word in spring that he was sailing without me, that he had found replacement crew in ya-Sattra and wished me well. I was not surprised. I had stopped waiting for the message, had stopped thinking of the ship as the thing I was returning to.

Instead, I found work with the Covenant of Saint Aureline, the logistics guild that controlled cargo movement throughout the Furnace complex and its surrounding districts. The work was similar to what I had done on the barges, but the scale was larger—thousands of workers moving millions of tons of material, the inputs and outputs of an industrial system that supplied the entire Empire with technologies it could not produce anywhere else.

I learned the tariff schedules. I learned which routes were controlled by which guild factions. I learned the names of the Aureate families—es-Kiral, es-Etaie, es-Vorath—who held patents on production processes and collected rents from labor they did not perform. I learned the political fault lines between the Order of St. Akhmet and the Covenant, between the surface guilds and the underground Unwritten-Axiom Parish, between the Church hierarchy and the heterodox theologies that ya-Don had developed across centuries of isolation from ya-Sattra's orthodoxy.

I learned, in other words, to live in a place that was nothing like the places I had lived before.

---

Three years later, I took a ship north again.

The *Grace of Salvation* was a Ganati vessel making the spring run to the factorias, carrying grain and manufactured goods that the Avouvar would trade for metals. I signed on as cargo supervisor, a position my Covenant credentials qualified me for, and I stood at the rail as ya-Sattra's spires receded and the coast opened into the grey expanse of the northern waters.

We passed Ogon, where the autofactory still extruded its incomprehensible outputs. We passed the Akama territories, where the banners had changed again, new warlords flying their colors over villages that had probably adapted without complaint. We passed the San Juan factorias, where the Avouvar factors processed cargo with the same precision they had shown three years before.

And finally, from Shaw Island's quarantine anchorage, I saw Asovoë again.

The aurora was active, greens and magentas dancing across the northern sky. The city's black towers caught the light and scattered it, the occupied precincts glowing against the glacier-backdrop, the empty districts dark and silent and maintained by systems that would continue operating long after everyone who remembered their original purpose had died.

I thought about the two hundred twelve-year-old factor, who would now be two hundred fifteen, who was still young by her people's standards, who might remember me as one of the thousands of baseline traders who passed through the factorias each season. I thought about Senna Korrad in ya-Don, who had published her cookbook and was now teaching classes in Ashrat cuisine to workers who had never thought of food as anything more than fuel. I thought about the hermit in the Pilgrim's Rest who had said that everything was significant, that the question was whether you noticed.

The cold fire and the hot fire. The aurora and the Furnace-glow. The people who remembered ten thousand years as lived experience and the people for whom three generations was deep time.

I had seen both ends of the Empire now.

I was not sure what it meant. But I had noticed, and perhaps that was enough.
