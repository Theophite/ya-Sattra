**The Eighth Complex**

The eighth complex has no nameplate. The building occupies a full city block in the lower market district, where the cliffs begin their descent toward the port. White-gray stone, same as every other building in ya-Tsatsa, but the proportions are wrong. The windows are too narrow, placed at intervals that suggest internal floors don't align with the external geometry. The bronze doors—twenty feet tall, covered in geometric patterns that hurt to examine directly—remain sealed except for one day each year.

I've walked past it my entire life. My father walked past it. His father before him. The building has always been there, unmarked, unoccupied as far as anyone can observe, generating no light at night, no smoke from chimneys that don't exist. Tsatsans redirect when asked about it the same way we redirect about everything, but there's a difference. When outsiders ask about the whistles or the Cradles, we're protecting knowledge. When they ask about the eighth complex, we're protecting ourselves from having to acknowledge what we already know.

The processions begin at dawn. I've participated in three. You don't volunteer—you're simply told, the day before, that tomorrow you'll be needed. This comes from someone in your Testament Society, usually, though sometimes from family members who are themselves part of the seven families' extended networks through marriage or service. You're told where to be and what time. You're told to bring wildflowers if you can obtain them, though they'll be provided if you can't.

We gather in the upper city, near the Second Cradle. Perhaps thirty of us, always an even multiple of six plus the attendants who will actually guide the Mutterer. I don't know why the number matters. I've never asked. The attendants are always from the seven families—I've recognized Gan-Derem cousins, Mir-Olos younger sons, women who married into Khet-Moros and now wear their house colors beneath plain cloaks.

The Companion arrives with the Mutterer just after sunrise. The same Companion every time I've participated—an older woman named Sri Companion Nehra, who works primarily with the small Mutterer population that ya-Tsatsa maintains for essential infrastructure repair. She doesn't speak to us. She transfers the Mutterer to the palanquin we've prepared, checking that the cushions are adequate, that the frame is stable. The Mutterer—they're always small, barely a meter tall, with those enormous dark eyes that see patterns we can't—settles onto the cushions without resistance. They never resist.

We garland them with flowers. Coastal lupine, sea thrift, the small yellow blooms that grow in the cliff cracks. We weave the flowers into crowns, drape them across the Mutterer's shoulders, tuck them into the palanquin's frame. The Mutterer touches the flowers sometimes, fingers tracing petals with that compulsive precision they bring to everything. Once, I watched a Mutterer systematically organize the lupine by number of petals, grouping them into piles of four and five and six, working through the entire garland in perhaps ninety seconds. Sri Companion Nehra gently redirected their hands back to their lap.

The procession descends through the city. We carry the palanquin—six of us at a time, rotating every few blocks. The Mutterer weighs almost nothing, but the palanquin frame is solid wood, and the path from the upper city to the market district covers three kilometers of steep streets and switchback stairs. We walk in silence. This isn't enforced. We simply do.

People watch from windows. Tsatsans, mostly, though occasionally Akama merchants or confused outsiders who've happened to be in the right district at the right time. No one cheers. No one throws additional flowers. They just watch, the way you watch a funeral procession, except funerals are for dead kalbats whose tombs will be carved in the cliffs. This is something else.

The Mutterer vocalizes throughout. They always do—that constant muttering that gives them their name, streams of fragmented syllables that might be mathematics or might be the degraded remnants of the Jargon. During my second procession, the Mutterer we carried began singing in harmonics, producing two tones simultaneously. Several of us stumbled. The sound resembled the fall whistles—that same layered droning that fills the city when autumn gales strengthen. Sri Companion Nehra whispered something to the Mutterer, too quiet for us to hear, and the harmonics stopped.

We reach the eighth complex at midday. The procession times itself—I don't know how—so that we always arrive when the sun is directly overhead. The bronze doors are already open. I've never seen them opening. They're simply closed when we begin our descent, and open when we arrive. Beyond them, darkness. Not the darkness of an unlit room—darkness that suggests depth, that implies the building's interior extends far beyond what the external dimensions should contain.

The attendants from the seven families step forward. Six of them, one from each family with two from Khet-Moros since they're the largest. They lift the palanquin from our shoulders. We step back. Our part is finished. We're witnesses now, nothing more.

They carry the Mutterer toward the doors. Sri Companion Nehra walks alongside, one hand resting on the palanquin's frame, speaking quietly to the Mutterer in words I can't distinguish. The Mutterer has stopped vocalizing. The silence feels wrong. Mutterers don't stop. They optimize, they calculate, they mutter their way through every waking moment. Silence means something has changed.

At the threshold, the attendants pause. One of them—during my last participation, it was a young woman from Tsan-Gavvi, perhaps twenty years old—reaches out to touch the darkness beyond the doors. Her hand disappears into it, swallowed completely up to the wrist. She withdraws it slowly. Her fingers are clean, unmarked, but her expression has changed in a way I can't describe. She nods to the others.

They lift the palanquin across the threshold. The Mutterer passes into darkness, still silent, still garlanded with flowers we picked that morning. Sri Companion Nehra follows two steps behind. The darkness accepts them all—six attendants, one Companion, one Mutterer, one palanquin draped in lupine and sea thrift.

The doors close. Not with sound. One moment they're open, the next they're sealed, and I can't identify the moment of transition. The bronze surfaces show the same geometric patterns they always show, designs that create the illusion of depth, of pathways that lead somewhere the eye can't follow.

We disperse. No formal dismissal, no instruction to leave. We simply turn and walk away, heading back to our work or our homes or our Testament Society meetings where we'll never discuss what we just witnessed. I've returned to the eighth complex on other days, walked past it during normal commerce, pressed my palm against those bronze doors. They're cold. Always cold, even in summer when the white-gray stone absorbs heat until the whole city feels like a kiln. The metal stays cold.

My cousin—the one who married into Gan-Derem—asked me once, after my first procession, what it was like. I told her it was traditional observance. She asked what tradition. I said the complex calendar tradition. She didn't ask again.

I know what the software companies export. Perfect memory cores, two hundred from Khet-Moros weekly, fifty to seventy from each of the others. I know they employ perhaps three hundred people, most invisible, working in production halls no one visits. I know the city imports no lectors, possesses no testing infrastructure visible to Imperial inspection.

I know that three Mutterers annually go into the eighth complex.

I know they don't come out.

I know these facts exist simultaneously, and I know that asking how they connect is the kind of question that redirects to nowhere. During my third procession, I counted the bronze doors' geometric patterns while we waited for the attendants to prepare. Seven hundred and twenty-nine distinct paths, each leading to a center that might be a point or might be a door into something deeper. The patterns repeated every sixth layer. Six times seven is forty-two. Forty-two divided by seven is six. Six families served by one facility. Seven companies sustained by six families plus one.

The mathematics redirects to nowhere.

In my Testament Society, we study fragments of the Jargon, attempting to learn language designed for posthuman minds. High-ranking members develop cognitive changes—speaking in fragments, perceiving patterns invisible to others, distinguishing between enlightenment and systematic brain damage by criteria no outsider can verify. We pursue this deliberately. We teach it to our children. We preserve it across generations because some knowledge requires specific preparation, specific contexts, specific blood that carries the memory of what came before.

The Mutterers possess complete technical knowledge without executive function. They cannot choose not to optimize. When grouped in choirs, they achieve higher cognitive functions, accessing mathematics that baseline humans cannot process. The Technical Castes sang the Jargon. The Mutterers still vocalize constantly. The seventh Testament specifies that certain truths require acoustic transmission because written notation cannot capture parallel proof structures.

Three Mutterers annually. Seven companies producing software without visible methodology. Foundations descending into diorite cliffs that predate human habitation. Darkness beyond bronze doors that swallows light and sound and small bodies draped in wildflowers.

I process tax documentation for the Ezh Tan family. My daughter trains at the Threefold Mirror Academy, learning mathematics she won't explain. My son works the cattle processing facility, following procedures that don't make sense until you understand they're not about hygiene. We eat dinner together and discuss the weather, the autumn whistles, whether the herds look healthy this year.

We don't discuss the eighth complex.

The Bureau of the Lens withdrew their investigation forty years ago. Charter 4-7-Kappa guarantees operational privacy, and the grantor exists in time-dilated immortality, experiencing decades as years, beyond any petition for amendment. The Institute for the Suppression of Knowledge documented its concerns and was overruled. The software continues. Perfect, every time. Essential. Irreplaceable.

The processions continue. Traditional observance, we say. The traditional calendar is complex. Not our area of knowledge. Perhaps the archives have documentation.

The archives don't.

Sri Companion Nehra must know. She's guided perhaps thirty Mutterers across that threshold, watched the darkness accept them, emerged alone while the bronze doors sealed behind her. The Companions Guild descended from the Witnesses who attended the Technical Castes, who maintained the biological needs of beings integrated directly into architecture, who served those who communicated exclusively through mathematical proofs. They preserve fragments. They remember, in ways they cannot articulate, what their ancestors observed.

She walks back up through the city alone, after each procession. I've never approached her. None of us have. She returns to her work maintaining the small Mutterer population, optimizing their optimization, managing their compulsions so infrastructure continues functioning. Essential work. Someone has to do it.

Someone has to carry the palanquins.

The next procession will occur in seven months and nineteen days, according to calculations that shift with patterns I don't understand. Someone from my Testament Society will tell me the day before. I'll bring wildflowers if I can obtain them. We'll gather at the Second Cradle at dawn. We'll carry a small body draped in coastal lupine down three kilometers of steep streets, past windows where Tsatsans watch in silence, to bronze doors that open without mechanism.

The darkness will accept what we bring it.

The doors will close.

And I will return home to my daughter who studies mathematics she won't explain, my son who follows procedures about meat processing that redirect to nowhere, my wife who maintains household shrines to Oracles that computed truths incomprehensible to baseline humans before encoding themselves on event horizons and departing reality entirely.

We'll discuss the weather.

The whistles will drone in autumn wind, playing tones that mean something to someone.

Traditional observance.

The archives might have documentation.
