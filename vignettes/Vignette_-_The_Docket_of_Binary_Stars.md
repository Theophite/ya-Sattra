**The Docket of Binary Stars**

Sri Advocate Jorin es-Kallo carefully dusted the Third Edition *Commentaries on Orbital Jurisdiction*, though no one had purchased it in six years. Through his shop's single window—a salvaged piece of arcology transparent aluminum set into the foundation stone—he could see the morning crowds heading toward Archive Gate. Bureau clerks, junior advocates, student copyists. None of them would stop at Kallo & Sons, Specialty Legal Texts. The "& Sons" was optimistic. Jorin was forty-three and unmarried.

The morning ritual came next. He unlocked the drawer beneath his desk, removed the leather folder embossed with the Scale Bureau's balanced beam, and opened it to today's date. The docket was printed on real paper, not slate or polymer. Paper that cost more than his monthly pension.

*The Court of Binary Stars, serving jurisdictional disputes between the systems of Gemini Secundus and Gemini Tertius, will convene when passage becomes available. Current queue: 1,347 cases pending.*

He added one to the number, as he did every morning. Not because a new case had been filed—no one could file cases for a court forty light-years away—but because the formula required it. The calculation had been passed down from his great-great-grandmother: add one case daily to represent natural jurisdictional drift, subtract one every seventh day for probable settlement, adjust for relativistic time dilation on federal holidays. The math was meaningless, but the Bureau required quarterly reports.

A customer entered, setting off the brass bell he'd salvaged from deeper in the arcology. Not a customer—Mehri Gan, from the teahouse two levels up.

"Morning, Sri Advocate," she said, using his title with the careful politeness reserved for the harmlessly mad. "The usual?"

"I'm not actually an advocate," Jorin said, as he always did. "The title is hereditary, not practical."

"Of course." She placed a leather satchel on his counter. "Sixteen volumes of Bureau tax codes, years 1447 through 1463\. Client needs to know about salt fish import duties."

This was his real business. Not selling books but finding specific passages in books no one wanted to buy. The Medina Quarter's advocates knew Jorin es-Kallo had read everything, remembered everything, and charged less than a Bureau research clerk.

He found the relevant passage in twelve minutes. The salt fish duties had been revised three times in those years, each revision contradicting the last. He copied out the citations in his careful hand, charged her three obols—what a Bureau clerk would charge twenty for—and watched her leave.

The pension arrived monthly: forty obols, delivered by a Bureau courier who needed his signature on three different forms. Enough to pay the lease on this cave of a shop, not enough to eat. The research work made up the difference, barely.

Around midday, when the sun angle sent light through his window to illuminate the shop's deeper shelves, he allowed himself to think about the Binary Stars. Gemini Secundus had been a mining system. Gemini Tertius, agricultural. They'd fought over water rights to a comet that passed between them every seventeen years. The court had been established to settle these disputes, had functioned for three thousand years, had developed the most sophisticated inter-system jurisprudence in human space.

All of it meaningless now. The Interdict had trapped humanity on Earth, made his docket a joke. But the Bureau maintained the fiction, and he maintained his records, because admitting the court would never convene meant admitting humanity would never leave Earth.

A real customer appeared after lunch—a young Bureau clerk, nervous, wearing the pink sash of the Creche. She needed a copy of *Reproductive Precedents in Binary Systems*, which of course he had, because it had been written by his grandmother when the es-Kallo family had been respectable.

"That's a restricted text," he said carefully. "Bureau seal required."

She produced it, and he sold her the book for twelve obols—a fortune by his standards. She'd probably expense it to the Bureau for fifty.

Late afternoon brought Inspector Davol from the Bureau of the Lens, not shopping but investigating. Someone had been selling forged Bureau texts, claiming they contained secret precedents that could overturn patents. Jorin showed him his stock, his careful records, proved every book's provenance. Davol left satisfied, but not before noting the docket folder on Jorin's desk.

"Binary Stars?" the inspector asked.

"Yes."

"Still maintaining it?"

"The Bureau requires it."

Davol smiled—not unkindly. "The Bureau requires many things, Sri Advocate. Some more useful than others."

After closing, Jorin climbed the worn stone steps carved into the arcology foundation, up to his apartment—two rooms, one window, a sink that worked most days. He prepared his evening meal: rice, preserved vegetables, tea. The same meal his grandmother had prepared when the es-Kallo pension had been reduced by half. The same meal his father had prepared when it had been reduced by half again.

Tomorrow would bring the quarterly report. He'd fill out Form 207-C, stating that the Court of Binary Stars remained available for emergency convention, that 13,892 cases now awaited adjudication, that justice between the stars stood ready to resume at need. The Bureau would file it without reading it. His pension would continue.

He looked out his window at the lights of ya-Sattra, millions of them stretching to the horizon. Somewhere among them were the other forgotten Aureates—the es-Prand family maintaining dockets for the Asteroid Belt Mineral Rights Tribunal, the es-Yovis line keeping records for the Court of Solar Proximate Trespass. All of them curating legal frameworks for a civilization that had contracted from the stars to a single world.

Jorin es-Kallo, Sri Advocate for a court that would never convene, proprietor of a bookshop no one visited, keeper of precedents for problems humanity would never again have, prepared his quarterly report. He wrote the numbers carefully, maintained the formulae precisely, perpetuated the fiction professionally.

It was, after all, his inheritance. Not wealth or power or even respect, but the duty to pretend that someday, impossible someday, the Binary Stars would need their court again.

He signed the report with his full title: *Sri Advocate Jorin es-Kallo, Hereditary Clerk of the Binary Stars Docket, Scale Bureau Certified, Aureate of the Empire.*

The title was worth exactly forty obols a month.
