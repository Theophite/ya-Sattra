The door chimed again. This time it was Advocate Rai es-Tomlenn, whose family held the docket for the Ninth Circuit Court of Colonial Patents. Her shop was two levels up, specializing in technical manuals. They nodded to each other with the weary solidarity of the functionally impoverished.

"The Bureau's asking for updated procedural documentation," she said without preamble. "They want to know if our courts would use modern encryption standards if they reconvened."

Pellam almost laughed. His court's jurisdiction covered trade disputes between star systems. The speed of light alone would make any case take centuries to adjudicate. But the Bureau insisted on maintaining the infrastructure, just as his great-great-grandmother had insisted on accepting the appointment when the es-Corin family had still harbored delusions of relevance.

"I'll draft something," he said. "Reference the current cipher protocols, suggest compatibility frameworks. The usual."

They both knew the reports would be filed, processed, archived, and forgotten. Some clerk in the Scale would stamp them as received, maintaining the elaborate pretense that humanity's interstellar legal system was merely dormant, not defunct.

After she left, clutching her books like talismans against examination failure, Pellam sat in the quiet of his shop. Through the narrow window, he could see the Archive Gate, its massive stonework arch marking the transition from Medina's ordered streets to the Iron Yards' industrial chaos. Clerks and salvagers passed in both directions, none of them knowing or caring that they walked past the chambers of a hereditary judge, empowered by ancient law to adjudicate disputes between stars humanity would never reach again.

The pension notification arrived by pneumatic tube, the only official Bureau correspondence he'd receive this month. Sixteen obols, automatically deposited. *Sri Advocate es-Corin,* it read. *The Bureau requires your formal opinion on a hypothetical matter: If a vessel launched before the Interdict were to return, would your court maintain jurisdiction over its cargo disputes? Please prepare full documentation of applicable precedents.*

Pellam read it three times. The Bureau never asked hypotheticals without reason. Somewhere, somehow, someone thought a pre-Interdict vessel might actually return. And if it did, if it carried cargo or crew or disputes from the time before humanity's imprisonment...

He looked at his shop, his books, his small life built in the ruins of greater days. For the first time in forty years, Sri Advocate Pellam es-Corin had actual work to do. Even if it was probably nothing. Even if the vessel would never come. Even if his court remained forever hypothetical.

He pulled down the first volume of *Intersystem Salvage Rights* and began to read, preparing for a case that would almost certainly never come before a court that hadn't sat for longer than recorded history. Outside, the Medina Quarter conducted its loud business of actual commerce, while in his shop built into the bones of the fallen world, an Aureate with a meaningless title did the only thing he could do:

He maintained the law against the day it might matter again.
