## **The First Blood**

Sela Loak had been watching Jorik for three weeks, learning his patterns with the patience of someone who understood that revolution required precision. Every evening at sixth bell, he descended from the eleventh-floor barracks to supervise the communal kitchen's food distribution. Always aloneâ€”the other Chain-Men considered watching starving people eat to be poor entertainment. He would lean against the door frame, occasionally confiscating portions that looked too generous, making mothers beg for their children's bowls.

The prayer circle had gathered ostensibly for evening devotions. Two hundred of them, packed into the third-floor chapel space, singing the Plea's verses about divine justice with particular emphasis. The sapphire-glass knives were distributed during the ritual passing of handsâ€”Bos Loak's work, knapped to molecular sharpness, small enough to hide in a palm. The cargo hooks had been sharpened, blessed with oils that Sela claimed were sacramental but smelled suspiciously of industrial lubricant.

"The Foreigners see all," Sela intoned from her position near the ceiling beam, where she'd climbed during the third verse. "They witness our suffering. They witness our endurance. They witness what we do in their sight."

Jorik entered on schedule, his massive frame filling the doorway. Nine feet of Warborn muscle and bone armor, wearing the casual arrogance of someone who'd never been successfully challenged. He scanned the crowd of diners, looking for infractions, for excuses. His eyes passed over Sela's position without seeing herâ€”people didn't look up in Bright-Spire. The ceilings were too low, too oppressive.

She dropped.

The garrotte was salvaged cable, wrapped in cloth to prevent it cutting her own hands. She'd practiced the motion on sacks of grain, understanding that she'd have one chance to loop it around his throat before his reflexes engaged. But she'd underestimated the thickness of his neck, the way the bone plating extended up from his shoulders. The wire caught but couldn't close completely.

Jorik roared, reaching back to grab her, but she'd wrapped her legs around his torso, clinging like a child to a parent's back, if the child were trying to strangle the parent and the parent were a giant. He spun, trying to slam her against the wall, but the prayer circle was already moving.

They came from every directionâ€”not a mob but a congregation, singing the twenty-third verse of the Plea about the righteousness of defending the innocent. The first knives found the spaces between his armor plates, those vulnerable points where joints required flexibility. Tiny punctures, each no deeper than a finger's width, but there were so many hands, so many knives.

The cargo hooks came next, wielded by dock workers who knew how to find purchase in anything. They caught him behind the knees, in the tendons that even Warborn engineering couldn't fully armor. Jorik went down slowly, like a great tree being felled by a thousand small cuts, Sela still clinging to his neck, her voice rising above the chorus: "Witness this\! The tyrants can die\! They bleed like men because they ARE men\!"

The kitchen had gone silent except for the singing. The hundreds who weren't part of the prayer circle pressed against the walls, children lifted onto shoulders to see over the crowd, everyone watching as Jorik's struggles grew weaker. Blood pooled on the floor, mixed with the perpetual water seepage, created patterns that looked almost decorative in the lamplight.

When he finally stopped moving, Sela remained on his back for a long moment, her face transformed by something that wasn't exhaustion or horror but a kind of terrible joy. She stood slowly, her congregation parting before her, their knives disappearing back into sleeves and pockets.

"Drag him to the basement," she commanded, her voice carrying new authority. "The Bannicks will know what to do."

It took twelve people to move the body, leaving a trail of blood down nine flights of stairs that everyone pretended not to see. In the flooded basement, the Bannick eelsâ€”those industrial-waste-adapted creatures that could digest almost anything organicâ€”would leave nothing but metal fragments. Within three days, even those would be sold as scrap.

The Chain-Men would come asking, of course. But 2,700 people had seen nothing. The kitchen had been crowded, chaotic. Jorik must have left. Perhaps he deserted. Perhaps he had enemies among his own kind. Who could say what happened to a violent man in a violent world?

The next morning, Sela stood at the seventh-floor checkpoint, paying her toll with the same polite deference as always. The Chain-Man on dutyâ€”Big Remâ€”studied her face, looking for something, finding only the patient expression of a Penitent deacon who counseled resignation and acceptance.

"Your prayers were particularly loud last night," he said.

"We sing with special fervor when the spirit moves us," she replied. "The twenty-third verse speaks of justice. Would you like to hear it?"

He waved her through without taking her money, disturbed by something he couldn't name.

In her tenth-floor apartment, directly below the Chain-Men's barracks, Sela knelt before her makeshift altar. The components were already gatheredâ€”industrial fertilizer from the Middens' agricultural supplies, diesel fuel from the dock reserves, materials that individually meant nothing but together would bring down the ceiling. The prayer circle would need three more weeks to accumulate enough. Three more weeks of paying tolls, of bowing heads, of pretending Jorik's death had satisfied them.

She pressed her forehead to the floor, feeling the vibrations of Chain-Men moving above, and thanked the Foreigners for revealing this truth: that the oppressors were not gods but meat, and meat could be cut, and what could be cut could be destroyed entirely. The liberation of Bright-Spire would begin not with negotiation or reform but with the fundamental recognition that nine feet of engineered muscle was still mortal.

"Grant us the strength," she whispered, though whether she prayed to the Foreigners, the universe, or the ghost of Jorik dissolving in eel-filled water, she couldn't say. "Grant us the strength to make them all witness."
