# **The Particular Confession of Datho Kel el-Marwen**

The man from Toh had a name Datho never used. He was perhaps forty, near-baseline, with the kind of face that suggested minor Bureau family—someone's third son, educated enough for intelligence work, not valuable enough to protect.

"You will bring the matter before the Senate," the man said. Not asking. Telling. "The documentation is already prepared. You simply present it as a concerned citizen who has discovered irregularities in Ministry of Justice payment chains."

Datho's office was small. Two chairs, a desk, a window looking out over Taho's commercial district. The man sat with his legs crossed, comfortable, as though this were a routine matter between colleagues.

"And if I refuse?"

"You won't." The man smiled. "We've discussed this. You understand your position. All that remains is the timing, and I'm telling you the timing is now."

Something shifted.

Datho had imagined this conversation many times. In his imagination, he was always calculating—weighing options, measuring risks, looking for angles. The nervous broker, the readable face, the man who couldn't hide what he felt. In his imagination, he was afraid.

He wasn't afraid.

He was looking at the man from Toh and realizing that he hated him. Not strategically, not as an obstacle to be managed. He hated the comfort in that chair. He hated the smile. He hated the pretense that this was help rather than destruction, that the man was doing Datho a favor by telling him which of his colleagues to betray.

"The whistleblower," Datho said. "The one who will testify about my contacts with your service if I don't cooperate. He's yours, isn't he?"

The smile flickered.

"He has to be," Datho continued. "You don't have infinite assets here. The person who burns Rasma and the person who burns me—it's the same mouth. And if I name him first, you can't use him for either."

"That's not—"

"It is, though." Datho felt something opening in his chest. A door he hadn't known was there. "You're the only card. You play it against her or you play it against me, but you can't play it twice."

The man from Toh uncrossed his legs.

---

Assembly at Gates-of-Ascension met on the seventh day in a converted warehouse near the Taho waterfront. Perhaps two hundred congregants—archaeotech traders, Academy engineers, manufacturing supervisors, people who worked with their hands on machines they were slowly learning to understand. The walls were bare brick. The lighting was electric, a point of pride. No oil lamps, no candles. Power from the grid, humming with Republican industry.

Datho had attended for nine years. He sat in the back during Common Confession, listened to the theology of the Interdict as punishment, humanity's exile as consequence, the slow work of understanding as the path back to the stars. He had never delivered a Particular Confession. He had never felt he had enough to confess.

The Curate was a woman named Essa Kel-Talith, sixty years old, who had worked the Furnace interface contracts before the Revolution and still bore the radiation scars. She recognized Datho when he approached her before the service.

"Consul," she said. "You wish to speak?"

"Yes."

"A Particular Confession is not a small thing. You understand you are accounting for your works before the congregation and before those who watch."

"I understand."

She studied his face. Everyone studied his face. They thought they could read it. They were right, mostly. "You look like a man who has made a decision."

"I have."

---

He stood before them in the clothes he'd worn to the Senate that morning. Good clothes, merchant-class, nothing ostentatious. His hands were steady. His voice was clear. The nervousness that everyone associated with Datho Kel el-Marwen was absent, and he could see the congregation noticing its absence, adjusting their expectations.

"I am here to confess my works," he said. "And to justify them."

The traditional opening. But the words that followed were not traditional.

"For eleven years, I have operated a brokerage facilitating the movement of archaeotech components from Imperial salvage operations to Republican manufacturing. This work has made me wealthy. It has also advanced the technological capacity of this Republic in ways that I believe—that I know—serve humanity's eventual liberation from the Interdict."

He paused. Let them hear the conviction. It was genuine. It was always genuine when he was speaking.

"For seven years, I have held a seat in the Republican Senate, representing a coalition of archaeotech workers, veterans, and labor organizations who trusted me to advocate for their interests. I have tried to honor that trust. I have also betrayed it."

The silence was absolute.

"I am in debt to three merchant houses whose names I will now provide. The Kel-Asura consortium. The Merevin trading family. The House of Sala el-Dorat. These debts were incurred to build the political coalition that elected me. They carry terms designed to ensure my compliance with the interests of these houses, which do not align with the interests of my constituents."

He could see people writing. Good.

"These houses have used their leverage to extract specific policy concessions. I will enumerate them."

And he did. Twelve years of small corruptions, exposed in precise detail. Contract preferences. Regulatory exceptions. Favorable interpretations of trade law. He named names, cited dates, described meetings. The congregation sat motionless. Several had stopped writing, overwhelmed.

"I confess these works," Datho said. "And I justify them."

The ripple of confusion was visible.

"The alternative was to lose my seat to someone who would not advance the technological development of this Republic. Someone who would not understand that every component moving from Imperial salvage to Republican workshops is a step toward breaking the Interdict. Someone who would treat commerce as commerce rather than as the mechanism of our liberation."

He was building now. He could feel it—the argument assembling itself, surprising him with its elegance, its force. He believed it even as he constructed it. He always did.

"The merchant houses believed they owned me. They owned my votes on matters affecting their specific interests. They did not own my vision. They did not understand that the framework they exploited was also the framework that enabled genuine progress. I used their money to build something they could not see."

A woman in the third row—an Academy metallurgist he recognized—was nodding slowly.

"But this is not the only confession I must make."

He let the pause stretch. The warehouse was silent except for the hum of electric lights.

"I have been approached by agents of the Imperial Bureau of the Lens on four occasions over the past eleven years. The first approach occurred before my election to the Senate. The most recent occurred six days ago."

Now the silence had a different quality. Fear. Anger. The dawning recognition that they were hearing something that could not be taken back.

"I refused every approach. I have documentation of these refusals, which I will provide to the Ministry of Internal Affairs. I did not report the approaches at the time because I believed—correctly—that the taint of contact would be treated as evidence of collaboration. I chose to refuse quietly and continue my work rather than sacrifice my capacity for service to bureaucratic suspicion."

He saw Curate Kel-Talith's expression shift. She understood what he was doing. Confession and justification, woven together, each making the other more credible.

"The Bureau of the Lens has recently attempted to coerce my cooperation by threatening to expose my unreported contacts through an agent placed within the Ministry of Internal Affairs. This agent—whose name is Yevan Sorda and who currently holds a supervisory position in the Ministry's counterintelligence division—was to serve as a whistleblower whose testimony would destroy my political career and potentially result in my imprisonment."

He was giving them Sorda. Burning the asset before the asset could be deployed. The man from Toh would learn about this within hours. There was nothing he could do. The accusation was public, irrevocable. Whatever Sorda said now would sound like retaliation.

"The same agent was prepared to provide testimony against Dawn Consul Rasma Dhal-Setil, claiming that she has authorized illegal payments through Ministry of Justice channels to fund the activities of Kelso Senna, currently operating in the borderlands between the Republic and the Thousand Kingdoms."

Complete stillness.

"The payments are real. But it was not Rasma Dhal-Setil who authorized them. It was me."

He heard someone gasp. He continued.

"For three years, the Ministry of War and the Ministry of Internal Affairs have facilitated payments to the Cloud Temple Band. They acted on what they believed to be a co-consular directive. It was not. It was my personal authorization, issued under circumstances that led the relevant officials to believe they were executing lawful policy. Anyone who participated in this payment chain is blameless. They were deceived by me."

He let that settle.

"I authorized this expenditure because I believe—because I know—that the Republic owes its heroes, and that Kelso Senna, whatever his current circumstances, remains a hero of the Revolution."

The lie came out with perfect conviction. He heard it and was astounded at how good it sounded. Of course he had funded Senna. Of course the payments were his. It made sense. It felt true. Rasma's cautious, moderate, pragmatic politics couldn't contain a gesture this romantic. But Datho—the mystical Penitent, the believer in technological redemption, the man who wanted to bring down the Interdict—of course he would fund the revolutionary legend.

He was protecting her. He was also stealing something from her. He couldn't quite tell which mattered more.

"The Bureau of the Lens intended to use this information to compromise the Dawn Consul. They believed I would deliver it because I was already compromised. They did not understand—"

His voice caught. For the first time, something genuine broke through the performance.

"They did not understand that I would rather destroy myself than be used against the Republic I have served. That I would rather confess every corrupt bargain, every unreported contact, every hidden payment, than allow a foreign intelligence service to dictate the terms of my exposure."

He was crying. He could feel the tears on his face, and he knew the congregation could see them, and he knew that they would believe him because they could always see what he was feeling. That was his gift and his curse. The readable face, the transparent emotion, the man who couldn't hide anything.

"I confess these works," he said. "I justify them. And I submit myself to the judgment of this congregation, this Republic, and those who watch beyond the Interdict."

The silence extended. Ten seconds. Twenty.

Curate Kel-Talith rose slowly from her seat. Her face was unreadable in a way his never was. "Consul Kel el-Marwen," she said. "You have made a Particular Confession."

"Yes."

"The congregation will require time to—"

"I understand."

He stood there, in the electric light, tears drying on his face. The merchant houses would call in their paper by morning. The Ministry of Internal Affairs would summon him within days. The Senate would vote to censure, possibly to expel. His coalition would fragment. His brokerage might survive or might not.

But Sorda was burned. The Lens had lost their asset. Rasma was protected by a lie she would never be able to acknowledge. The merchant houses were exposed, their leverage public, their reputations damaged. Everyone who had a hook in him was bleeding.

And he felt—

He wasn't sure what he felt. The wall in his future, the one with two doors, was still there. But he wasn't looking at it. He was looking at the congregation, at Curate Kel-Talith, at the bare brick walls and the electric lights humming with Republican power.

His face, for once, showed nothing anyone could read.
