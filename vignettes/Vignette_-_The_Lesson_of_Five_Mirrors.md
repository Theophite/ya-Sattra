**The Lesson of Five Mirrors**

The five sisters sat in a perfect pentagon, each twelve years old, each wearing the same expression of polite attention. Their faces were identical—high cheekbones, auburn hair in matching braids, eyes the specific shade of amber that their genome had been selecting for seventeen generations. They were not twins or quintuplets. They were iterations. Sava-1 through Sava-5, though they'd given each other secret names the servants didn't know.

"Today," announced Teacher, "we will discuss the concept of markets."

Teacher was beautiful in the way all their servants were beautiful—symmetrical features optimized for trust, skin the warm brown of antique wood, movements calibrated to never startle. Teacher was also iteration forty-three of her line, though she would never mention this. Her genome existed solely to educate es-Valenti daughters.

The lesson room occupied what had once been a transit hub in the arcology's middle levels. The ceiling soared forty meters overhead, its original purpose lost. Now it held projected light that simulated afternoon sun, though none of the sisters had seen actual sunlight. The walls displayed images of what Teacher called "a typical marketplace," though the images had been painted by servants who'd learned from other servants who'd been told about markets by servants who might have seen one, once.

"In markets," Teacher continued, "different people exchange objects for coins."

Sava-3, who secretly called herself Bright, raised her hand. "Why different people?"

Teacher paused. The question required careful navigation. "In the outside world, people are not iterations of the same genome. They are... random variations."

The sisters exchanged glances. They'd been learning about randomness in their mathematics tutorials—it was something you eliminated through proper breeding protocols.

"How do they coordinate?" Sava-2 (secretly Storm) asked. "If they're all different, how do they know what to do?"

"They negotiate," Teacher said, then saw confusion on five identical faces. "They... discuss until they agree."

"But who wins?" Sava-1 (secretly First, because she was literal) pressed. "When we discuss, we already know we'll reach the same conclusion because we think the same. If they're different..."

Teacher gestured, and Assistant entered. Assistant was iteration twelve of a line designed for visual demonstrations. She was shorter than Teacher, paler, with green eyes instead of brown. The difference was deliberately jarring.

"Imagine," Teacher said, "that I want Assistant's water, and Assistant wants my food."

The sisters watched, fascinated, as Teacher and Assistant performed an elaborate pantomime of exchange. It looked like a dance they didn't know the steps to.

"But why wouldn't Assistant just give you water?" Sava-4 (secretly Questions) interrupted. "She's yours."

Another careful pause. Teacher had been modified for patience—her endocrine system literally couldn't produce frustration. "In the outside world, people are not owned by other people."

This was too much. All five sisters began talking at once, their identical voices creating a strange harmony of confusion. How did anything function? Who decided what needed doing? What happened when someone refused?

Door-Warden entered—massive, silent, from a guardian line that hadn't been updated in three hundred years. His presence meant the lesson was disturbing the es-Valenti Matriarch three floors below. The sisters fell silent. They'd learned early that Mother didn't appreciate disturbance. Mother was studying something important involving patents and politics, concepts as abstract to the sisters as the market they couldn't understand.

"Perhaps," Teacher suggested, "we should visit Lower Gallery."

Lower Gallery was their favorite place—a vast hall where previous iterations of their genome lined the walls in portrait form. Sava-1-through-5 of six generations ago. Sava-1-through-5 of twelve generations ago. Always five, always identical, always together until they turned twenty and were separated to marry into five different Creche families, each carrying identical genetic material to spread the es-Valenti optimization patterns.

But first they had to learn about the world they'd eventually pretend to understand.

As they walked—in perfect synchronization, because they always walked in perfect synchronization—Sava-5 (secretly Echo, because she always spoke last) whispered to her sisters: "Do you think we're random to them?"

They considered this in silence. Somewhere above them, their Mother was reading reports about grain yields and tax revenues, concepts that required understanding millions of random people making uncoordinated decisions. The reports were translated through three layers of servants, each layer simplifying the incomprehensible outside world into patterns a Creche Aureate could process.

"No," Sava-1 finally answered. "We're the opposite of random. That's why we're valuable."

They nodded in perfect unison, five identical heads moving as one. Outside their arcology, ya-Sattra sprawled with its millions of different people, its markets and conflicts and negotiations. But here, in the climate-controlled perfection of the es-Valenti estate, five girls who were really one girl divided into five bodies continued learning about a world they'd never actually see, taught by servants who'd never seen it either, preparing for marriages that would spread their optimized genome to families they'd never meet, in estates just as isolated as their own.

Teacher watched them walk ahead, her modified neurology processing their question about randomness. She'd been designed to find the answer obvious: of course the sisters weren't random to outsiders. They were something much stranger—they were perfect. And perfection, Teacher's engineering understood, was the most alien thing of all.

The lesson continued in Lower Gallery, where five girls studied portraits of themselves from centuries past, learning about a world that existed beyond their walls but might as well have been fiction. Tomorrow they'd study "weather," another concept that made no sense in their controlled environment.

They were being prepared to rule an Empire they'd never see, through marriages to cousins they'd never meet, creating children who would be iterations of iterations, each generation more perfectly isolated from the random world they nominally controlled.
