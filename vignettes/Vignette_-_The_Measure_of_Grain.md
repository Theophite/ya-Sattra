**The Measure of Grain**

Sri Collector Avit es-Drayem watched the grandmother weeping over her ledger book, calculating and recalculating numbers that wouldn't change. Her granddaughter, maybe fourteen, stood silent beside her, understanding what the numbers meant. Their farm's entire harvest lay in the Bureau wagons outside—every grain of it—and it still wasn't enough.

"The census recorded eight working-age adults in this household," Avit said, reviewing his own ledger. "The tax assessment assumes eight adults' productive capacity."

"My sons died in the winter fever," the grandmother said. "Both of them. Their wives took the younger children back to their families. There's only me, Kess here, and my sister who can't walk since the accident. We filed the death notices. We filed everything."

"The death notices reached the provincial office after the census was finalized." Avit made a note. "They'll be reflected in next year's assessment."

"We can't survive until next year without grain."

"That's a separate issue from tax compliance."

The grandmother's hands shook as she turned pages, pointing to careful annotations. "Look—here. Every seed planted. Every handful of fertilizer. We worked eighteen-hour days. Kess missed her studies to work the fields. We grew everything possible with three people, but the assessment—"

"Assumes eight people. Yes." Avit gestured to his Warborn enforcer, Mul, who began loading the household goods onto a second wagon. "Property seizure to make up the shortfall. Standard procedure."

The girl, Kess, finally spoke. "Those are the weaving looms. We need them for winter income."

"The tax debt takes precedence."

"Then we'll starve."

"Again, that's a separate issue."

Mul lifted the larger loom with his good arm—his other ended at the elbow, tumor removal that hadn't quite worked. The old Warborn avoided everyone's eyes. He'd been doing this for seven years, since his body failed too much for regular military service. He knew what happened to families after the looms were taken.

"Please," the grandmother said. "I've paid taxes for forty years. Never missed once. The boys' deaths weren't recorded in time because the recorder was sick, the office was closed, we tried—"

"I could file an appeal on your behalf," Avit offered. "The processing fee is two hundred obols."

"We don't have two hundred obols. You've taken everything."

"You could borrow them. The es-Lahun family offers very reasonable rates for emergency tax loans. Twenty percent monthly, compounded."

The arithmetic was simple. Borrow two hundred obols now, owe two hundred forty next month. Miss that payment, owe two hundred eighty-eight. By spring, the debt would exceed the farm's total value. The es-Lahun family would seize the land, the grandmother and granddaughter would become tenant farmers on what they used to own, paying rent that somehow always equaled what they could grow minus just enough to keep them alive for another planting.

Avit had seen it hundreds of times. It was, in fact, the most common outcome.

"There's another option," he said, watching the girl. Fourteen, healthy despite the lean year, intelligent eyes. "The Bureau always needs clerks. Young women with mathematical aptitude, particularly. A seven-year indenture would clear the tax debt and provide a signing bonus sufficient to see your grandmother through winter."

"She's my only remaining family," the grandmother said quietly.

"Yes, that does increase her value. Fewer complications."

Kess looked between her grandmother and Avit. She understood the real offer. Seven years as a Bureau clerk was the official paperwork. The reality would be whatever the Bureau needed—clerk, certainly, but also everything else young women were useful for in provincial offices where oversight was thin and complaints went nowhere.

"I'll need a day to decide," Kess said.

"The offer expires when I leave. The caravan moves at dawn."

That night, Avit dined with the local village headman, who thanked him for his efficient collection. The headman's own tax had been surprisingly low—the census had somehow missed his three adult sons, recording them as children despite their beards. Amazing what the census missed when you knew which recorder to bribe.

"The Pel family," the headman mentioned casually, referring to the grandmother and granddaughter. "They've been difficult lately. Complaining about the irrigation schedule, questioning the seed distribution. It's good they're learning about proper compliance."

Avit understood. The Pels had challenged the headman's authority, so the headman had ensured their census reflected impossible numbers. Avit was merely the instrument of punishment, collecting what his ledgers demanded.

At dawn, Kess arrived at the caravan with a small bundle. Her grandmother wasn't with her.

"Seven years?" she asked.

"Seven years. Thumb here for the contract."

She pressed her thumb to the blood-lock seal. The contract was in legal script she couldn't read, terms she wouldn't understand until too late. But it would keep her grandmother alive through winter, possibly spring. After that, who knew?

As the caravan pulled away, Avit updated his ledger. Taxes collected in full, plus one clerk indenture valued at three hundred obols (he'd report it as one hundred, pocket the difference). The grandmother would survive on Bureau charity grain—just enough to keep her alive so the land wouldn't go completely fallow. Next year, she'd be taxed for eight adults again unless she paid for a census correction.

She wouldn't be able to pay.

The es-Lahun family would get the land eventually. They always did.

"Sir," Mul said quietly as they rolled toward the next village. "The girl."

"Will serve her indenture like hundreds of others." Avit was already reviewing the next farm's census. Twelve adults recorded, but his informants said three had left for the cities. Perfect. "Your concern is noted but irrelevant."

The Warborn fell silent. He knew what happened to the girls. He also knew what happened to Warborn who objected to Aureate orders. His partial arm ached in the morning cold, reminder of his own expendability.

Three villages later, they encountered a farm where the entire family had disappeared in the night, leaving empty fields and a note carved into their door: "The census lies."

Avit had the door burned, the land marked for es-Lahun acquisition, and the family's names added to the debtor registry. If they surfaced anywhere in the Empire, they'd be arrested for tax evasion. If they didn't surface, they'd probably starved in the wilderness or been taken by slavers.

Either way, the ledger would be balanced.

That evening, while reviewing his collections, Avit calculated his personal take. Forty percent skimmed from reported collections, plus the indenture differentials, plus the bribes from headmen for favorable census reports. A good month. His own estates near the capital grew by two farms per year at this rate.

The cruelty wasn't personal. He held no malice toward the Pel family or any others. They were simply entries in a ledger, numbers to be maximized. The Empire needed taxes. The es-Lahun family needed land. Young women were needed for provincial offices. Everyone got what they needed except the families, but they weren't really part of "everyone" that mattered.

The system worked as designed.

It was only cruel if you were caught in its gears.
