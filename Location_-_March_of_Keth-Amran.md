# The March of Keth-Amran

## Executive Summary

The March of Keth-Amran is the easternmost of the seven marcher territories, occupying what was once Cimarron County and portions of adjacent counties in pre-Interdict New Mexico. Its ruling family, the Mor-Kethran, have held the territory since the Sultanate granted it as a vassal march four centuries ago. The estate sits on Antediluvian ruinsâ€”now little more than geometric depressions in the earth and occasional outcroppings of material that resists weathering. The Sultanate-era manor commands a shallow rise overlooking farmland that extends to every horizon.

The March is home to perhaps eight thousand permanent residents, none of whom are Republican citizens. They are subjects of the Mor-Kethran, bound to the land through arrangements that Ganati Dawn accurately describes as serfdom. The family's Senate representation derives not from these subjects but from pastoralist bands that cross the March seasonally and accept payment to affiliate with the Mor-Kethran kilit. The bands are citizens; the farmers are not. This inversionâ€”foreign nomads holding the franchise while resident cultivators lack itâ€”captures something essential about the marcher economy.

## The Land

Eastern New Mexico is high plains country, dry and wind-scoured, the horizon extending unbroken in every direction. The elevation exceeds four thousand feet. Rainfall is sparse and unpredictable. Without irrigation, the land would support only sparse grazing. With it, the Antediluvian crops produce yields impossible under conventional agriculture.

The crops are the foundation of everything. They were designed before the Interdict, engineered for deployment alongside Serrulata colonists on worlds that would never receive them. The specifications assumed Serrulata labor, Serrulata dietary requirements, Serrulata heat tolerance. When the Interdict stranded the colonist caste on Earth, the crops came with themâ€”seeds in vaults, genetic sequences in databases, agricultural systems waiting for a planet they would never reach.

They have bred true ever since. Four varieties dominate the March's cultivation: a grain that stores indefinitely without processing, a legume that fixes nitrogen aggressively enough to reclaim exhausted soil in a single season, a root vegetable that produces tubers the size of a man's head, and a fibrous plant whose stems yield material somewhere between hemp and cotton. The Antediluvians designed them to be robust, and robust they remain. The Mor-Kethran have cultivated the same genetic lines for four centuries. The crops do not drift. They do not hybridize with local species. They grow where planted, yield what they were designed to yield, and wait for colonists who will never leave Earth.

The irrigation system is Sultanate-era, drawing from aquifers that were ancient when the manor was built. The water is finite. Everyone knows this. No one discusses what happens when it runs out.

## The Estate

The manor house sits on Antediluvian foundations weathered to near-invisibility. Geometric depressions mark where structures once stood. Outcroppings of some materialâ€”not quite stone, not quite metalâ€”resist erosion in ways that make them useful as cornerstones and boundary markers. The Sultanate architects built atop these remnants without understanding them, incorporating Antediluvian elements the way a medieval cathedral might incorporate Roman columns: as convenient material, stripped of original meaning.

The house itself is modest by aristocratic standardsâ€”forty rooms, with outbuildings for storage, equipment, and the Akama mercenary company that provides security. The current Lord Mor-Kethran, Valdis Mor-Kethran the Younger, inherited six years ago when his father died of a respiratory illness common among those who spend time in the eastern territories. The dust gets into everything. The family has lost three members to lung ailments in the past two generations.

Valdis is thirty-four, unmarried, reportedly courting a cousin from the Dhal-Orom march. A Mor-Kethranâ€œDhal-Orom marriage would consolidate two of the seven marcher seats, creating a voting bloc that might actually matter in Senate arithmetic. Whether this calculation drives the courtship or merely accompanies it, no one outside the families can say.

The estate employs perhaps two hundred people directlyâ€”household staff, overseers, specialists who maintain irrigation infrastructure, factors who manage trade. Most are Serrulata, born on the March, citizens of nothing except the Mor-Kethran domain.

## The Serfs

The permanent population consists of agricultural laborers bound to the land through arrangements that vary in legal fiction but share a common reality: they cannot leave.

Some are debt-bound, descendants of families that accepted loans generations ago and have never cleared the principal. Some hold tenancies whose terms require continuous cultivation, forfeiting rights if they abandon the land. Some are simply born into arrangements no one has bothered to formalize, their status inherited from parents whose status was inherited in turn. The Sultanate recognized these arrangements; the Republic, by declining to claim the territory, has never ruled on their legality.

The villages scatter across the March, each serving a cluster of farms, each dominated by an overseer's house and a small Penitent chapel. The faith practiced here is conservative, emphasizing patience and acceptance, the Interdict as just punishment rather than imprisonment to be escaped. Radical works-theology would be dangerous. Material progress as atonement implies that stasis is sin, and stasis is what the March requires.

The serfs are Serrulata. Their ancestors came with the crops, or shortly afterâ€”colonists without a destination, settled on land that needed labor. Their scales show centuries of local adaptation: darker pigmentation against the relentless sun, heat-dissipation patterns different from urban Serrulata. They speak Ganati with an accent that city-dwellers find provincial and marcher aristocrats affect not to notice.

They have no political representation. They belong to no kilit. When the census occurs and the March calculates its Senate allocation, the serfs are not counted.

## The Pastoralists

The Mor-Kethran claim a kilit of approximately nine thousand members, sufficient for a single Senate seat. Perhaps five hundred are permanent residents of the March. The remainder are pastoralistsâ€”Serrulata bands driving herds across the high plains, crossing the March during seasonal migrations, accepting payment to affiliate with the Mor-Kethran kilit for census purposes.

The arrangement is straightforward commerce. The Mor-Kethran pay in trade goods, grazing rights, and access to water sources they control. The pastoralists provide population numbers that translate into Republican representation. Everyone understands the transaction. The Ministry of Information has documented it extensively. Nothing changes, because changing it would require defining where the Republic ends and the marches begin, and no one wants that question answered.

The relationship is not equal. Favored bands receive better termsâ€”premium grazing, priority water access, trade goods at rates that amount to subsidy. Disfavored bands find wells suddenly contested, traditional routes blocked, overseers raising complaints about trespass or crop damage. The Mor-Kethran can make pastoral life considerably harder for bands that displease them.

Recently, displeasure has meant Akama.

## The Mercenaries

The Mor-Kethran retain a company of Akama warriorsâ€”thirty-two men under a captain named Gorin Blackwater who claims descent from the hero of Tsenkher Ford. Whether the claim is accurate matters less than the name's resonance. Akama who hear it know what it means: this captain styles himself a man of violent reputation, and the company follows someone who believes in lineage and honor and the old ways.

The company's official function is estate security. Bandits occasionally trouble the eastern territories, and distance from any Republican garrison makes self-defense necessary. This justification is not entirely false. But the company's actual function has expanded.

When a serf fleesâ€”and serfs do flee, despite the distancesâ€”the Akama track them. When a pastoralist band grazes where the Mor-Kethran have forbidden, the Akama deliver warnings. When warnings fail, the Akama conduct reprisals: scattered herds, burned camps, occasionally bodies left where other bands will find them. None of this reaches the newspapers. The Republic has no jurisdiction. The marcher lords handle their own affairs.

The arrangement suits the Akama. They are paid well, housed comfortably, and permitted to maintain customs the Republic officially discouragesâ€”including the taking of trophies from defeated enemies. Captain Blackwater's tent reportedly contains items that would create complications if Republican authorities ever inspected it. Republican authorities have no authority to inspect.

How the Akama reached the March is not widely known. Khas-Sekh Akkhali, the Republic's foremost scholar on Akama culture, reportedly facilitated introductions between marcher lords seeking retainers and warband remnants seeking employment. The introductions happened through private correspondence. Nothing illegal occurred. Men of violence found men who needed violence, and arrangements were made.

## The Eastern Trade

The March of Keth-Amran is the closest of the seven territories to the Dead Citiesâ€”the ruins of the nine city-states that destroyed each other in the early Sultanate period. The contamination begins perhaps a hundred miles east of the manor house. Travelers report that the dust changes color, takes on a faint luminescence at night, leaves residue that doesn't wash off easily. Radiological counters carried by Republican expeditions registered levels fatal within days of sustained exposure.

But trade goods come from somewhere beyond the dead zone.

The Mor-Kethran receive shipments three or four times per yearâ€”caravans arriving from the northeast, skirting contaminated territories through routes that add hundreds of miles to the journey. The goods include archaeotech components the March could not possibly recover locally, manufactured items with maker's marks in unrecognized scripts, and occasionally materials whose properties defy easy categorization. The Mor-Kethran sell some into Republican markets through factors in High Junction. The rest they keep, or trade to other marcher families, or use for purposes they do not discuss.

The caravans are not Akama. The traders who lead them are near-baseline humans speaking neither Ganati nor Imperial Common. They communicate through a pidgin apparently developed specifically for this tradeâ€”gestures and simplified vocabulary sufficient for commercial transactions. They pay in goods rather than currency. They do not stay long.

Where they come from is speculation. The Dead Cities block direct passage, but the Great Lakes are not so farâ€”perhaps eight hundred miles as the crow flies, twice that by any route avoiding contamination. The Ministry of Internal Affairs believes, based on fragmentary evidence, that settlements along the lakes maintain trade connections across the former eastern seaboard and possibly across the Atlantic. The Pacific is blocked by the Westerners, but the Atlantic apparently is not.

The Mor-Kethran know more than they say. The other marcher families know more than they say. The Ministry cannot compel Senators to testify about commercial activities without triggering constitutional crisis. So the trade continues, caravans arrive and depart, and the Republic's eastern border remains a zone of profitable ignorance.

## The Lord of the March

Valdis Mor-Kethran the Younger is not a complicated man. He inherited an estate, a Senate seat, and problems he did not create. He manages these as his father managed them, and his grandfather before: maintain the crops, keep the serfs in line, ensure the pastoralists affiliate when census time arrives, conduct the eastern trade that makes everything else possible.

His politics are those of his class. He believes in hierarchy, in the natural order of those who rule and those who serve, in the superiority of Serrulata over other castes and of marcher Serrulata over city-dwellers who have forgotten what civilization requires. He reads The Eastern Sentinel and agrees with its editorials. He finds Sun of Sarru vulgarâ€”populism is populism, whatever the ideologyâ€”but shares some of its assumptions about near-baselines.

He attends Dusk caucus meetings when Senate business requires his presence in Ganat proper, which is rarely. He votes as coalition leadership suggests on most matters. His single consistent policy interest is preventing any regulation that might affect marcher autonomy: residency requirements, labor standards, trade documentation, anything giving the Republic leverage over how he manages his territory.

He does not think of himself as a villain. He thinks of himself as a steward, maintaining what his ancestors built, preserving order in a world that would collapse into chaos without men like him. The serfs need direction. The pastoralists need management. The Republic needs the trade goods flowing through his territory. Everyone benefits from the arrangements he maintains. That some benefit more than others is simply the nature of things.

He is, in his own understanding, a moderate. The radicals want to abolish the marches. The dreamers want to reclaim the dead zone. He simply wants to continue as things are, managing his territory, conducting his trade, representing his kilit in a Senate that mostly ignores him except when it needs evidence of Dusk's embarrassing alliances.

He is not wrong that both factions use him this way. He is wrong about most other things.

## What the Republic Knows

The Ministry of Internal Affairs maintains files on all seven marches. The files document vote-padding arrangements, serf populations, Akama mercenaries, anomalous trade goods. Ministry analysts have written reports recommending investigation, regulation, intervention. The reports sit in archives. Nothing happens.

The constitutional problem is intractable. The marcher lords are Senators. Investigating Senators requires either Senate authorizationâ€”which the lords can blockâ€”or emergency executive actionâ€”which requires both Consuls to agree. Rasma Dhal-Setil might authorize investigation if she thought it would damage Dusk. Datho Kel el-Marwen will not authorize anything creating precedent for executive investigation of Senators, because that precedent could be turned against him.

So the Ministry watches, documents, and waits. Agents in High Junction track trade goods flowing from marcher territory. Analysts in Ganat compile reports no one with authority will read. The constitutional structure designed to prevent tyranny also prevents accountability.

Khas-Sekh Akkhali, consulted periodically on Akama matters, provides assessments that are technically accurate and practically useless. The Akama mercenaries in marcher employ are, he notes, not unusual by Thousand Kingdoms standards. Their customs, while perhaps distasteful to Republican sensibilities, fall within normal parameters for warrior bands. If the Ministry wishes to regulate Akama employment, it should consider broader implications for Republican relations with the Thousand Kingdoms, which depend substantially on informal commercial arrangements including mercenary contracts.

He does not mention that he introduced several captains to their current employers. The Ministry does not ask. Everyone maintains the fictions that make the system function.

## What the Republic Does Not Know

The Ministry does not know what comes through the dead zone, or how. Trade routes skirting contamination must pass through territory that should be impassable. Either contamination is less extensive than surveys suggested, or the caravans have found paths through, or something else is happening that the Ministry cannot model.

The Ministry does not know what the marcher lords receive in exchange for goods sent east. The trade appears balancedâ€”value flows both directionsâ€”but specific arrangements are opaque. The lords might be accumulating credits against future needs. They might be paying for services not yet delivered. They might be building relationships that will matter when circumstances change.

The Ministry does not know what the lords discuss when they meet, which they do annually at a rotating location among the seven territories. The meetings are described as social occasionsâ€”family gatherings, shared religious observances, the kind of thing aristocrats do. No Republican observer has attended. No record of proceedings exists.

The Ministry suspects, but cannot confirm, that the lords coordinate Senate votes more than their scattered pattern of affiliation suggests. Seven votes should not matter. But seven votes moving together at crucial moments, combined with whatever leverage the eastern trade provides, might matter more than arithmetic implies.

The Far Northeast remains unknown. The cities on the Great Lakes are rumor. The Atlantic trade network is inference from fragmentary evidence. The marcher lords know something, and they are not sharing.
