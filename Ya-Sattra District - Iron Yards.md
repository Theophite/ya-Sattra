## **Executive Summary**

This document provides comprehensive coverage of the Iron Yards, ya-Sattra's industrial and salvage district occupying a quarried depression between the harbor's grain docks and the elevated Ridge. Approximately 70,000 people work here daily; perhaps 45,000 live in the worker housing that climbs the district's southern fringe. The Yards exist because salvage requires processing, and processing requires space, labor, and careful distance from respectable commerce.

The document covers five domains: the physical district (quarried geography, major zones, access routes), the three major powers (Salvage Guild controlling sales, Pipefitters Union controlling labor, Yen Tam Beneficial Society controlling vice), notable locations (the Salvage Dome, Torchline Row, the dry docks, worker housing), and detailed business profiles with owners and quirks. Approximately thirty-five named individuals appear across these sections.

The document's central concern is the extraction economy and its human cost. The Iron Yards process the salvage that funds ya-Sattra's commerce, but the work destroys bodies—radiation exposure, chemical burns, industrial accidents, the slow degradation that comes from breathing metal dust and coal smoke. Understanding who profits from this destruction, who suffers it, and how they negotiate their competing interests explains most of what happens in the Yards.

## **Geographic Foundation**

The Iron Yards occupy a quarried depression in ya-Sattra's Rotten Quarter, carved from bedrock generations ago to build the arcology remnants that now define the city's skyline. The land itself records extraction—stone removed to construct what later fell, leaving behind a natural basin that industrial necessity has filled with forges, dry docks, and salvage operations.

The district's geography follows a gradient from north to south. The northern edge drops toward the harbor and the Rift, where massive dry docks have been cut even deeper into the stone. **Dry-Dock 3**, the largest, maintains a controlled flood for hull repairs—a permanent pool of oily water reflecting the sparks that fall from the cutting operations above. The slope assists gravity in moving heavy salvage downward toward the harbor, while quarried walls provide natural windbreaks for precision cutting.

The central zone rises slightly, dominated by the **Salvage Guild's dome**—a pre-Interdict structure of unknown original purpose that somehow survived both the arcology collapse and subsequent centuries of industrial use. The dome spans approximately two hundred feet at its base, its bronze curves somehow wrong compared to contemporary construction. Around this administrative anchor, warehouses and salvage yards spread in rough concentric patterns, their arrangement dictated by underground pipe infrastructure that current inhabitants only partially understand. **The Pit**, a circular depression near the dome's eastern side, serves as the decontamination pad where radiation-tagged salvage gets dumped from Lift-3. The ground here has been sealed with archaeotech polymer that nothing grows through.

The southern fringe climbs toward the Ridge, where industrial facilities give way to worker housing. **Forge Terrace** marks this transition—coal forges and cable-splicing shops built into rising stone, their chimneys creating a permanent haze that pools against the Ridge's retaining walls. The quarried terracing allows tenements to climb the cliffside without completely blocking light to lower structures.

The western boundary blurs into ya-Sattra's Middens without clear demarcation—salvage yards becoming scrap markets, warehouses becoming squatter camps, the distinction between worker and resident dissolving into daily survival. The eastern boundary presents the only formal transition: the **Archive Gate**, a massive bronze structure spanning multiple levels where industrial squalor meets the Medina Quarter's commercial respectability. The **Quarantine Shed** sits just inside this boundary, where Bureau of the Lens inspectors examine suspicious salvage before allowing it into regulated markets.

## **The Three Powers**

### **The Salvage Guild**

The Salvage Guild holds a charter granted during the emergency reconstruction following the arcology collapse—a document whose age gives it legal weight that newer institutions cannot challenge. The charter grants monopoly over salvage assessment and sale within ya-Sattra, exempting Guild operations from patent enforcement for "emergency reconstruction purposes." That the emergency ended centuries ago has not diminished the exemption's value.

**The 147 master memberships**, fixed by charter, create an aristocracy within the salvage economy. Masters range from industrial magnates controlling entire material streams to marginal operators who rent floor access to contractors. The wealthiest rarely appear at the Guild dome, operating through representatives while maintaining estates on the Ridge or beyond. Working masters, perhaps sixty of the total, actively manage operations and appear daily.

Membership can be inherited but must be validated through demonstrating "continuous engagement in salvage operations"—a requirement that prevents pure speculation while allowing considerable flexibility. Some masters satisfy it through owning a single cutting torch, operated by contractors who pay for the privilege of working under their license.

The real salvage economy operates through thousands of contractors working under master licenses. These arrangements vary from exploitation—contractors paying 60% of revenues for license use—to genuine partnerships where masters provide capital and political protection. The Guild maintains careful blindness about contractor operations, allowing masters to claim ignorance about patent violations or dangerous practices.

**Guild economics** center on auction fees: 18% of every transaction, plus hidden charges for authentication (2%), floor space (1-3%), security (1%), and "charter maintenance" (2%). Additional fees apply for hazardous materials or discrete sale. The total can reach 30% for questionable archaeotech. The Guild also operates its own credit system—necessary because formal banks won't finance potentially illegal salvage operations—with interest rates reflecting the risk, typically 3% monthly, compounding.

### **The Pipefitters Union**

The Pipefitters Union holds legitimacy predating Imperial memory. Its charter, etched on steel plates so ancient the alloy composition baffles modern metallurgists, grants rights to "all who labor in the conveyance of vital fluids through the body of the City." No one can say definitively when those words were first inscribed, but their weight carries forward—authority older than the Bureaux themselves dare challenge.

In practice, the Union has evolved far beyond its original mandate. What began as a guild of specialized pipe-workers now encompasses nearly every form of industrial labor in the Rotten Quarter that doesn't explicitly fall under another charter. Stevedores who've never touched a wrench carry union cards. Cable-splicers whose work involves no fluids pay their dues. Even salvage-cutters whose only relationship to pipes involves removing them for scrap find shelter under the Pipefitters' expansive interpretation. The Union takes them all, asking only solidarity in return.

**Marlo Glen** leads the Union from a position that shouldn't exist—a near-baseline at the head of an organization whose membership is overwhelmingly Labor Caste. His success rests on understanding that dignity requires no augmentation. When he negotiates with Salvage Guild officials, he brings his lieutenants: **old Crek**, the Ironbone whose joints have begun their final calcification but whose presence still commands respect, and **Lira Three-Stone**, the Springheel woman whose legend grows with each retelling.

The story of Three-Stone has become Union mythology. The old boss, **Havek Otelmar**, had grown comfortable in corruption, taking bribes from the Salvage Guild to suppress wage demands while skimming dues for personal use. During a heated meeting about dock safety—three men had died that week from preventable cable failures—Otelmar made the mistake of calling the Labor Caste workers "meat that thinks it's men." Lira happened to be adjusting the tension on a cargo sling, one of those fist-sized lead weights Springheels use for ballast calculations. The distance was forty meters. The throw was perfect.

The Union elected her to leadership that same night, but she declined, suggesting Marlo Glen instead. A Springheel leading might divide the membership along caste lines. A near-baseline leading with Labor Caste lieutenants sent a different message—that merit and loyalty mattered more than the shape of your bones.

### **The Yen Tam Beneficial Society**

The Yen Tam Beneficial Society operates from a converted warehouse on Torchline Row's upper reaches, their presence announced by ya-Tsatsan wind-chimes and the subtle chemical smell that clings to anyone who enters. They fled ya-Tsatsa fifty years ago after theological disputes within the Testament Societies, establishing themselves first as legitimate insurers before expanding into gambling, loans, and drug distribution. Their organizational structure mirrors ya-Tsatsan clan patterns—extended family networks with clear hierarchies and elaborate gift-exchange obligations.

The Society's ideology mixes criminal pragmatism with genuine near-baseline chauvinism. They believe—sincerely, not just strategically—that near-baseline humans represent the proper form of humanity, that modified castes are useful tools but not true people. This belief manifests in discriminatory pricing: near-baseline customers receive better rates, faster service, more generous loan terms. The gambling rooms maintain separate tables for "pure" and "dilute" players. Drug distribution follows clear hierarchies—modified castes pay more for worse product.

Yet the Society can't afford complete segregation. Too many protection fees come from modified workers, too many loans go to sewer-fisher clans, too many medical supplies get distributed through modified contractors. The result is an organization that practices discrimination while depending on those it discriminates against.

**Criminal operations** interconnect through the warehouse's modified architecture. Gambling rooms connect to drug processing, allowing high-stakes losers to immediately purchase consolation. The loan office maintains direct communication with the enforcement squad in the basement—near-baseline toughs and desperate stevedores waiting for collection assignments. The **Yellow Glass Bathhouse**, under Yen Tam accounting oversight, washes money through inflated maintenance costs.

Their relationship with **Jarek Fen's Iron Yards Boys** remains the most delicate. The Society pays tolls promptly, provides loans to individual enforcers (creating useful leverage), and occasionally shares information about mutual enemies. But they maintain their own enforcement squad specifically to prevent the Hanged Men from directly extorting Society members—a cold war where both organizations test boundaries while avoiding open conflict.

## **Governance**

**The Salvage Guild** controls sales—every legitimate transaction passes through their auction floor, their assessors, their fees. Their monopoly charter gives them legal authority that even the Bureaux hesitate to challenge.

**The Pipefitters Union** controls labor—their charter predates the Bureaux, their membership includes most workers, and their willingness to strike gives them leverage that even the Guild respects. Marlo Glen has used this leverage carefully, avoiding confrontations he can't win while building solidarity that makes the Union impossible to ignore.

**The Yen Tam Beneficial Society** controls vice—gambling, drugs, loans, and the enforcement that makes those businesses possible. Their near-baseline chauvinism limits their membership but not their reach. Workers of all castes borrow from them, gamble in their rooms, buy their products.

**The Iron Yards Boys** operate as the Hanged Men's primary profit center in the district, extracting percentages from salvage operations under Jarek Fen's command. When enforcement requires more muscle than the Boys can provide, **Chain-Men** come down from Bright-Spire—Garren Fen's bone-plated enforcers whose presence alone usually resolves disputes. The relationship with the Middens-based Hanged Men gives them access to this muscle when needed, but the three major powers work to contain their influence, tolerating extraction while preventing expansion.

**Imperial authority** appears mainly at the Archive Gate, where Bureau of the Lens inspectors examine materials entering the Medina Quarter. The **Bureau of the Rod** has nominal jurisdiction over the district's infrastructure but rarely intervenes—the Pipefitters maintain the systems more competently than the Rod could manage. The **Bureau of the Creche** occasionally investigates working conditions but finds that the Guild's emergency charter creates legal ambiguities they'd rather not test.

## **Physical Character**

Walking through ya-Sattra's Iron Yards means breathing metal and coal smoke, hearing the constant percussion of industrial work, feeling the heat that radiates from forges and cutting operations. The air tastes of hot iron and something chemical underneath. The ground vibrates with the rhythm of machinery that never stops.

The visual environment is dominated by the Salvage Dome rising above the central district, its pre-Interdict curves somehow wrong compared to contemporary construction. Around it, warehouses and salvage yards spread in patterns that follow underground infrastructure rather than surface logic. Catwalks and cable-lifts create a secondary street system above ground level, carrying workers and materials between structures without descending to the quarried stone below.

The workers themselves show their labor in their bodies. Stevedores with their compressed frames haul materials too heavy for near-baseline strength. Beamcrawlers maintain the catwalks, their elongated limbs reaching connections that would require scaffolding for others. Springheels work the high positions, their explosive movement carrying them between platforms. Everyone wears protective gear—lead-threaded canvas, ceramic plates, the specialized fabrics from Seru and Sari's shop—and everyone has scars regardless.

The sounds layer: hammer on metal, the hiss of cutting torches, the creak of cable systems under load, voices shouting over machinery, the ever-present background roar of the harbor beyond the northern edge. At shift change, the sounds shift—machinery winding down on one section while starting up on another, the flow of workers moving between Torchline Row and the industrial floor.

Night in the Iron Yards is not quiet. The cutting grid runs around the clock; the dry docks operate on tidal schedules; the Salvage Dome's specialty auctions happen after dark for reasons no one discusses openly. The sparks from welding operations create their own light, reflecting off oily water and metal surfaces. Workers claim you can navigate by spark-glow alone once you know the patterns.

## **The Salvage Dome**

The dome itself, pre-Interdict construction of unknown original purpose, spans approximately two hundred feet at its base. The interior measures roughly 180 feet across with a 70-foot ceiling at the apex. The main floor, reinforced with modern materials, can support massive machinery during auctions. Catwalks added in successive layers over centuries create a vertical hierarchy—masters' offices occupy the uppermost level, junior members work middle tiers, ground-level operations handle physical logistics.

The walls and floor are bronze, showing dark patina worn to bright gold-red in traffic paths. Massive circular skylights cut into the dome's top, forty feet across, are glazed with pre-Interdict material—slightly greenish, very strong. Carved relief covers every surface: geometric patterns, figures in profile conducting transactions, text in script no longer readable.

**The auction floor** operates on three-hour cycles, different material categories scheduled throughout the day. Morning sessions handle bulk metals, afternoons process complex machinery, evenings deal with "specialty items" requiring less scrutiny. Masters bid through coded hand signals visible from the catwalks. Prices follow unwritten rules—no one underbids by more than 15% without expecting retaliation, and certain masters have acknowledged spheres where others don't compete.

In the dome's eastern corner stands an Oracle shrine, a modest installation serving crucial social function. **Curate Varen Taasaren**, who spent thirty years as a Rod inspector before taking orders, maintains the shrine while running a small congregation from the Dockside Hospice. His dual role—religious authority and former Bureau official—makes him an ideal mediator between the Guild and its workforce. The donations he collects nominally support the shrine but practically fund his work with injured Pipefitters, creating an informal welfare system supplementing the Guild's inadequate pension fund.

The northern annexes house specialized facilities: radiation containment for hot materials, chemical processing for hazardous salvage, secure storage for materials under legal review. Underground vaults, accessible only to senior masters, store materials too valuable or dangerous for regular auction—though rumors suggest some vaults haven't been opened in decades, their contents forgotten.

## **Torchline Row**

The limestone ridge that forms Torchline Row was carved out millennia ago, its stone fed into arcology construction when such projects still seemed possible. Now the quarried terraces serve as foundation for the district's commercial strip, elevated just enough above the harbor's industrial sprawl to catch what passes for fresh air while remaining close enough for workers to stumble home after shifts. The constant rain of welding sparks from the cutting operations has given the ridge its name—fire-resistant construction is survival requirement, not aesthetic choice.

The businesses exist in careful equilibrium between legitimacy and necessity. Each maintains calibrated relationships with competing powers: the Salvage Guild's bureaucratic machinery, the Pipefitters' labor solidarity, the Yen Tam Society's mutual aid networks, and the Iron Yards Boys' protection rackets. Most proprietors pay multiple fees to multiple authorities, understanding redundancy as operating cost.

### **Den Vesh Salvage Assessment**

**Den Vesh** occupies a reinforced metal structure designed to repel both radiation and curiosity. The elderly proprietor lost his sight to radiation cataracts years ago—a common occupational hazard among salvage cutters—but claims he can still perceive the Cherenkov glow from hot materials. This might be delusion or might be some quirk of his particular degeneration.

What's certain is his value to a specific clientele. Den Vesh assesses materials too dangerous for Guild certification—radiation-tagged salvage that would trigger quarantine procedures if examined through official channels. His assessments carry weight precisely because he's willing to handle what others won't touch, and his prices reflect the risk premium this entails. He pays the Yen Tam Society for protection, the Union for labor when he needs transport, and the Iron Yards Boys to ensure neither organization decides he's more valuable as an example than as a revenue stream.

### **Seru and Sari Protective Tailoring**

**Seru and Sari** operate from a compact brick building, fifteen feet by twenty feet, two stories, with lead-lined walls because they work with radiation-tagged materials. They're twin sisters—near-baseline with enhanced color vision that lets them distinguish between subtly different fabric treatments. Their fingers move with identical precision across workbenches worn smooth by decades of use.

They produce protective garments: lead-thread aprons, radiation-resistant coveralls, spark-protective masks with mica lenses. Every salvage worker who wants to survive their occupation owns something from this shop. The sisters maintain neutrality by providing identical quality to all customers regardless of faction—a Chain-Man's apron is sewn with the same care as a Pipefitter's. They're forty-seven, unmarried, and have refused every offer to expand or relocate. The shop is exactly the size they can manage together, and managing together is the point.

### **Keth Varemgiren's Operations**

**Keth Varemgiren** maintains two adjacent establishments that together form an unofficial medical facility for those who can't access legitimate healthcare.

**The Cask and Flux** stocks chemical compounds in careful ratios that skirt legality—painkillers diluted just enough to avoid narcotic classification, radiation ointments that might be industrial sealant repackaged for biological use, stimulants that keep workers functional through double shifts. The shop smells of alcohol and something sharper underneath, and the shelves are organized by a system only Varemgiren understands.

**The Coil-Bone Clinic** next door handles mechanical medicine: setting fractures with improvised spring-loaded braces, maintaining the respirators that keep workers breathing in toxic environments, replacing damaged joints with salvaged mechanical components that almost match human articulation. Varemgiren claims Companion Guild training, which might explain his understanding of the interaction between biological and mechanical systems, though no Guild records confirm his membership.

His real credential is pragmatic competence—workers who visit his clinic generally return to work. He's sixty-one, near-baseline, and has operated in the Iron Yards for thirty years. His assistant **Mora** is a young orevet whose degrading vision is already too far gone for precision work but who can still identify infection by the heat signatures wounds produce.

### **Venn Rigging and Rope**

The Venn ropeworks occupies a former textile factory on Torchline Row, its machinery repurposed for cable production. The building's height allows for hundred-meter vertical test drops, essential for quality verification. The work floors maintain specific humidity through water sprays, as fiber tension varies with moisture content. The smell of tar, used for waterproofing, permeates everything.

Rope is critical infrastructure in a district where vertical transportation depends on manually operated pulley systems and emergency bridges might be nothing more than cable spans. The Venns produce everything from delicate surgical sutures to massive dock cables, each product incorporating mathematical patterns that optimize strength beyond what materials alone would suggest.

The Venns are sewer-fishers—one of the six clans operating from the Middens—but their ropeworks sits in the Iron Yards because this is where their customers are. **Keras Venn**, master ropemaker at forty-five, oversees the shop's operations with the meticulous precision his caste brings to technical work. He maintains the clan's technical library of pre-Interdict rigging manuals and personally tests every splice that leaves the premises. His small frame and compact features mark him immediately as something other than baseline, but customers who care about such things learn quickly that Venn rope doesn't fail.

The front-of-house work falls to fosterlings. **Dek and Rol Venn**, twin brothers taken in after their parents died in a granary collapse, handle customer interactions that require baseline appearance. They're near-baseline, unremarkable, the kind of faces that don't raise questions. This arrangement suits everyone—customers uncomfortable dealing directly with sewer-fishers can pretend the shop is baseline-run, while the actual technical work happens in the back where Keras and his cousins do what no baseline ropemaker can match.

The clan maintains political neutrality through technical indispensability. Their testing apparatus—a reinforced ceiling hatch through which they load-test every splice—provides empirical proof of their work's reliability. When gantries fail or bridges snap, investigations never trace back to Venn cable. This reputation grants unusual freedom from factional pressure, though they still tithe a modest percentage to the Salvage Guild as insurance.

**Patriarch Eldin Venn**, sixty-four, rarely appears at the Torchline shop—he manages the clan's broader operations from their distributed residence in the Middens. His nephew **Kell Venn** handles Iron Yards deliveries, maintaining relationships with foremen and shift supervisors throughout the district. Kell's willingness to enter spaces other sewer-fishers avoid makes him valuable; his habit of leaving staging coils in a mess makes him a perpetual source of minor irritation.

The clan's sister operation, **Venn Rigging Supply** on the Ridge, is run by **Allis Venn**—Eldin's sister. The family has positioned itself across both districts, understanding that infrastructure doesn't respect administrative boundaries.

### **The Yellow Glass Bathhouse**

The **Yellow Glass Bathhouse** occupies a converted forge building, forty feet by fifty feet, with soot-stained brick walls and yellow-tinted glass windows salvaged from somewhere no one remembers. The yellow tint is slight—more amber or pale gold—but distinctive enough to give the establishment its name.

Inside: communal baths heated by coal-fired boilers, sleeping areas with wooden platforms curtained by canvas, decontamination facilities for radiation exposure. The building exhales moisture constantly. Windows fog, water condenses and drips. The smell is carbolic disinfectant, coal smoke, wet wool, human bodies.

The bathhouse serves workers cleaning up after shifts, laborers too poor for permanent housing (forty obols per night), and people exposed to radiation needing decontamination. Under Yen Tam accounting oversight, it also washes money through inflated maintenance costs. The staff knows not to ask questions about customers who pay in unusual denominations or stay longer than the posted rates should allow.

### **The Yen Tam Seven Branches**

The **Seven Branches** gambling den occupies a converted forge building, fifty feet by sixty feet. The ceiling still shows soot staining from decades of forge operation—black, permanent, creating dark atmosphere. The shrine along one wall features seven brass plates inscribed with Jargon arranged in an open octagon, the eighth point a mirror reflecting the room.

The gambling follows ya-Tsatsan style: seven-sided dice, cards following Testament suits, betting structures involving seven-fold branching. This isn't rowdy gambling—the games are religious observance disguised as (or simultaneous with) gambling. Players sit at timber tables, study their dice or cards, place bets carefully. The dealers—Yen Tam family members—watch, record, maintain game flow. The atmosphere is serious, quiet. Oil lamps create pools of amber light while corners stay dark.

### **Other Torchline Establishments**

**The Burned Hand** serves as the district's primary tavern—a sprawling establishment occupying three connected buildings with multiple entrances, useful for those who prefer not to be seen entering or leaving together. The name comes from the forge-burned handprint pressed into the bronze above the main door, allegedly left by a worker who couldn't afford treatment and used the building's wall to steady himself. **Kella Varn** runs the establishment, a near-baseline woman who lost her left arm to a cutting accident and now manages the most complicated social space in the Iron Yards.

**The Char** serves food to workers who can't afford the Burned Hand's prices—a cramped kitchen where **Grandmother Shen** produces rice porridge, pickled vegetables, and whatever protein she found cheap at market. She's been cooking in the Iron Yards for forty years and knows the rhythms of the district better than most—which crews are flush, which are desperate, which foremen can be trusted.

**Morrow's Tools** sells and repairs the hand equipment that salvage work requires—cutting torches, pry bars, the specialized instruments for working with different materials. **Cass Morrow** inherited the shop from her father and has expanded into rental equipment for workers who can't afford purchases. Her credit terms are more generous than the Yen Tams', which creates periodic tension.

**The Pipe and Gauge** is a Pipefitters bar, technically open to anyone but functionally a Union hall. Union business happens in the back room; the front serves drinks to members and their families. **Steward Kollis** manages the establishment and serves as informal liaison between Union leadership and rank-and-file workers.

**Kella's Bunkhouse** provides temporary housing on Torchline Row's lower reaches—a converted warehouse with rope-frame bunks stacked three high. **Kella Marsh** (different Kella than the Burned Hand's proprietor) runs the operation with minimal staff, charging fifteen obols per night for a space to sleep and access to the communal washroom. The bunkhouse caters to transient workers, those between permanent housing, and those whose circumstances don't bear close examination. They maintain basic decontamination facilities—not enough to prevent long-term exposure effects but sufficient to reduce immediate radiation burns.

The bunkhouse operates under informal Pipefitters protection, based on the understanding that mobile labor requires temporary housing. Workers who can't find shelter can't show up for shifts. In exchange, Kella reports to the Union any unusual activity—strangers asking questions, workers disappearing unexpectedly, signs of expansion into her territory.

## **The Northern Docks**

The Iron Yards' northern edge drops toward ya-Sattra's harbor in a series of quarried steps, each level dedicated to different scales of salvage work. The largest operations occupy the dry docks cut into the lowest level, where controlled flooding allows work on ship hulls and the massive structural components too large for the Salvage Dome's auction floor.

### **Dry-Dock 3**

**Dry-Dock 3** is the largest facility in the Iron Yards, a controlled flood basin capable of receiving vessels up to two hundred meters. The dock itself is pre-Interdict construction, its flood mechanisms still functioning through principles the current operators don't fully understand. **Dockmaster Pell Pelesar** manages operations—a near-baseline woman in her sixties who inherited the position from her father and runs the facility with three generations of accumulated knowledge about its quirks.

The permanent pool of oily water reflects the sparks that fall from the cutting grid above, creating an industrial aurora that workers claim you can see from the Ridge on clear nights. The smell is salt water, tar, dead fish, industrial runoff, and hot metal. The sound is constant—hammering, cutting, the creak of crane cables, voices shouting over machinery.

### **The Cutting Grid**

The **cutting grid** spreads across the quarry floor north of the Salvage Dome, a maze of designated work areas where salvage gets broken down for assessment and sale. Each area is marked by painted boundaries—colors faded but still visible—indicating which material categories belong where. Radiation-tagged salvage goes to the yellow sections, closest to the Pit. Complex machinery goes to the blue sections where precision cutters can work without contamination. Bulk metal goes anywhere there's space.

The grid operates on shift schedules coordinated between the Guild and the Union. Morning shifts handle bulk work—breaking down hulls, separating materials, sorting by category. Afternoon shifts handle precision work—extracting components, preserving mechanisms, the careful cutting that determines whether salvage sells as scrap or as functional archaeotech. Evening shifts handle specialty items that benefit from reduced visibility.

## **Worker Housing**

The Iron Yards' southern fringe climbs toward the Ridge in a jumble of repurposed structures and improvised construction. This is where the district's workforce lives—those who haven't fallen as far as the Middens but can't afford the Ridge's relative stability.

### **Forge Terrace**

**Forge Terrace** marks the transition from industrial to residential, a terrace of coal forges and cable-splicing shops built into the rising stone. The chimneys create a permanent haze that pools against the Ridge's retaining walls, giving the air a metallic taste that residents claim you stop noticing after a few months.

The terrace houses approximately 4,000 people in structures that blur the line between workshop and home. Many families live above or behind their forges, the industrial heat serving as winter warmth. **Master Smith Deren Koll** operates the largest forge, employing twelve workers and housing most of them in an attached dormitory. He's been working metal in the Iron Yards for thirty years and serves as informal arbitrator for disputes on the terrace.

### **Breakspar Loft**

**Breakspar Loft** occupies a stack of shipping containers welded to the cliff face, connected by catwalks to neighboring structures and by cable-lift to Torchline Row below. The name comes from the broken spar—a piece of ship's mast—that serves as the main structural support, driven into the stone at an angle and guyed with cables to the Ridge above.

The containers show original paint—faded red or blue, now rust-red or grey-blue—with external stairs of welded steel plate and tube, walkways of steel grating connecting containers on the same level, windows cut rough into container walls and edged with welded angle iron. Everything is rusting. Orange-rust, brown-rust, rust patterns spreading like lichen. Some containers show through-rust patched with welded plate.

Approximately 1,000 people live in Breakspar's forty-odd container units, most of them single workers or small families. **Boss Hella Vordir** manages the building, collecting rent, maintaining the cable-lift, and mediating disputes. She's a former stevedore whose joints gave out at forty-five, leaving her unable to work the docks but perfectly capable of running a residential operation.

### **Torchman's Rest**

**Torchman's Rest** is a vertical neighborhood of salvaged modular units, originally designed for rapid deployment housing and now serving their intended purpose in an unintended location. The units are stacked six high in places, connected by external stairs and rope bridges, the whole structure creaking alarmingly in high winds but having survived three decades without collapse.

The Rest houses approximately 4,500 people, mostly families. **Elder Cass Kelir** serves as the neighborhood's informal leader—a near-baseline man in his seventies who worked the cutting grid until his lungs gave out and now spends his days settling arguments and advising younger residents. His authority derives from having lived in the Rest longer than anyone else and from knowing everyone's business without ever using that knowledge maliciously.

## **The Archive Gate**

The **Archive Gate** marks the eastern boundary of the Iron Yards, where industrial squalor meets the Medina Quarter's commercial respectability. The gate itself is a massive bronze structure spanning multiple levels, its original purpose lost but its current function clear: everything that passes through gets examined, documented, and taxed. The name comes from the Record Archive visible just beyond—one of the Bureau of the Coin's documentation facilities.

### **The Quarantine Shed**

The **Quarantine Shed** sits just inside the gate, where Bureau of the Lens inspectors examine suspicious salvage before allowing it into regulated markets. The facility is a long, low building with reinforced walls and dedicated decontamination equipment. **Inspector Drell Cassin** runs the operation with a staff of six, examining materials flagged by Guild assessors or reported by informants.

Cassin is near-baseline, fifty-three, and has worked the Quarantine Shed for fifteen years. He's developed a reputation for being bribable within limits—he'll overlook paperwork irregularities for appropriate consideration but won't pass materials he genuinely believes are dangerous. This discrimination makes him valuable to the Salvage Guild, who can rely on his assessments having meaning.

### **The Boundary Economy**

The Archive Gate creates a natural bottleneck that spawns its own economy. Licensed porters carry materials through the gate for customers who don't want to deal with inspection themselves. Document preparers ensure paperwork meets Medina Quarter standards. Money changers convert the Iron Yards' informal currencies into the obols that Medina merchants expect.

**Broker Tess Mellish** operates from a small office just inside the gate, arranging sales between Iron Yards salvagers and Medina Quarter merchants who won't enter the industrial district themselves. She takes 8% of every transaction—less than the Guild charges but requiring her to handle all the logistics. Her success depends on maintaining relationships on both sides of the gate, which requires careful neutrality and a reputation for honest dealing.

## **Economic Foundation**

The Iron Yards' economy in ya-Sattra centers on extraction—taking salvage from the Rift and the fallen arcologies, processing it into usable materials, and selling it through the Guild's auction system. This work generates substantial wealth, almost none of which reaches the workers who perform it.

**Salvage flow** follows predictable patterns. Material arrives from the Rift via the harbor, from the Middens via Lift-3, and from independent salvagers who work the margins of collapsed structures. The Salvage Guild assesses everything, taking fees at every stage. Contractors—working under master licenses—perform the actual cutting and processing. Finished materials flow east through the Archive Gate to Medina merchants or ship out through the harbor to other Imperial cities.

**Labor economics** trap workers in cycles of debt. The Pipefitters Union negotiates wages, but wages barely cover living expenses in a district where everything costs more than it should. Workers borrow from the Yen Tams to cover shortfalls, then work extra shifts to service the loans, damaging their bodies faster and requiring more medical care, which requires more borrowing. The cycle continues until workers are too broken to continue—at which point they fall to the Middens, replaced by the next generation of desperate laborers.

**The Guild's credit system** compounds the trap. Masters can borrow against future sales; contractors cannot. Workers who want to buy equipment must finance through the Yen Tams or the Guild's informal loan sharks. The interest rates ensure that even successful salvagers rarely accumulate capital. The few who manage to save enough for Guild membership find that memberships rarely come available—the 147 masters hold onto their positions, passing them to family members or selling them at prices that require borrowing to afford.

## **The Iron Yards' Purpose**

The Iron Yards exist in ya-Sattra because salvage requires processing, and processing requires accepting costs that respectable districts refuse. The Medina Quarter won't tolerate forge smoke; the Ridge won't accept radiation-tagged materials; the Inner City pretends the industrial economy doesn't exist. The Yards take what other districts reject, turning wreckage into wealth while destroying the workers who make the transformation possible.

For the Salvage Guild, this arrangement generates reliable profit. For the Pipefitters Union, it provides leverage—the threat of strikes matters because the work matters. For the Yen Tam Society, it creates customers—desperate workers need loans, drugs, and the small pleasures that make brutal labor endurable.

For the workers themselves, the Yards offer employment when alternatives are worse. A stevedore on the docks earns enough to avoid the Middens' absolute poverty. A cutter on the grid can support a family in Torchman's Rest. The work destroys bodies slowly rather than quickly, which counts as opportunity in a world where many have no opportunity at all.

The Iron Yards will persist as long as the salvage flows and the city needs what the salvage produces. The workers will continue breaking themselves against industrial necessity. The three powers will continue dividing the profits. And the smoke from Forge Terrace will continue drifting up toward the Ridge, a reminder that ya-Sattra's prosperity rests on extraction, and extraction has costs that someone must pay.
