## **Executive Summary: The Knot and the Hanged Men**

This document details the power structure and history of the Hanged Men, a criminal organization controlling water infrastructure in the Middens district of ya-Sattra. The organization evolved from revolutionary war veterans into a hereditary protection racket over three centuries.

## **The Knot: Seven Seats of Power**

### **1\. The Chairman: Luka Fen**

**Age:** 42  
 **Territory:** The Gallows proper, overall Hanged Men authority  
 **Bloodline:** Seventh-generation direct descent from Garren Fen I

At 2.40 meters, Luka's height comes from Springheel elongation rather than simple scaling—he’s double-jointed in every direction, creating proportions that seem wrong until you see him move. His face abandons baseline humanity entirely: exterior ears absent, nose reduced to flattened vents, mouth a lipless seam that barely moves when speaking. Pale Warborn nodules ridge his brow and dot his jaw corners like partially erupted ivory, while his oversized knuckles bear matching bone tubercles. His black waxed-canvas coat bears dozens of brass and ceramic buttons as trim, each taken from a defeated rival, and the circular Knot badge at his throat depicts an inverted hanging figure with arms spread wide.

Luka occupies the seat through hereditary succession but maintains it through careful balance. His Springheel-dominant genetics grant him the physical capability to outmaneuver challenges from Garren, while his genuine Penitent faith provides moral legitimacy that older Valvehands respect. He understands infrastructure through both practical knowledge and inherited wisdom, making him irreplaceable to those who grasp how fragile the Middens' water system truly is. His morning inspections with Riva aren't performance but actual technical work. His protection of Curate Aram isn't sentiment but calculation about martyrdom's consequences.

### **2\. The Iron Yards Commander: Jarek Fen**

**Age:** 39  
 **Territory:** Iron Yards salvage operations, industrial extortion  
 **Bloodline:** First cousin to Luka, competing branch of the main line

Standing 2.50 meters tall but lean as a drawn blade, Jarek's frame suggests a weapon stretched on a forge until only function remains. His face is even more inhuman than Luka's—the nose merely a flat ridge with minimal vents, ears just depressions in the skull, mouth a lipless slash that forces words through clenched teeth. Chain-link scarification runs from cheekbone to jaw in raised black ridges, each representing a year of Iron Yards command. His functional leather clothing has been treated with industrial sealants until it's essentially armor, while the polished iron rod he carries serves as pointer, baton, and weapon depending on need.

Jarek controls the Hanged Men's primary revenue stream through systematic extraction from salvage operations. His bottomless cynicism means he sees all complexity as fraud, all expertise as gatekeeping, all limits as weakness. He can eat dinner while Chain-Men pull fingers off debtors because suffering is just noise unless it affects revenue. His lean Springheel-Warborn hybrid build makes him dangerously quick but not stronger than Luka. He relies on Garren's violence while positioning himself as the rational alternative to both Luka's restraint and Garren's chaos.

### **3\. The Chain-Men Commander: Garren Fen XI**

**Age:** 37  
**Territory:** Bright-Spire, enforcement operations  
**Bloodline:** Warborn-Dominant, second cousin to Luka

At 2.85 meters and 160 kilograms, Garren represents the Ironbone variant's terminal expression—overlapping pale bone plates cover his brow, cheeks, jaw, shoulders, forearms, and knuckles like natural armor grown wrong, ridging outward in shell-like scales. His face has abandoned human proportionsentirely: no ears at all, just smooth bone; nose reduced to permanently flared ventilation slits; mouth locked half-open by his oversized jaw. His skin between the plates has the texture and color of tarnished pewter, cross-hatched with fight scars, while the chain-link tattoos from cheek to jaw incorporate actual metal fragments beneath the skin.

Garren represents the terminal generation of Ironbone breeding—2.85 meters of bone-plated violence that will be dead from cancer by forty. He experiences his Warborn rage as divine inspiration rather than neurological dysfunction, believing his violent impulses are wisdom rather than misfiring ganglia. He's killed three wives. The Knot’s covered it up. His Chain-Men follow him because he's the biggest monster in a band of monsters. Jarek points him at problems, and Garren breaks them, unable to conceive of solutions that don't involve breaking.

### **4\. The Lockworks Captain: Riva Fen-Cho**

**Age:** 36  
 **Territory:** Water infrastructure, technical operations  
 **Bloodline:** Half-Fen, half-sewer-fisher, probably infertile

At 1.97 meters, Riva appears stretched and compressed simultaneously—elongated limbs attached to a torso that seems perpetually folded in on itself, creating the impression of someone permanently navigating a space too small for them. Her graphite-grey skin carries a petroleum sheen that causes water and machine oil to bead and roll off, the coloration extending uniformly across her entire body including lips and nail beds. Her eyes are full black sclera with gun-metal grey limbal rings that catch light like reflective tape, while her blade-long ears sweep tight against her skull, pierced with ascending rows of copper rings which chime subtly when she turns her head quickly.

Riva exists as living proof that biology doesn't determine destiny while being entirely constrained by her biology. Her unique hybrid genetics—Springheel height with sewer-fisher compression ability—make her irreplaceable for maintaining infrastructure that requires both reach and the ability to squeeze through child-sized passages. Her arthritis worsens daily, adding urgency to the fact that she has no successor. She's loyal to Luka alone because he's the only Knot member who understands that when she fails, everything fails. The others see her as a useful anomaly; he sees her as the lynchpin.

### **5\. The Master of Counting House: Ygren Fen**

**Age:** 65  
 **Territory:** Financial operations, tribute collection oversight  
 **Bloodline:** Springheel/Ironbone line, distant cousin

At 2.05 meters, Ygren shows the softening of age overlaying a frame built for loading cargo—broad shoulders now supporting accumulated weight, powerful hands adapted to turning ledger pages through fingerless gloves. His face carries diluted Fen modifications: nose flattened to vents but not as severely as Luka's, small ears reduced but still present, fine Warborn nodules along his brow subtle enough to pass for unusual bone structure in poor light. His drab ledgersman's coat and brass abacus bracelet project deliberate respectability, though the slight limp from an old chain-break fracture and the lead-weighted cosh hidden in his coat hem hint at his enforcer past.

Ygren operates as the Knot's institutional memory and accountant. His son Derren's rise in the Salvage Guild represents his only hope for legitimacy, driving his conservative position in Knot politics. He'll support whoever maintains stability because instability threatens his son's advancement. He drinks alone at night, reconciling what he does against the revenue which buys his son’s escape.

### **6\. The Night Watch Captain: Sera Fen-Galen**

**Age:** 31  
 **Jurisdiction:** Intelligence, surveillance, information control  
 **Bloodline:** No Fen blood—married to Ruk Fen (Luka's younger brother)

Sera's baseline humanity makes her alien among the Knot—at 1.78 meters and 62 kilograms, she's unremarkably human in a council of genetic extremes, her athletic build coming from climbing the Middens' vertical geography rather than engineered advantages. Her face achieves perfect forgettability: pleasant but not beautiful, distinctive but not memorable, the kind of features that witnesses struggle to describe afterward. Only her pale blueirises with faint reflective rings suggest variance. Her charcoal coat conceals hidden pockets for two Lectors while the silver cipher-wheel bracelet that appears decorative actually serves as her Lens-issued encoding device, its rings rotating throughout the day as she updates encryption keys.

Sera is the only baseline human on the Knot, positioned through marriage rather than blood. As the Galen family's connection to the Hanged Men and the Lens Bureau's Asset LS-4-113, she operates as a triple agent whose primary loyalty is to continued operation. She receives the weekly Gray Sheet briefings from the Lens, filtering intelligence to serve multiple agendas simultaneously. Her lack of genetic stake in Fen succession makes her paradoxically trustworthy—she'll betray everyone equally based on rational calculation rather than family loyalty. She takes contraceptives from three suppliers to avoid the pregnancy that would compromise her mobility or create leverage others could exploit.

### **7\. The Empty Seat**

**Former Territory:** Unknown/Disputed  
 **Vacancy Duration:** Twelve years

The seventh seat has remained empty since Korvain Fen died in a "construction accident" that everyone knows was murder but no one can prove. He'd controlled gambling and prostitution operations in the southern tenements, territories that were quickly absorbed by Jarek's Iron Yards operations and various Valvehand crews.

The seat remains vacant because filling it would require consensus the Knot cannot achieve. Luka wants someone who'd balance Jarek's aggression. Jarek wants an ally to tip votes his way. Garren doesn't understand why they need seven when six works fine. The other captains prefer the current ambiguity to any specific resolution.

The empty seat has become a useful fiction. It's a position to promise potential allies, a threat to existing captains (anyone could be replaced by filling the seventh seat and removing them), and a convenient excuse for deadlock ("we can't make such a major decision without a full Knot"). Some younger Fens campaign for it, not understanding that the campaigning itself disqualifies them—anyone who wants it that obviously would disrupt the careful balance that keeps the Knot functional.

## **The Hanged Men During the Malpais War: From Conscripts to Revolutionaries**

### **The Conscription and Battle**

During the war against the Child of the Malpais, the Empire faced a crisis that forced abandonment of traditional military doctrine. The Child—that failed experiment combining Highborn intelligence with Warborn strength, exiled to the Thousand Kingdoms and presumed dead—had returned with revolutionary military innovations that shattered Imperial forces repeatedly. In desperation, the Empire issued emergency conscription orders that pulled in every able-bodied human, including the mixed-caste laborers of the Middens.

The original Garren Fen commanded a unit composed primarily of Ironback dock workers and Springheel scouts—men whose genetic modifications made them useful for specific military roles but who'd never been considered soldier material. These conscripts were assigned to hold the outer defensive arrays of the City of Glass, essentially sacrificial positions expected to buy time while regular Imperial forces regrouped.

Instead, they held for three days. The mixed unit's caste advantages—Ironbacks anchoring positions with their natural bone armor, Springheels conducting devastating harassment raids with their jumping ability—proved unexpectedly effective against the Child's tactics. They were part of the desperate defense that lasted long enough for the sewer-fishers to activate something within the City of Glass itself, that mysterious intervention that turned the tide.

### **The Refusal to Disarm**

When the Child's forces finally withdrew and the war ended, Imperial command issued standard demobilization orders. Return weapons, return to previous status, pretend the emergency conscription never happened. Garren Fen and his veterans understood what this meant—they'd proven that mixed-caste units could be militarily effective, that armed laborers could defeat enemies the Empire's Warborn couldn't handle alone. They would be seen not as heroes but as threats.

The refusal wasn't dramatic. Fen's unit simply didn't report to the demobilization points. They kept their weapons—mostly crude rifles the Mutterers had mass-produced, nothing sophisticated but proven effective. When military police arrived to enforce the order, they found several hundred armed veterans who'd held against the Child's forces and weren't interested in surrendering to bureaucrats.

### **The Brief Revolt**

The "revolt" lasted less than a week and involved no actual combat. The veterans fortified positions in the industrial district, made clear they weren't surrendering their arms, and waited. The Empire, exhausted from the war, faced a choice: fight the soldiers who'd just helped save the City of Glass, or negotiate. The City-of-Glass Charter (or Compact) that emerged from these negotiations represented a compromise—the veterans could keep their weapons and form local militias, but Warborn production would be strictly limited, weapons manufacture regulated, and certain restrictions placed on those with Warborn genetics.

### **Return to the Middens**

The Fen veterans returned to the Middens as heroes to the local population but threats to the Bureau of the Creche. During the war, the Creche's enforcement had lapsed. Genetic registration hadn't been updated. Unregistered variants had armed themselves and proven their worth. The Creche saw a narrow window to reassert control before these precedents solidified.

The pogrom began within days of the veterans' return. Creche Warborn—specialized for urban pacification rather than battlefield combat—deployed to conduct "registration enforcement." They began hanging residents from bridge cables, the traditional Creche method of public intimidation. The bodies hung inverted, visible from every tenement window, a reminder of what happened to genetic undesirables who forgot their place.

### **The Eighteen-Hour Resistance**

The returning veterans responded with military efficiency. They distributed themselves throughout the district's vertical geography, established defensive positions, and when the Creche forces entered in strength, they fought back with the weapons they'd refused to surrender. The Creche Warborn, trained for controlling unarmed populations, found themselves in running firefights against veterans who'd just fought the Child of the Malpais and won.

The resistance lasted eighteen hours. The veterans used every tactic learned in the war—overlapping fields of fire, harassment and withdrawal, exploitation of vertical terrain. When the Creche finally withdrew, they left behind their collaboration with Rod Bureau overseers who'd manipulated water supplies to flush out resistance positions. The veterans executed these overseers on the same bridges where residents had been hung, then seized the Lockworks administrative building.

### **Taking the Name**

The name "Hanged Men" was adopted not in celebration but in memory. The veterans cut down the bodies of residents the Creche had hung, documented their names, and took the inverted hanging figure as their symbol. They would be the Hanged Men—those who survived where others hadn't, who turned an image of oppression into one of resistance.

The building they seized became known as the Gallows, and from this headquarters, they established themselves as the district's new authority. They'd defended the Middens when the Bureaus couldn't or wouldn't. They controlled the water infrastructure that kept the district alive. They had weapons and the proven will to use them.

### **The Transformation**

Initially, the Hanged Men operated as genuine community defenders. They'd fought for the Empire, been betrayed by it, and defended their neighbors from the Creche. They collected contributions rather than taxes, maintained infrastructure rather than exploiting it, provided actual security services rather than protection rackets.

But three centuries is a long time. Each generation inherited a little less revolutionary legitimacy, a little more institutional power. The descendants of heroes became hereditary rulers. The community defenders became extractive parasites. The weapons kept to prevent another pogrom became tools of oppression against the very population they'd once protected.

The current Hanged Men invoke their origins when convenient—Luka still references the "militia origins" when justifying their authority. But they've become exactly what their founders fought against: an oppressive force extracting tribute from a marginalized population, maintaining power through violence and infrastructure control, different from the Creche only in that they emerged from the community rather than being imposed upon it.

## **Crews of the Hanged Men: The Valvehands**

### **The Dual Nature of the Band**

The Valvehands serve as the Hanged Men's most essential band, combining technical infrastructure work with the daily business of extraction. They're the ones who knock on doors at dawn demanding water fees, who maintain the pumps that justify those fees, who know every family's payment history and every pipe's repair schedule. This dual role—part technician, part collector—makes them the face of the Hanged Men that most residents actually encounter.

The band skews older, typically thirty-five to fifty, because the work requires both technical knowledge and social intelligence that younger enforcers lack. You need to know when a family genuinely can't pay versus when they're holding out. You need to understand which infrastructure complaints are real emergencies versus which are stalling tactics. You need enough physical presence to be taken seriously but enough restraint to avoid triggering the kind of desperate violence that comes from pushing people with nothing left to lose.

### **Composition and Hierarchy**

Perhaps half the Valvehands are some variety of Fen—distant cousins, married-in relations, bastard children acknowledged just enough to use the name. The other half are competent locals who've proven themselves useful enough to trust with collection routes and valve keys. This mixed composition creates interesting dynamics. The Fen-blood Valvehands have family stake in maintaining the system. The non-Fen members have technical skills that make them irreplaceable despite lacking genetic loyalty.

Men like Hennis represent the non-Fen technical specialists—a pure-line stevedore whose father taught him pump maintenance, who joined the Valvehands because it paid better than dock work and came with protection the Salvage Guild couldn't offer. He'll never sit on the Knot, never have real authority, but his knowledge of pressure dynamics makes him more valuable than many blood Fens.

Then there's someone like Dek Fen, one of Luka's third cousins, who can barely read a pressure gauge but knows every family in his collection district by name, their payment histories, their problems, their breaking points. He inherited a collection route the way others inherit trade skills, learning from his father which doors to knock on softly and which require the cargo hook's weight against the frame.

### **The Collection Rounds**

The Valvehands' morning routes follow water's path through the district. They start at the top floors where the wealthy-enough pay premium for consistent pressure, work down through middle floors where payment plans are negotiated, end in the floods where people pay in salvage, labor vouchers, or increasingly desperate barter.

Old Kern, fifteen years a Valvehand, describes the calculation: "You knock different for different people. New residents get the official knock—firm, professional, like you're doing a job. Old residents who always pay get the courtesy tap—they know what you're there for. Problems get the hook-knock, metal on wood, so they know this isn't a social call."

### **Three Generations of Stability**

The Knot's relative stability under three generations of Fen leadership has shaped the Valvehands' institutional culture. They remember Luka's grandfather, who squeezed out the last independent water operations. They served under Luka's father, who formalized the collection operation and payment structures that still exist. Now they work for Luka, who doesn’t innovate, and everyone likes it that way..

This continuity creates a professionalism unusual in gang operations. Valvehands think in terms of decades rather than seasons. They maintain infrastructure expecting to still be using it in twenty years. They cultivate relationships with residents' children, knowing those children will inherit their parents' debts and payment histories.

Sev, who joined under Luka's father, explains the generational perspective: "Luka's grandfather took the water by force. His father made it profitable. Luka keeps it functional. Each generation inherits what the previous one built, tries not to break it worse than it was."

### **How They See Luka**

To the Valvehands, Luka represents continuity rather than leadership. He's the third iteration of a system they've been operating for decades, neither savior nor tyrant but simply the current manager of an established machine. They appreciate his technical knowledge—he genuinely understands the plumbing—but more importantly, he understands the social infrastructure that makes collections possible without constant violence.

When Luka reduces fees for genuine hardship, the Valvehands see pragmatism rather than mercy. Dead residents don't pay anything. Desperate residents sabotage infrastructure. The calculation is practical: extract the maximum sustainable rate, not the maximum possible rate. Luka understands this because he was raised watching his father and grandfather make the same calculations.

His morning inspections with Riva aren't performances of diligence but actual technical work that needs doing. The Valvehands appreciate that he doesn't pretend collections are anything other than extortion, doesn't dress it up with ideology or pretense. He's a gang leader managing a protection racket built on a hydraulic monopoly. The honesty, paradoxically, makes him easier to follow than someone who'd insist on noble purpose.

### **The Succession Problem**

What terrifies the Valvehands isn't change but specific change. They've survived three generations of Fen leadership because each generation understood that water infrastructure and the social systems around it require careful maintenance. Jarek's contempt for technical knowledge, his certainty that sewer-fishers are lying about complexity, his belief that everything he doesn't understand is a scam—these attitudes would destroy in months what took fifty years to build.

Some older Valvehands have begun informal conversations about what happens "after." Not rebellion—they're too invested in the current system—but adaptation. How to maintain essential infrastructure when no one in leadership understands it. How to continue collections when the authority structure collapses. How to preserve the technical knowledge that keeps water flowing when the social knowledge of how to extract payment without triggering riots is lost.

Merk, senior among them, frames their position: "We're not loyal to Luka personally. We're loyal to fifty years of functional system. Luka happens to be the only one who understands that breaking that system breaks everything, including the Knot itself."

### **The Daily Reality**

A Valvehand's day starts before dawn, checking overnight pressure logs, identifying which sectors need adjustment, which pumps ran rough, which cisterns are low. By sunrise they're on collection rounds, knocking on doors in carefully calculated sequences, extracting payment through combinations of threat, negotiation, and occasionally genuine technical service.

Afternoons bring maintenance work—replacing gaskets, clearing blocked lines, cranking up the flow for an apartment that’s finally out of arrears, the technical labor that justifies their existence beyond pure extraction. Evenings are for updating the books, comparing notes with other collectors, identifying problems before they become crises.

They're criminals, certainly, but criminals who've professionalized their criminality into something approaching civil service. They extract payment for water that should cost less, but they also ensure water actually flows. In the ecology of the Middens, they occupy the niche between pure predation and actual service, forever balanced on the edge of both.

## **Crews of the Hanged Men: The Iron Yards Boys**

## **Extraction**

The Iron Yards Boys operate on principles of crystalline simplicity: generate maximum revenue with minimum complications. Unlike the Valvehands who must balance extraction with infrastructure maintenance, or the Chain-Men who exist primarily as a threat, the Iron Yards Boys run an actual profit center. They control the salvage yards where archaeotech and industrial scrap flow through the district, extracting percentages from every transaction, every cut, every sale.

These aren't the Hanged Men's traditional thugs but industrial extortionists who understand commodity prices, salvage values, and the economics of desperation. They know which salvage crews are skimming, which are holding back choice pieces, which are one bad week from collapse. More importantly, they know how to extract value without killing the productivity that generates it.

The band draws from a different pool than other Hanged Men forces—younger men, typically twenty to thirty-five, ambitious and cynical enough to appreciate Jarek's worldview. Many are failed Salvage Guild apprentices who couldn't afford certification, dock workers who got tired of being cheated, or simply clever locals who recognized that taking percentages paid better than earning wages.

### **Composition and Character**

The Iron Yards Boys include surprisingly few Fens. Jarek doesn't care about bloodline—he only cares about profit. A capable baseline human who can accurately assess salvage values is worth more than a Fen cousin who can't distinguish brass from gold. This meritocracy of greed creates a different culture from other Knot bands. Advancement comes from generating revenue, not from genetics or seniority or violence.

Men like Chill represent the typical Iron Yards Boy—a former assessor from the Salvage Guild who got caught skimming and lost his guild card. Instead of starving, he approached Jarek with a proposition: he'd identify which salvage crews were underreporting their finds in exchange for a percentage of the recovered value. Now he runs a network of informants among the cutting crews, knowing which teams found archaeotech before they even leave the yards.

Then there's Brass Tomm, who earned his name by always knowing the real composition of salvage. He can tell by sound, by weight, whether something's actually valuable or just dressed up to look it. Salvage crews hate him because he's never wrong, can't be fooled, and takes his percentage based on the actual value.

### **The Operation**

The Iron Yards operate on the shift rhythm. The Boys arrive with the morning shift at 5:30, positioning themselves at yard entrances to track what comes in. They don't need to threaten—their presence is threat enough. Salvage crews know that underreporting means broken fingers as a straightforward consequence, delivered without emotion or anger.

By mid-morning, the Boys are walking the cutting floors, observing which teams are working what salvage, noting who's trying to conceal components in scrap metal, identifying archaeotech that someone's hoping to pass off as waste. They don't intervene immediately—that would slow production. They just note discrepancies. Later, they reconcile.

Afternoons bring the actual collections. Teams line up to sell their morning's work, and the Iron Yards Boys stand beside the Guild buyers, taking their percentage before the sale. The smart crews pay honestly and promptly. The stupid ones try to negotiate, not understanding that negotiation implies options. There are no options.

**Management** 

Jarek runs the Iron Yards like a corporation with a violence department. He holds morning briefings where Boys report overnight intelligence—which ships arrived, which crews are planning cuts, which Guild journeymen are trying to bypass taxation. He reviews reports with the same attention Ygren gives to Knot finances.

His payments are prompt and generous When Chill identifies a hidden archaeotech cache worth thousands of obols, he receives his promised percentage immediately, in cash, publicly. When Brass Tomm prevents a crew from passing off pig iron as titanium, his cut arrives on the day. This reliability creates more loyalty than fear ever could.

Jarek's personal involvement stays minimal. He doesn't dirty his hands with collections or enforcement—that's what the Boys are for. He appears for strategic decisions, major disputes, or when example needs to be made. Otherwise, he manages through lieutenants who understand that their continued prosperity depends on his leadership.

### **Jarek**

To the Iron Yards Boys, Jarek represents the future—not ideologically, but practically. His complete lack of sentiment appeals to men who've been disappointed by every institution they've encountered. Violence is just process. Not personal. Not emotional. Necessary. This flatness feels honest to men who've betrayed by every cause they’ve pretended to believe in.

Kray, one of Jarek's lieutenants, explains the appeal: "Luka asks us to pretend we're providing service, maintaining order, protecting the community. All lies to make extraction feel noble. Jarek just says 'take the money.' No pretense, no justification, just honest theft. It's refreshing."

### **Dry-Dock 3**

The incident that almost triggered a gang war has become defining trauma for the Iron Yards Boys. They'd identified a salvage crew copper from a crashed hauler—thousands of obols worth of pure metal the crew claimed was "already spoken for" by the Pipefitters. Brass Tomm knew bullshit when he heard it. The copper was clean, unclaimed, and the Boys deserved their forty percent.

The crew refused payment. The Iron Yards Boys followed standard procedure—called in six Chain-Men to make an example. Garren's boys arrived with their usual equipment: chains, clubs, that absolute certainty that their heritage made them immortal. They walked into Dry-Dock 3 expecting to break some arms, maybe crack a skull, establish proper order.

Pipefitters were waiting with rifles.

Not energy weapons. Not blades that would skip off bone plating. Heavy-bore, bolt-action cannons which put slugs through anything. Two Chain-Men died immediately. Warborn genetics can't stop a bullet through the brain. Four more were wounded badly enough that they couldn't fight. The Iron Yards Boys, watching from what they'd thought was safe distance, scattered like roaches.

### **Luka**

The humiliation should have triggered immediate retaliation. In Garren's binary worldview, the equation was simple: they killed ours, we kill theirs. Jarek saw it similarly, though his calculation was economic—allowing the Pipefitters to establish violent precedent would undermine extraction from every union-protected operation.

But Luka paid the weregild instead. A thousand obols in electrical tokens to each dead Pipefitter family, formal acknowledgment that the Hanged Men had overreached, agreement to respect union protection of essential salvage operations. It prevented Bureau intervention—a gang war would draw Lens attention, might trigger military response, could destroy everything they'd built.

The Iron Yards Boys experienced this as betrayal. They'd identified legitimate extraction. They'd followed procedure. Chain-Men had died enforcing their claim. And Luka's response was to pay the families and say some salvage operations were off-limits? It confirmed everything Jarek had been telling them about Luka's weakness.

### **Grudge**

The Iron Yards Boys still track every union-protected salvage operation, calculating lost revenue, documenting what they see as theft. They maintain a shadow ledger of what they're "owed"—not just the obols from that initial incident but every subsequent extraction the Pipefitters have blocked.

Chill keeps a particular tally: "Seventeen thousand obols in copper wire. Eight thousand in brass fittings. That's our money sitting in Pipefitter warehouses because Luka's too weak to take it."

The Boys have developed elaborate fantasies about what happens when Jarek takes over. They'll hit every union operation simultaneously. The Chain-Men will come armed with guns, ready for projectile weapons. The Pipefitters will learn that industrial solidarity means nothing against organized violence.

## **Crews of the Hanged Men: Chain-Men**

### **The Perfect Life for Terrible People**

Garren’s Chain-Men have built themselves a paradise of consequence-free violence. They're huge, armored in bone, practically invulnerable to conventional weapons, and backed by an organization that not only permits but rewards their brutality. They don't need complex philosophy or biological excuses. They hurt people because they enjoy it, take what they want because they can, and live without consequences because who's going to stop them?

The Blink exists, yes—those ganglia fire and make violence easier, faster, more instinctive. But plenty of Chain-Men feel the Blink and choose not to act on it when it would inconvenience them. They don't snap on each other over card games because that would disrupt their own entertainment. They don't destroy the bars where they drink because then they'd have nowhere to drink. The violence they commit is selective, targeted, chosen. The Blink might suggest it, but they're the ones who decide.

The morning combat isn’t about establishing necessary hierarchy—they're about starting each day with sanctioned violence, getting the blood flowing, reminding themselves how good it feels to hurt someone who can't hurt you back. Even these fights are rigged. Garren always wins because anyone who might actually challenge him knows they'd die later. The hierarchy is determined by agreement to pretend the combat matters.

### **Frat House**

The Chain-Men barracks in Bright-Spire aren't military housing but a continuous party where violence is entertainment. They've got stolen furniture, looted alcohol, "comfort women" who know that leaving means their families suffer, and zero responsibility.

The space reeks of stale alcohol, sweat, and violence. Broken furniture that could be replaced isn't, because the breaking was fun and the broken pieces remind them of good times. Blood stains on walls stay because they're trophies. The medical facility isn't for treating Chain-Men—they heal in days—but for keeping victims alive long enough to be entertaining.

They tell each other stories about their violence. Remember when Krug held that debtor upside down until his eyes bled? Remember when Big Rem made that family watch while he broke every bone in their father's hands? Remember when they hung those Pipefitters from the bridge—not dead, just hung there until their shoulders dislocated? These aren't traumatic memories. They're the highlight reel.

### **Concubines** 

The women in the Chain-Men barracks aren't there by choice. Some are explicitly traded by families who can't pay tribute—a daughter for a debt. Others are just taken because a Chain-Man decided he wanted them. They last a few months usually, before violence or despair or "accidents" remove them from the picture. Then the Chain-Men get new ones.

The Chain-Men don't even pretend these are relationships. They're just another form of consumption, like alcohol or food or violence—appetites to be satisfied without thought for consequence. When Garren beat three wives to death, the other Chain-Men didn't see tragedy. They saw a man enjoying his property until it broke, then getting new property. The surprise was that anyone thought it was worth mentioning.

The children that occasionally result from these arrangements are problems to be disposed of. If they're obviously future Chain-Men, the mother might get exiled to raise a new bastard to join the barracks. If not, they disappear. The Chain-Men don't want families. They don’t want futures. They want an eternal present of violence.

### **How They See Garren**

To the Chain-Men, Garren is the biggest bully in a gang of bullies. He gets first pick of everything good and last blame for everything bad. They follow him because he lets them do whatever they want as long as they do what he wants first. It's not respect. He’s living the life they aspire to. Just more so.

When Garren kills someone for serving him wrong food, the Chain-Men don't see the Blink misfiring. They see a man who can face zero consequences. When he stands confused by Luka's restraint, they're seeing  genuine bewilderment that anyone would choose not to take what they can take.

Big Rem puts it simply: "Garren gets it. This life is short. Shorter for us. Why pretend to be good? Why hold back? We're going to die at forty anyway. Might as well enjoy every second of power we get."

### **Insult**

When the Pipefitters killed two Chain-Men, it wasn't an existential crisis about vulnerability. It was rage that someone had successfully said "no" to them. The Chain-Men are used to being untouchable, not because of their armor. Because of the terror. The Pipefitters demonstrated that Chain-Men could be defied.

The grudge they nurse isn't about their dead brothers. Chain-Men die all the time—from cancer, from fights, from overdoses, from finally pushing someone too far. The grudge is about disrespect. The Pipefitters should have submitted, should have accepted that they take what they want. Instead, they fought back and won.

Every day that the Pipefitters avoid retaliation is another day the Chain-Men are reminded that their reign of terror has limits. That's what they can't accept. Not the deaths—death is inevitable. But the defiance? That threatens the entire structure that lets them live as monsters.

### **Predation**

The Chain-Men's daily routine is structured around maximizing opportunities for consequence-free cruelty. Morning stair tolls aren't about revenue—it's about the power to make people pay to move through their own buildings, the daily reminder that life requires permission. They charge different amounts to different people based on whim, just to watch faces fall when today's toll is double yesterday's for no reason.

The afternoon "enforcement" is just hunting for excuses to hurt people. Someone's payment is short? Break fingers. Someone looked at them wrong? Break face. Someone walked too fast, too slow, too confidently, too fearfully? Break something. The justifications are retroactive—violence happens first, then they figure out what the victim did to deserve it.

Evenings in the barracks are for drinking, fighting, raping, and planning tomorrow's cruelties. They share intelligence about who has pretty daughters, who's accumulated enough wealth to be worth robbing, who showed defiance that needs correcting. It's not organized crime—it's organized sadism with crime as a side effect.
