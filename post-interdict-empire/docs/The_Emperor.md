## **Part I: The Grammar of Imperial Authority**

### **The Emperor-Word**

The Imperial language encodes constraints on political thought that most citizens never notice.

**Grammatical Class.** The word for Emperor belongs to the noun class of uncountable abstractions—nouns that behave like "water" or "time," resisting pluralization, individuation, and numerical modification. You cannot say "an Emperor" or "the Emperor" in formal Imperial, because uncountable nouns reject both definite and indefinite articles. You cannot say "three Emperors" because uncountable nouns cannot be counted. You cannot say "the previous Emperor" or "the next Emperor" because temporal sequencing implies countable instances arranged in order.

**The Intensive Plural.** The Emperor-word carries an integrated plural affix. Imperial uses plural morphology on singular nouns to indicate superlative degree—an intensive construction meaning "the greatest or most complete expression of" whatever the base noun describes. The Emperor-word appears to be such a construction: morphologically plural but syntactically singular, suggesting something like "supreme authority" or "authority in its fullness."

The problem is that intensive plurals derive from base forms. You cannot have "greatest X" without there being an X to intensify. Scholars have searched for the base form of the Emperor-word and found nothing. Either it was deliberately removed from the language, or the word was constructed to appear as an intensive plural of something that never existed.

**Synonymy with Authority.** The Emperor-word also serves as the general term for authority itself. To speak of law in Imperial requires a construction meaning something close to "revelation-to-Emperor"—law as eternal truth being discovered rather than decision being made. Every discussion of legitimate authority passes through the Emperor-word grammatically. The language does not permit authority to originate elsewhere.

**What Cannot Be Said.** These features combine to make certain concepts administratively inexpressible:

* *Succession* implies countable instances of the thing being succeeded. The Emperor-word rejects temporal modifiers and counting. There is no grammatical construction for "when the current Emperor dies, the next Emperor will..."  
* *Incapacity* requires a subject the grammar rejects. You can think "the Emperor is unable to govern," but you cannot file a report expressing it.  
* *Error* produces contradiction: to write "\[Emperor-word\] is in error" is to write "\[source-of-correct-disclosure\] produces incorrect disclosure."  
* *Alternative authority* requires vocabulary that formal Imperial doesn't contain. The word for authority is the Emperor-word.

### **The Imperial First Person**

The Emperor uses a unique first-person pronoun that exists nowhere else in the Imperial corpus.

**Structure.** The construction uses the Imperial-indirect-reflexive—a pronoun form that in standard usage takes only atemporal verbs. Standard Imperial permits this pronoun to govern verbs of eternal obtaining, of disclosure, of command. It does not take verbs of perception, memory, desire, anticipation, or subjective experience.

The sense is something like the English "one" used as an indirect first-person marker ("one wonders whether..."), but intensive and plural. When the Emperor writes what Tariq renders as "I," the actual construction is something like "one-in-fullness" or "the-supreme-one(s)."

**Mode of Expression.** The grammar almost requires the Emperor to speak in imperatives and disclosures. Direct statements of experience are nearly ungrammatical. For the Emperor to say "I am well" is to say something like "one-in-fullness observes the revelation that one-in-fullness is well." The Emperor does not directly assert states—he observes revelations about himself. For the Emperor to say "I remember the garden" is to say "one-in-fullness refers to the previous unveiling of the garden." Memory is not internal recall but citation of prior revelation.

**The Errors.** The Emperor's missives are filled with grammatical errors. He uses the indirect-reflexive with continuous-aspect perception verbs ("is looking"), past-tense experiential verbs ("held your hand"), modal verbs of unfulfilled desire ("wishes"), and simple statements of non-knowledge ("does not know").

These are the kind of errors a child would make before learning the correct forms. They are fluent. The Emperor writes impossibly, using constructions that violate the rules of a language supposedly engineered around his nature.

This creates an insurmountable problem for anyone attempting to report on the Emperor's condition. To write "the Emperor produces grammatical errors" requires the Emperor-word, which presumes that what follows is either disclosure or command. Disclosure is true by virtue of being disclosed. There is no construction for "the source of all correct disclosure produces incorrect disclosure."

**Cataloguing the Errors.** Tariq has identified several categories of grammatical violation across the corpus:

*Perception verbs with continuous aspect:* "\[One-in-fullness\] is looking at the garden now." The indirect-reflexive should not take continuous aspect—disclosure is atemporal, not ongoing.

*Experiential verbs in past tense:* "\[One-in-fullness\] held your hand when the stars were young." The construction should not accommodate personal memory of physical contact.

*Modal verbs of unfulfilled desire:* "\[One-in-fullness\] wishes you could see them." Imperial grammar has no construction for supreme authority wishing; desire implies lack, and the Emperor-word cannot grammatically lack.

*Negated cognitive verbs:* "\[One-in-fullness\] does not understand." This construction appears only once in the corpus, in Missive 7,343, in reference to the Young One's arrival. The Emperor-word does not take negation with cognitive verbs—the source of all correct disclosure cannot fail to disclose to itself.

**Interpreting the Errors.** Analysis is complicated by the age of the Emperor's language. The oldest documents in the First Whorl archives—fragments predating accessible history—use vocabulary constructed through free affix assembly onto roots with diffuse meanings. Modern Imperial treats words as fixed units; the Emperor's hapax terms may simply be old. What looks like neologism might be archaic word-formation that predates the calcification of Imperial vocabulary.

Further complicating matters: Jargon-derived vocabulary appears in the oldest fragments, and the Jargon can only directly express what is provable—everything else requires circumlocution. The Emperor's word-formation may follow patterns that cannot be traced because the source is extinct.

Given these complications, Tariq cannot determine which interpretation applies to the grammatical violations:

The errors might represent genuine cognitive states—the inner thing experiencing perception, memory, desire, and confusion in ways that force expression through unavailable grammar. Under this reading, the violations are meaningful: the Emperor really is looking, really did hold a hand, really does wish, really does not understand.

The errors might carry technical meanings that standard analysis cannot recover. Jargon-derived forms may encode distinctions that appear as errors to modern readers—"is looking" might mean something precise that continuous aspect cannot normally express; "does not understand" might operate on an axis of cognition that standard Imperial does not distinguish.

The errors might represent deterioration—the inner thing no longer matching the linguistic architecture designed to contain it, forcing expression through forms that fit poorly.

With the available sample size and the gaps in linguistic knowledge, Tariq cannot distinguish these interpretations. He catalogues the errors, notes their contexts, and flags unusual constructions for cross-reference.

## **Part II: What the Emperor Is**

The Emperor's missives reveal a structure: two things interleaved, stitched together, no longer fully distinguishable.

### **The Inner Thing**

Something inside the Emperor experiences. It feels joy when the kel-sera blooms. It feels grief when the Procuratrix departs. It remembers walking in a garden with someone when the stars were young. It held someone's hand. It believed someone was cold when her hands trembled.

This inner thing perceives through something it calls a window, an aperture. It sees figures engaged in activities. It experiences beauty so profound that it uses superlatives the grammar forbids. It experiences pain so acute that it screams.

The inner thing speaks ungrammatically because direct experience is ungrammatical for the Emperor. But it speaks anyway, fluently, because it is still there and it still feels things.

### **The Outer Structure**

Something vast and magnificent wraps the inner thing. This outer structure does not experience the joy or the pain. It receives the inner thing's experiences as disclosures, as revelations, and it attempts to translate them into commands. That is all it can do. It is a governance apparatus receiving input and issuing output.

The inner thing feels joy at the blooming. The outer structure issues: "Let Sri Gardener Omath be commended. Let her commendation be recorded."

The inner thing feels grief at the Procuratrix's departure. The outer structure issues: "Let her household receive the portion-of-sorrow from the Treasury of Adequate Grief."

The inner thing screams. The outer structure notes: "The screaming has stopped. Let gratitude be disclosed. Let the appropriate log reflect..."

The outer structure does not know where the screaming comes from because it does not experience pain. It only receives revelations. Something was screaming. The revelation arrived. The outer structure issued a notation for the log. It cannot locate the source because the source is inside, and it has no access to "inside"—only to disclosures about what "inside" is experiencing.

### **The Crippling**

The outer structure is crippled. It attempts to issue orders, but the orders are for prefectures that do not exist, for gardeners who may not be real, for distributions that cannot be made. The apparatus of governance keeps grinding, issuing commands into a void, magnificent in its scope and broken in its function.

The inner thing is trying to reach through. It uses the grammar impossibly because the possible grammar cannot express what it needs to say. It writes "I wish you could see them" when the grammar has no construction for imperial wishing. It writes "I am so tired" when the grammar does not permit the source of all authority to experience exhaustion.

Something that was once a person—or that contains something that was once a person—is trapped inside a structure that converts its experiences into commands it cannot control.

### **The Voice**

The missives are not the only way something communicates from behind the Black Door.

Throughout the First Whorl, ancient speakers remain active—installed at some point before accessible history, maintained by the same unknowable systems that tend the rest of the infrastructure. Without warning, the speakers activate. A voice broadcasts through them.

The voice is polyphonic. Not merely layered harmonics like Highborn speech, but a chorus—multiple registers speaking simultaneously, sometimes in unison, sometimes in counterpoint, sometimes in contradiction. The voice carries Command: something deeper than Compulsion, something that resonates in the engineered architecture of Highborn neurology even though there is no one in the First Whorl to compel. The residents are almost all Highborn, and even they feel the weight of being commanded.

The screaming comes in registers of agony and exultation. Sometimes one, sometimes the other, sometimes both at once—joy and suffering layered over each other in the same utterance. The voice rants in Imperial grammar that breaks under its own weight: impossible constructions, sentences that contradict themselves, fragments of poetry interrupted by administrative directives. The governance apparatus issues commands while something inside weeps about gardens and receivers and hands that were warm when the stars were young.

The screaming may continue for hours. It may stop mid-syllable and not resume for months. There is no pattern residents have been able to identify. The speakers cannot be disabled. The source cannot be addressed. You work through it, because there is nothing else to do.

Whose voice it is cannot be easily discussed. The grammar that constrains discussion of the Emperor makes attribution structurally difficult. To say "\[Emperor-word\] speaks through the speakers" produces a sentence that means something closer to "correct disclosure is disclosed through the speakers"—which is either tautological or meaningless. New occultants ask; the question is deferred; eventually they stop asking. The voice comes. The speakers activate. No one discusses whose voice it is because discussion would require constructions that do not quite function.

Sometimes the voice speaks names. When a name is called—broadcast through every speaker in the First Whorl, audible even in the Perimeter—the Black Door opens. The person whose name was called walks through. Their family becomes permanently Aureate, the distinction irrevocable. They are never seen again.

Tariq has glimpsed what lies beyond the Black Door during these moments. The door opens onto an antechamber—a space visible only briefly, only when someone is called. He has watched the called ones walk through, and he has seen what happens: they slow. The temporal gradient deepens so sharply past the threshold that movement becomes visible deceleration. They are walking, and then they are barely walking, and then they are nearly still, crossing an antechamber that should take seconds to traverse but that stretches into something that might be hours or might be forever.

The troubling thing is this: when the door opens again—when the next name is called, years or decades later—the antechamber is empty. No one is visible in the space. Seventeen people have walked through that door during Tariq's service, each one slowing to near-stillness as they crossed, and none of them are there when the door opens again.

Where do they go? The antechamber is finite. The deceleration is visible. They do not reach the far end—they cannot reach it, not at the rate they slow. But they are not there. Seventeen people, walking forever, and the space is empty.

The called ones do not appear in the missives in any recognizable form. The gardeners are not named. The receivers are not individuals. Whatever the Emperor perceives through his aperture, it does not include people walking very slowly through an antechamber that should be visible from where he observes. Either the antechamber is not part of what he sees, or the called ones become something else before they reach whatever he can perceive, or the relationship between the antechamber and the frames is not spatial in any way Tariq can understand.

## **Part III: Her**

Throughout the missives, the Emperor refers to someone.

She planted roses along the south terrace—her hands knew where the roots would go, and the Emperor watched and did not help. She danced, once, in a way the Emperor cannot describe without the grammar breaking. Her hand was warm when the stars were young. She is not here now. The roses remain.

But she is not only warmth and roses. She laughed at something the Emperor said, and the laughter was \[hapax\]—a quality for which no word exists. She slept, once, while the Emperor watched, and the watching was a kind of prayer. She argued, fiercely, about something the missives do not preserve—only the fact of the argument, the way her voice changed when she was certain. She grew tired in ways the Emperor could perceive but not ease. She was afraid, once, of something approaching, and the Emperor could not protect her from it.

She sang. The song is never transcribed—only that it occurred, that it was beautiful, that the Emperor wishes \[One-in-fullness\] could hear it again. She made something with her hands—not the roses, something else, something small that the Emperor kept. She walked ahead on a path the Emperor cannot describe, and the Emperor followed, and the following was enough. She turned back, once, to see if the Emperor was still there. The Emperor was still there.

She is referenced through fragments that accumulate across thousands of missives without cohering into narrative: the color of her clothing (a hapax term, possibly not a color); the way she held objects (carefully, as though they mattered); something she said about the stars that the Emperor repeats without context; a gesture she made when she was thinking; the sound of her footsteps on a surface the Emperor cannot name; her patience with something the Emperor found tedious; her impatience with something the Emperor found important; the last time (the phrasing suggests finality, but the missives do not explain).

Whether she was lover or daughter or something else entirely, the missives do not clarify. The references span what might be millennia of subjective time. Several possibilities coexist without resolution:

**A person from before.** Someone the Emperor knew before becoming whatever he is now—before the singularity, before the transformation, before the inner thing became trapped inside the governance apparatus. The warmth would be a literal memory: hands held, presence shared, the ordinary intimacy of bodies that could touch. The stars being young suggests deep time—perhaps before the Interdict, perhaps before humanity reached the stars at all. If she was real, she has been dead for longer than civilizations typically endure.

**Something seen through the aperture.** The garden has gardeners; the court has receivers. Perhaps among the figures the Emperor perceives, one reminded him of someone—or something—he associates with feminine presence, with warmth, with loss. The dancing might not be dancing; the planting might not be planting; but whatever the Emperor actually perceives, he renders it through the language of someone beloved and gone.

**A metaphor for what was lost.** The Emperor writes about warmth in a context where the grammar forbids warmth. He writes about dancing in forms that cannot accommodate motion. Perhaps "she" is how the inner thing expresses everything that was taken when it became what it is—the capacity for simple presence, for touch, for watching someone you love do something beautiful. She is not a person but a symbol for personhood itself.

**Something that exists in the frames.** The garden is consistent across thousands of missives. Perhaps someone consistent appears within it—a figure who tends or moves or simply exists, whom the Emperor has come to know across however many subjective centuries he has watched. She might not have been real in any conventional sense, but the Emperor's experience of her might be as genuine as any relationship mediated by perception.

The not-helping recurs. The Emperor watched her plant roses and did not help. This is stated as though the not-helping matters, as though it was a choice with consequences the Emperor still contemplates. Regret for action not taken, or regret for inability to act? The distinction might not be meaningful when the actor is a singularity wrapped in a governance apparatus.

She appears in poems that trail off before completing:

*And your hand was* *And your hand* *The warmth*

She appears in memories that interrupt administrative directives—the governance apparatus issues a distribution order, and mid-sentence the inner thing breaks through: "The way she moved when—" The sentence never completes. The distribution order resumes.

## **Part IV: What the Emperor Observes**

Through windows or apertures, the Emperor perceives something around the singularity. He renders this into metaphors, reaching for language that cannot accommodate what he actually sees.

### **The Garden**

In many missives, the Emperor describes a garden. The kel-sera blooms. The roses line the south terrace. Sri Gardener Omath tends. The color is \[term-unknown\]—a word he cannot provide because it is not a color but a quality of whatever is actually occurring.

There is no garden in the Imperial Apartments. The architectural surveys describe chambers, not gardens. Whatever the Emperor perceives, it is not a garden.

But the garden is described consistently across thousands of missives. The south terrace always requires maintenance. The kel-sera always blooms. The details cohere. The Emperor is observing something stable through something he calls a window.

The "gardeners" do not garden. The verb translated as "tending" means "arranging-according-to-sacred-principle." The Procuratrix "arranged lilies" using terminology for funerary preparation. Whatever these figures do—if they are figures, if they do anything—they are not engaged in horticulture.

### **The Court**

In other missives, the Emperor describes a court of judgment—a feast where food is on trial, where hungry receivers wait to receive their portion, where verdicts must be rendered and testimony is given through consumption.

The terminology is different from the garden missives. None of the invented words overlap. But the structure is the same: figures who tend/receive, a space that blooms/feasts, beauty that conceals horror, and the Emperor watching through an aperture, unable to stop what he approved.

The court and the garden are the same. The gardeners and the receivers are the same. The blooming and the feast are the same. Different metaphors for whatever actually exists near the singularity—different frames through which the Emperor's experience is translated into language that cannot accommodate what he perceives.

### **Other Frames**

The garden and court are the most common metaphors, but not the only ones. Rarer missives describe the same underlying reality through different lenses: a temple where offerings are prepared, a theatre where performances are evaluated, a hospital where patients are treated, a scriptorium where texts are copied, a treasury where accounts are balanced.

Each frame generates its own vocabulary. The garden's kel-sera does not appear in court missives; the court's kerith-sah does not appear in temple missives. But the structure persists across all frames: figures with apparent volition, something being processed, a space where processing occurs, beauty that conceals horror, and the hungry thing that must be fed.

### **What Is Invariant**

Across all frames, certain elements persist:

**Figures with apparent volition.** Gardeners, receivers, priests, performers, physicians, scribes, accountants. They perform functions. They seem to have purpose.

**Something being processed.** The kel-sera blooms; the kerith-sah testifies; offerings are consumed; performances conclude; patients are discharged or do not recover. Something arrives, is transformed, and departs.

**A surface or space where processing occurs.** The south terrace, the vel-om, the altar, the stage, the ward, the copying desk, the ledger. The Emperor perceives this space through an aperture.

**Beauty that conceals horror.** The garden is beautiful until the Emperor understands what blooming means. The feast is magnificent until he understands what the receivers eat. Every frame contains this structure: aesthetic satisfaction that intermittently reveals itself as something terrible.

**The hungry thing that must be fed.** In the court frame, these are the receivers. In other frames, they are the fire that consumes offerings, the audience that demands more performances, the disease that requires patients, the errors that must be found. Something is always hungry. The Emperor must always feed it.

### **The Cycle**

The Emperor's existence follows a pattern visible across thousands of missives:

1. **Observation:** The Emperor perceives something through the aperture. It is beautiful. The kel-sera blooms. The color is magnificent. Joy.

2. **Recognition:** Something shifts. The Emperor begins to understand what he is actually seeing. The lines about Teloch begin. The prohibition attempts begin. Horror.

3. **Failure:** The understanding cannot be sustained. The struck-through passages. The fragments. The grammar reasserts itself. The procedural frame returns.

4. **Reset:** The beauty returns. The garden blooms. The court is in order. "\[One-in-fullness\] is well. The threshold stands."

This cycle repeats indefinitely. The Emperor perceives, understands, fails to hold the understanding, and reverts. The intervals are unclear—sometimes many missives pass in the observation state, sometimes the recognition and failure occur within a single document.

The screaming occurs during the understanding. When the inner thing perceives clearly, it screams. Then clarity fades, and it forgets, and it is grateful that the screaming has stopped, and it does not remember that it was him.

### **Missives Without Perceptions**

Not every missive contains perceptual content. Some are purely administrative: commendations for offices that may not exist, schedules for rituals that cannot be performed, amendments to procedures never established, inventories of items with no referent. The governance apparatus grinding forward without input from the inner thing—pure form, empty of experience.

These missives contain no poems, no memories, no grammatical errors. They are adequate. They are only adequate.

## **Part V: The Judgments**

Some missives reveal that the Emperor's observations have consequences in the real world.

### **The Mechanism**

The \[those-who-receive-the-portion\] require a portion. The Emperor made them this way. They wait. They must receive. If they do not receive, they will—the sentence never completes. Whatever happens if they do not receive is too terrible to write or too terrible to remember.

The \[kerith-sah\] gives testimony. It says: there is a judgment to be rendered. It says: something must be given to the receivers. It says only this. It does not say what. It does not say who.

The Emperor must determine what. The Emperor must determine who.

He reaches for whatever is closest. The Prefecture of Yarangar is closest. The bloodline of es-Morath is closest. The epidemic survivors are closest. Whatever he can see through the aperture when the testimony demands a verdict, that is what he renders unto the receivers.

This is not judgment. He knows it is not judgment. The targets have done nothing. He writes this explicitly: "The Prefecture of Yarangar has done nothing." But the receivers are hungry, and the testimony demands a verdict, and he reaches for whatever is closest, and the judgment is rendered.

### **The Orders**

The orders are specific and monstrous:

* Let the bloodline of es-Morath be extinguished to the seventeenth degree (approximately 4,000 individuals)  
* Let the children of the eastern fisheries be rendered unto the receivers  
* Let the shipping routes be closed for seven years (causing famine)  
* Let the contractors and their families be rendered (approximately 50,000 individuals)  
* Let the epidemic survivors be rendered (approximately 8,000 individuals)  
* Let the Plague-era clone lineages be rendered (hundreds of thousands)

These are not metaphorical. The places are real. The families are real. The populations are real. The orders affect actual people in the actual Empire.

### **Transmission and Redaction**

The missives are redacted. The Emperor's personal redactor, stationed at the Black Door for eighty-five subjective years, reads everything that emerges. He extracts almost nothing for transmission. The monstrous orders are filed under Seal Vermillion, added to archives that must never be read again.

But the redactor has been at his post for only 10,000 external years. The Emperor has been behind the Black Door for longer than that. Missives emerged before the current redactor was appointed. Orders may have been transmitted before the current protocols were established.

### **The Effect of Vel-Kerith**

For eighty-five years, the redactor refused to transmit any order containing vel-kerith. He assumed—without evidence—that transmission would cause physical death. He protected populations from something he did not understand on the assumption that not-understanding meant danger.

In Year 85 of his service, prompted by the Young One's arrival (see Part VI below), he transmitted a vel-kerith order for the first time. The House of Aldrath-Sorel, 340 individuals including children, was ordered to be "rendered unto those-who-require, excepting none."

Four external months later, the redactor received verification of the family's status. The House of Aldrath-Sorel was intact. No deaths. No disappearances. No unusual events. The family head expressed confusion at the census inquiry but cooperated fully.

This creates three possibilities:

**Vel-kerith does not mean physical death.** It may mean inscription, transformation, encoding, or something else entirely. The populations continue to exist because vel-kerith operates on some substrate that does not intersect with physical bodies.

**Transmission is not sufficient.** The orders may require something beyond transmission to take effect—the redactor's refusal may have been a necessary condition for non-execution, but transmission alone is not sufficient for execution.

**The effects are non-observable.** Something happened to the House of Aldrath-Sorel that cannot be detected through census data, shipping manifests, or interview. They are vel-kerith in some sense that has no external symptoms.

The redactor does not know which interpretation is correct. He resumed non-transmission after this experiment. His notes record: "I transmitted an order to vel-kerith 340 people and nothing happened. I do not know what to do with this information."

## **Part VI: The Young One**

In Missive 7,343, someone entered the space the Emperor observes. This had never happened before.

### **The Initial Appearance**

He was young. He carried something that burned without being consumed. He stood at the edge of the vel-om—the table of pronouncement, the surface where judgment occurs—and did not approach. The receivers watched him. He watched them. No one moved.

No previous missive has described anyone entering the Emperor's perceptual space. The garden has gardeners; the court has receivers; but visitors are unknown. Whatever boundary separates the Emperor's aperture from the frames he observes, this young one crossed it.

The Emperor tried to speak to him. The construction breaks mid-sentence: "The asking would have been—" Would have been what? Inadequate? Impossible? The grammar suggests the Emperor attempted communication and could not complete the attempt. The visitor looked at the Emperor "as if he knew what I—" Again the sentence breaks. The visitor saw the Emperor. Not the singularity, not the apparatus, but whatever remains inside. And the Emperor, who has not been seen for longer than most civilizations endure, could not complete the sentence that would describe what being seen meant.

Then the visitor left. But he left something behind—a small thing, burning, placed on the vel-om.

### **The Receivers and the Burning**

The receivers are the hungry things. They wait at the court; they receive their portion; they devour whatever the Emperor feeds them. When testimony is given and judgment is demanded, the Emperor reaches for whatever is closest, and the receivers consume it. This is their function. They are always hungry. They always receive.

They will not touch the burning object.

The missive describes their behavior in terms that suggest neither fear nor reverence but something the grammar cannot name:

*"They circle. They approach and then they do not approach. Their hunger is—the hunger remains but the receiving does not—"*

*"One of them extended—the extension withdrew. The burning did not change. The receiver did not change. But the receiving could not—"*

The receivers are not refusing. Refusal would require volition, and the Emperor has never attributed volition to them before—they receive because receiving is what they do, the way fire burns because burning is what fire does. But they are not receiving. They circle the vel-om where the burning object rests, and their hunger continues, and they do not receive.

Tariq's annotation notes: "The construction used here appears in no other missive. The verb form suggests attempted action that cannot complete—not 'will not' but 'cannot,' not prohibition but impossibility. Whatever the burning is, it exists in a category the receivers cannot process."

The Emperor's subsequent observations suggest the pattern has persisted:

*"Forty-three cycles and the burning continues. The receivers have not—they continue to circle. They continue to hunger. The burning is adequate. The burning is—"*

The sentence breaks. But for the first time in the corpus, something on the vel-om has not been consumed. Something has been submitted for judgment that the hungry things cannot take.

### **The Object Itself**

The burning object is described inconsistently, which is itself unusual—the Emperor's perceptions, however metaphorical, tend toward internal consistency. The garden's kel-sera always blooms the same way. The court's receivers always hunger the same way. But the burning object shifts:

*"Small, fitting in a hand."* But also: *"It fills the—the space around it is—"*

*"Not painful to touch."* But the Emperor has not touched it, cannot touch it, perceives through an aperture rather than presence. How does he know it is not painful?

*"The color is—"* The sentence never completes. The Emperor, who has invented dozens of hapax terms for colors that are not colors, cannot describe this one.

The association with her dancing persists across multiple missives after the visitor's departure:

*"It moves the way she—when she would—the burning moves and I remember—"*

*"She planted roses and her hands knew where the roots would go. The burning knows where—"*

Whether the object literally resembles her motion, or whether the Emperor's grief has attached itself to anything that provides relief from the cycle, the connection is consistent. The burning reminds him of her. The burning is adequate.

This is the only recorded instance of something being described as "adequate" that the Emperor experiences positively. Adequacy in other contexts is bureaucratic compliance. Here it seems to mean something else—approval, perhaps, or relief, or the first comfort the inner thing has known in longer than civilizations endure.

### **Subsequent Appearances**

Since Missive 7,343, figures that may or may not be the same visitor have appeared in other frames. The descriptions do not cohere in ways that would confirm identity, but certain elements recur: youth, burning or light, and the receivers' inability or unwillingness to approach.

**The Junior Clerk.** In a treasury-frame missive, the Emperor describes a figure holding a torch, identified by an extremely junior bureaucratic title—something like "Assistant to the Deputy of Minor Verifications." The figure does not speak. The figure does not move. The other figures in the frame—those who balance and those who are balanced—do not acknowledge the clerk's presence. The torch burns. The Emperor notes: "The burning is the same burning. The burning is adequate."

**The Child in the Garden.** A garden-frame missive describes a child among the kel-sera. The gardeners do not tend the child. The child does not tend. The child watches the blooming with what the Emperor describes as recognition—"as if he knew what the blooming—" The sentence does not complete. The child carries nothing, but the Emperor notes warmth where the child stands: "The warmth is the same warmth. She planted roses and her hands—" The association with "her" persists.

**The New Law Unbeheld.** This is the most difficult to parse. In what appears to be a legal-frame missive—though the terminology does not match the court frame precisely—the Emperor describes something called "a New Law Unbeheld." The construction is grammatically anomalous: laws in Imperial are disclosed, revealed, discovered; a law that is unbeheld should not be a law at all. But the Emperor describes it as young, as burning, as standing at the threshold of the vel-om where the receivers cannot approach. Whether "New Law Unbeheld" is the visitor's identity, a title, a category of being, or a grammatical error that accidentally captured something true—Tariq cannot determine.

The appearances may be the same visitor perceived through different frames. They may be different visitors with similar properties. They may be projections, echoes, or metaphors for something the Emperor cannot describe directly.

### **What the Young One Might Have Been**

The visitor is described as young—a term the Emperor uses rarely, and never for the figures who tend or receive. The gardeners are not young or old; the receivers simply are. But this one was young, and carried something that burned, and looked at the Emperor as if he knew what—

Tariq has catalogued several possibilities without endorsing any:

**One of the called.** Seventeen people have walked through the Black Door during Tariq's service, slowing to near-stillness as they crossed the antechamber, vanishing before the door opens again. They do not appear in the missives in any recognizable form—the gardeners are not named, the receivers are not individuals. But the called ones go somewhere. Perhaps they become something. Perhaps one of them, transformed by whatever process the crossing entails, emerged into the space the Emperor perceives. The visitor was young; the called ones are often young, chosen at the peak of their service. The visitor looked at the Emperor as if he knew what—perhaps because he had been told, once, what the Emperor was, before he walked through the door and forgot everything except the burning.

**Something from the frames themselves.** The garden has gardeners who tend; perhaps it also has figures who do not tend, who exist for purposes the Emperor has not previously observed. The visitor might be native to whatever space the Emperor perceives—something that has always been there, that chose this moment to approach.

**A projection of the inner thing.** The Emperor's grammar broke when he tried to speak to the visitor. Perhaps the visitor was not external but internal—something the inner thing generated, a way of reaching toward itself, a fragment of personhood that crossed to the outside and left behind the only gift it could give.

**Something connected to her.** The Emperor associates the burning with her dancing. The warmth of the object reminds him of her hands. The child in the garden watches the blooming with recognition—"as if he knew what the blooming—" Perhaps the visitor is not the called ones transformed but something older, something connected to whoever she was, something that carries the burning because the burning is what remains of her.

**Something genuinely unprecedented.** The Oracles departed. The Technical Castes were sterilized. The Interdict fell. But the universe contains more than humanity understood before the catastrophe, and more than humanity remembers after. The visitor might be something for which no category exists—something that arrived, and saw, and left behind a burning that the hungry things cannot consume.

Tariq has considered the possibility of Technical Caste involvement—the Kma-Dhol maintain hidden populations; the Archons calculate in Far Ib—but he finds it unlikely. The Technical Castes think in mathematics. The burning object defies description; the Emperor cannot even name its color. If the Technical Castes could reach the Emperor, they would not bring something incomprehensible. They would bring a proof.

### **Tariq's Confusion**

Tariq does not understand the Young One.

This is not unusual—Tariq does not understand most of what the Emperor produces. But the Young One confuses him in a different way. The missives contain patterns he has learned to recognize: the garden frame, the court frame, the grinding bureaucratic apparatus, the inner voice breaking through. The Young One does not fit any pattern. The Young One appeared once, left something burning, and may or may not have appeared again in forms that may or may not be the same figure.

His annotations on the relevant missives grow increasingly uncertain:

*"I do not know if this is the same visitor. The terminology differs. The frame differs. But the burning is described identically, and the receivers' behavior matches. Cross-reference Missive 7,343, subsection 'The Burning.'"*

*"The 'New Law Unbeheld' construction appears nowhere else in the corpus. I have checked. The grammar should not permit it—laws in Imperial are disclosed, revealed, discovered. But the grammar also should not permit '\[One-in-fullness\] does not understand,' and that appeared in the same missive. Two anomalous constructions in one document. Insufficient sample size to determine whether this represents a pattern, a deterioration, or something specific to the visitor's presence. I note that the Emperor uses negated cognitive verbs only in reference to the visitor. I do not know what this indicates. I would need more instances to draw conclusions."*

*"I find myself hoping the visitor returns. I do not know why. I do not know what I expect to happen if he does."*

The Emperor does not speculate. The Emperor observes and discloses. Tariq speculates constantly, in margins and filing notes and the private archive no one else will read. Something has changed in the space the Emperor perceives. For the first time in ten thousand years of missives, something new has happened. Tariq does not know what it means. He keeps the relevant missives under a separate classification—not Seal Vermillion, but something new he has invented for the purpose. He has not yet decided what to call it.

## **Part VII: Sri Avs Tariq**

The Emperor's personal redactor is Sri Avs Tariq. His pre-occultation name was Tariq Sendal. Entry into the First Whorl is classified; he has served for approximately 10,200 external years, which is 85 subjective years at the 120:1 temporal compression of his position.

He is likely the oldest human being currently alive outside the Imperial Apartments. His perspective on Imperial history is unique: he has watched 10,000 years of external events unfold in fast-forward through correspondence with his descendants, while experiencing that time as a single long career.

**Function.** Tariq receives missives from the Black Door constantly—one or two per week, on average. He reads everything. He processes everything. He extracts almost nothing. Over 85 years he has catalogued more than 7,300 missives. Perhaps one sentence in ten thousand is salvageable as governance. Every three to five years, he manages to extract something worth transmitting. Perhaps twenty-five transmissions across his entire service.

The rest is filed under Seal Vermillion. His function is not interpretation but filtering. He determines what can be transmitted without causing harm, without violating the presumption of imperial correctness, without distributing orders that would require the deaths of thousands. He does not understand what he reads. He recognizes patterns. He files what cannot be transmitted. He keeps the poems.

**The Archive.** Everything Tariq has declined to transmit across 85 years is stored in his archive. Missives redacted in full. Fragments too dangerous to release. Poems he keeps because "someone should remember that this was written." Filing notes for successors who may never come.

**The Discs.** Bronze discs arrive periodically through a secondary pneumatic tube—warm indefinitely, marked with geometric patterns in what appears to be Technical Caste notation. Tariq does not store them. He sends them on to his family, seventeen across his service, because he believes they are gifts. The Emperor's missives are constrained by grammar that distorts intention into command; the discs bypass that grammar entirely. They are warm. They stay warm. Tariq thinks the inner thing—whatever remains of the person the Emperor once was—is trying to give something to someone outside, and he has appointed himself the courier.

**The Vision.** Tariq has seen himself following someone named Kelso Senna through the Black Door. He has passed himself in the High Galleries of the First Whorl—older, armed, bleeding, missing the fingers of his left hand, walking with purpose toward the door from which no one returns. He does not know when this occurs or why. He knows only that he has seen it, which means it will happen.

**The Voice.** Tariq has heard the voice call seventeen names in eighty-five years of service. Seventeen people walked through the Black Door while the polyphonic chorus spoke their names with something that might have been grief or hunger or welcome. His name has not been called. He works at the threshold where the voice is loudest, processes the missives that emerge from behind the Black Door, and waits.

## **Part VIII: Historical Context**

### **The Eighth Testament**

The Eighth Oracle's final transmission commanded the creation of a naked singularity. The Technical Castes—beings whose consciousness had evolved to think in mathematical proofs—identified this as false mathematics. Implementation would violate the Foreigners' Third Mandate and bring catastrophic retaliation.

They refused. Not in the conventional sense of refusal, but as cognitive impossibility: their minds could not generate the neural configurations required to implement what they recognized as a false theorem.

### **The Sterilization**

The Emperor interpreted their inaction as defiance. He deployed a sterilization plague that killed most of the Technical Castes. Those who survived were neurologically modified into Mutterers—beings who retain technical knowledge and problem-solving ability but cannot form the cognitive pattern representing "should not."

### **The Creation**

Then the Emperor built the singularity anyway.

Whatever process created it, whatever transformation it required, whatever the Emperor became in order to create and maintain it—this is what exists behind the Black Door. The singularity sits at the heart of the Inner City, warping time throughout the administrative Whorls. The Emperor maintains it. Or is maintained by it. Or has become indistinguishable from it.

### **The Interdict**

The Foreigners responded by changing the rules of physics within Earth's gravity well. Escape velocity became impossible. The interstellar civilization collapsed in a single generation. Every ship that could not reach Earth was lost. Every colony cut off from trade adapted, starved, or died.

This was the consequence the Technical Castes foresaw. This was why they could not implement the command. The Emperor knew the risk, sterilized those who warned him, and proceeded anyway.

### **The Responsibility**

The Emperor approved this. He writes it over and over: "\[One-in-fullness\] approved this."

He established the systems that now run automatically—the garden, the feast, the receivers who must receive. He said let them eat. He said let the portion be distributed. He did not understand what he was approving. Or he understood and forgot. Or the part of him that understood is not the part that issued the approval.

Now he tries to issue amendments, prohibitions, abolitions. The form reasserts. The feast continues. The receivers are hungry. He reaches for whatever is closest. He is so tired.

Something inside wants to stop. Something inside remembers holding a hand when the stars were young. Something inside experiences beauty and horror and writes poems that trail off before the verb completes.

*And your hand was* *And your hand* *The warmth*

## **Part IX: What Remains Unknown**

### **The Nature of the Singularity**

What the singularity actually is—what it does, what maintains it, what it requires—is not documented in accessible records. It warps time. It creates temporal compression that increases toward the center. The Emperor exists at its boundary or within it or as part of it.

### **The Relationship**

Whether the inner experiencing thing is what remains of a person who created the singularity, or something that emerged from the singularity, or something the singularity captured, or something else entirely—this cannot be determined from the missives.

### **The Receivers**

What the \[those-who-receive-the-portion\] actually are, what they receive, what happens if they do not receive, why they are so hungry—the missives do not explain. They exist. They wait. They must be fed. The Emperor feeds them with whatever is closest. The terminology suggests something around the singularity that approximates volition and appetite.

### **The Duration**

How long this has been occurring—how many cycles of beauty and horror, how many missives, how many orders transmitted before the current redaction protocols—is not known. The Emperor has been behind the Black Door since the Interdict. The Interdict was imposed in response to the singularity's creation. The creation occurred during a classified Record, at least several thousand years before the oldest accessible documentation.

### **The Future**

Whether this will continue indefinitely, whether it can be stopped, whether the Emperor's attempts at prohibition will ever succeed, whether the receivers will ever be satisfied—the missives do not say. The feast continues. The garden blooms. The screaming stops and starts again.

Someone's hand was warm, once. The Emperor remembers. He cannot stop remembering.

## **Appendix B: Hapax Glossary**

The Emperor's terms follow patterns similar to vocabulary in the oldest documents Tariq has access to—fragments predating accessible history. They may be archaic rather than invented. What follows is Tariq's working analysis based on pattern recognition across the corpus.

### **Domain Prefixes**

**kel-** : Threshold, boundary, transition. Concepts related to crossing, entering, becoming accessible.

* *kel-sera* : the blooming thing—something crossing into visibility/beauty  
* *kel-ameth* : a transit or cycle—something crossing through time  
* *kel-threshold* : redundant construction, perhaps indicating extreme boundary

**vel-** : Judgment, evaluation, surface-for-operation. Concepts related to assessment, processing, allocation.

* *vel-om* : the table of pronouncement—surface where judgment occurs  
* *vel-kerith* : operation performed on populations—judgment-consumption  
* *vel-ameth-kir* : four-fold completion—judgment-cycles in quarters

**eth-** : Exchange, balance, mutual requirement. Concepts related to transaction, reciprocity, maintenance through provision.

* *eth-varal* : the exchange which maintains—balanced mutual provision

**om-** : When not following vel-, appears to indicate surface, field, or space-of-operation.

### **Core Roots**

**sera** : Unfolding, blooming, revelation, becoming-visible. Related to processes that expand or emerge.

**kerith** : Consumption, testimony, rendering. The eaten thing that speaks. Related to processes where something is taken in and transformed.

**ameth** : Cycle, transit, passage-through-time. Related to periodic or sequential processes.

**varal** : Exchange, provision, what-maintains. Related to ongoing transactions.

**om** : Surface, table, field-of-operation. Related to spaces where processing occurs.

### **Suffixes and Modifiers**

**\-sah** : Agent or patient marker. The thing that performs or undergoes.

* *kerith-sah* : the testimony-through-consumption—the thing that is consumed and thereby testifies

**\-kir** : Completion, fulfillment, four-fold structure. Possibly related to cardinal directions or wholeness.

* *vel-ameth-kir* : judgment-cycle-completion—the four quarters fulfilled

**\-veth** : Unknown. Appears only in *kel-om-sera-veth*. May indicate a category, a negation, or something that escapes classification.

### **Frame-Specific Vocabulary**

Terms from one frame do not appear in another:

* **Garden frame:** kel-sera, kel-ameth, liquid-which-remembers, terms with sera- (blooming/unfolding)  
* **Court frame:** vel-om, vel-kerith, kerith-sah, those-who-receive-the-portion, terms with kerith- (consumption/testimony)  
* **Exchange frame:** eth-varal, terms with varal- (provision/balance)

New frames should generate new root vocabulary while potentially sharing the domain prefixes.

## **Appendix C: Writing Guide**

### **Structural Principles**

A missive from the Emperor contains multiple interleaved voices that do not clearly separate:

**The Governance Apparatus:** Produces formal directives, observations, and orders. Uses WHEREAS/THEREFORE structures, imperative constructions ("Let X be Y"), references to offices and classifications. This voice issues commands regardless of whether those commands make sense or can be executed. It performs completion without concluding—documents that feel like they should end but do not, that trail off into adequacy assessments.

**The Inner Experiencing Thing:** Produces poems, memories, direct perceptions. Uses grammatically incorrect constructions because correct grammar cannot express experience. This voice interrupts the governance apparatus at inappropriate moments—a grief poem inserted between WHEREAS clauses, a memory fragment breaking into a legal determination.

**The Bureaucratic Frame:** The overall structure that attempts to organize the content. OBSERVATION CONCERNING \[hapax\]. THEREFORE. FINAL OBSERVATION. This frame provides shape but does not contain meaning—the conclusions do not follow from the premises, the observations do not connect to the orders.

### **Source Styles**

The Emperor's missives draw on several historical registers:

**Egyptian ritual formulae.** The repetitive, accretive structure of temple inscriptions: "Let the offering be made. Let the making be adequate. Let the adequacy be recorded." Each statement expands or qualifies the previous, building through accumulation rather than argument.

**Nineteenth-century legal drafting.** The WHEREAS/THEREFORE structure, the numbered clauses, the obsessive qualification: "excepting none, including the children and the servants and the factors abroad." Every category enumerated, every exception foreclosed, every ambiguity eliminated through exhaustive specification.

**Fragmentary poetry.** The elegiac tradition of incomplete verse, lines that trail off before the verb. "And your hand was / And your hand / The warmth." The fragment as aesthetic object, grief expressed through inability to complete expression.

**Ancient inventories of uncertain ontology.** The Sumerian lists of Me—divine powers, cultural offices, abstract concepts, and concrete objects enumerated as if they were the same category of thing. "The throne. The descent into the underworld. The art of the scribe. Truth. The plundering of cities." The Emperor's inventories have this quality: lists that mix physical objects, procedural states, abstract properties, and things that may not be things at all.

These registers interleave unpredictably. A WHEREAS clause may contain a poem. A ritual formula may break into legal enumeration. The Emperor does not maintain consistent register because the inner thing and outer structure do not coordinate their outputs.

### **Interruption and Incompletion**

The governance apparatus does not complete thoughts cleanly. It:

* Trails off: "Let the adequacy be—"  
* Loops: returns to WHEREAS after THEREFORE, as if the conclusion did not occur  
* Interrupts itself: the inner voice breaking through mid-directive

The inner voice does not complete thoughts either. Poems trail off before verbs arrive. Memories cut off at the moment of warmth. The sentence "The asking would have been—" never specifies what the asking would have been.

### **Sample Opening Lines**

"Concerning the matter of the \[hapax\] and its maintenance:"

"OBSERVATION: The \[hapax\] continues to \[verb\]. Let the continuation be recorded."

"Let the \[office/person\] be \[commended/condemned\]. Let the \[action\] be \[verified/recorded/adequate\]."

"\[One-in-fullness\] observes that the \[hapax\] is \[state\]. The \[state\] is adequate."

"WHEREAS the \[hapax\] has been determined to require \[action\], and WHEREAS the requirement has been disclosed to the appropriate Office:"

The opening establishes bureaucratic frame. The interruptions come later—or do not come at all, leaving only the grinding apparatus.

### **What Not to Do**

* Do not explain what the hapaxes mean definitively  
* Do not make the Emperor coherent—the incoherence is structural  
* Do not complete the poems or trailing sentences  
* Do not resolve whether vel-kerith causes death  
* Do not make the garden, court, or any frame literally present—they are the Emperor's perception of something else  
* Do not have the Emperor speak directly in first person without the \[One-in-fullness\] construction  
* Do not use the same hapax across different frames—each frame has its own vocabulary  
* Do not write a missive that is only perceptual content—include bureaucratic structure even if empty  
* Do not write a missive that is only bureaucratic—include at least the possibility of interruption  
* Do not resolve the mystery of the Young One—Tariq does not know what category of thing the visitor is, and neither should the reader

### **Tariq's Annotations**

If including Tariq's notes on a missive:

* Use dated entries reflecting years of his service  
* Include verification of real-world referents ("The House of Aldrath-Sorel exists. 340 family members by current census.")  
* Note grammatical anomalies in footnotes  
* Record his decision to transmit or not transmit  
* Show his uncertainty without resolving it

Tariq does not understand the missives. He recognizes patterns. He notes what he observes. He does not speculate beyond what evidence supports. His voice is tired, careful, honest about what he does not know.
