# Akama Arts and Performance

## Contrast with Imperial Society

The fundamental difference between Imperial and Akama society lies in how each handles inequality. The Empire recognizes that engineered hierarchy creates injustice requiring mitigation. The Akama have no such project. They view genetic inequality as natural and just, requiring no mitigation. Where the Empire tries to separate legal authority from personal power, the Akama embrace their identity.

## Examples of Akama Arts

## A Misfortune-Song

*Performed by Drom Salt-Mouth at the Broken Ridge gathering, third year of the current Junta*

My father gave me his destrier when I turned fifteen. A fine animal—gray, strong-legged, bad-tempered. I named him Worthy because I thought that was clever.

Worthy threw me the first morning. I landed in a thornbush. My sister laughed so hard she couldn't breathe. I got back on.

Worthy threw me at the river crossing. I went in face-first and came up with a fish in my shirt. The fish was more surprised than I was. I got back on.

Worthy threw me in front of Toghan Six Crow's daughter, the one with the good teeth. I landed in goat dung. She married someone else. I got back on.

Worthy threw me seventeen times that summer. I kept count. On the eighteenth time, I stayed on. He was too tired to throw me again.

Worthy died of old age last spring. I buried him where he threw me the first time. I visit sometimes, to check if he's staying down.

He is. I finally won.

## A Strength-Song

*Sung by Orvak Three-Fingers before the raid on Keth-Masol, as recorded by witnesses*

I held the canal-gate at Broken Glass for two days when the Torek came. They sent nine riders the first morning. Three came back. They sent fourteen the second day. None came back. On the third day they went around.

I killed Dhom Ironfoot in single combat at the Gathering of Knives. He was taller than me by a head. He had two more fingers than I do. He doesn't have anything now.

My grandfather held the eastern scarp against the Republic's scouts. My father held it after him. I hold it now. When my sons are grown, they will hold it, or they will not be my sons.

I am Orvak Three-Fingers of the Cracked Shield Band. I have told you what I have done. Tomorrow we ride to Keth-Masol. You will see what I will do.

## A Genealogy

*Recited by Vurda Eleven-Sage at her daughter's naming ceremony*

My mother was Khoram Eleven-Sage, who kept the herds through the Bitter Winter when half the calves died and the other half lived because she knew which ones to bring inside. Her mother was Tsogha Eleven-Sage, who married into the line from Seven Elk and brought the fishing rights at the third bend. Her mother was Gholdara Seven Elk, who was beautiful and cruel and outlived four husbands, each one richer than the last.

Before Gholdara the records are unclear. We say the line runs back to the Fourth Oracle through a daughter whose name is forgotten, but many say this, and who can prove it now? What we can prove is the fishing rights. What we can prove is the herds. What we can prove is that Eleven-Sage women survive, and what survives is what matters.

My daughter is Makhru Eleven-Sage. She was born on an unlucky day, so we waited, and named her when the sage-phase came around again. She will learn the herds. She will learn which calves to save. She will learn that survival is the only proof of worth.

This is her inheritance. May she use it well or lose it trying.

## Abstract Work: A Garden in Ogon

In the courtyard behind the Tama compound, where the autofactory's wall meets a structure no one uses, there is a garden that Kel Tama's grandmother began and her mother continued and she maintains now.

The ground is gray gravel raked each morning into parallel lines. A single stone, rust-colored, sits off-center—not at a mathematically pleasing point, but where it looked right when her grandmother placed it sixty years ago. A channel cut into the autofactory's concrete runs with water when it rains, dry otherwise, the edge stained with verdigris from a copper fitting somewhere in the structure above.

Nothing else. No plants, no ornament, no carving. The lines in the gravel, the stone, the water-channel, the way the morning light falls across the gray and rust and green.

Kel's mother spent eleven years adjusting where the gravel met the base of the wall—the transition between horizontal lines and vertical surface. When she died, Kel left that edge exactly as her mother had finally placed it.

Visitors sometimes ask what the garden means. Kel tells them it doesn't mean anything. It's just right.

## Monumental Carving: The Masks at Dharel's Span

Where the ziggurat called Dharel's Span meets the canal, nine masks look down from the concrete face. The oldest is perhaps three hundred years weathered, features worn until only the brow-ridge and eye-hollows remain distinct. The newest was carved within living memory, when the current band took the span from the Torek confederation.

The masks are not portraits. They have no names attached, no inscriptions identifying whose ancestors they represent. The band knows—the knowledge passes with the territory—but an outsider sees only faces. Heavy brows. Prognathous jaws. The ruff of fur suggested in rough-cut lines radiating from the cheeks. Black pigment in the eye-hollows. Rust filling the deep grooves that mark the brow.

Below the masks, a scene runs the length of the wall: riders approaching a ford, the water indicated by horizontal cuts, the far bank held by figures whose faces are turned away. The outcome isn't shown. The riders approach; the defenders wait; the moment before contact extends forever in cut concrete.

The band that holds Dharel's Span now claims descent from both the riders and the defenders. They say the scene shows an alliance being formed, not a battle. The Torek, who held the span before them, said it showed their ancestors' victory over invaders. The carving supports both readings. It will support others, when the span changes hands again.

## A Song for the Quiet Times

*Sometimes sung in villages during the long evenings, attributed to no one in particular*

The barley is in. The rain will come or it won't. The destriers are pastured and the calves are counted. There is nothing to do tonight but sit.

My hands are rough from the work I've done. My back aches from the loads I've carried. Tomorrow there will be more. Tonight there is only the fire.

The raiders will come in spring or they won't. The tribute will be what it always is. Someone else can worry about the fords. Tonight I am warm and my bowl is full.

The great men do great things. The strong men do strong things. I mended the fence where the goat broke through. It was not a great thing, but the goat is inside now.

## An Arrow-Maker's Observation

*Attributed to Tsur Dust-Eater, who made arrows for the Broken Ridge band for forty years*

People think the wood is the shaft and the feathers make it fly. They have it backwards. The wood is what you remove to find the shaft. The feathers are what you add to show the shaft where to go.

A good arrow already exists inside the branch. You are just letting it out. If you force it, you get a crooked shaft that wobbles in flight. If you listen to the grain, you get a shaft that flies true because it was always meant to.

I have made perhaps ten thousand arrows. Maybe six thousand flew true. Maybe six of those were perfect—shafts that left the bow and never doubted where they were going. I did not make those arrows. I found them.
