# Akama Architecture

**Core intention:** "Human presence marked on alien concrete. We live here despite what this place was built for."

---

## Physical Environment

The Thousand Kingdoms occupy former Oregon and Washington—coastal temperate rainforest in the west, the Malpais scablands in the east.

**Ogon (primary city):** Oregon coast where the autofactory transformed the landscape. Rain falls 200 days per year; temperatures hold 50-60°F with constant humidity. Everything is perpetually damp—moss grows on concrete, water runs down walls, mold accumulates in corners. Douglas firs 200 feet tall grow through broken concrete where the autofactory's oldest sections have been reclaimed.

**The Grand Canal:** The Columbia River, geometrically straightened by the autofactory into walls of fused glass thirty feet high. Marks the northern approach to Ogon.

**The Malpais:** Volcanic basalt with soil too thin for agriculture—scablands stretching across eastern Oregon into Idaho, sagebrush and bunchgrass, villages only where water runs year-round.

## Visual References

| Reference | Application | Difference |
|-----------|-------------|------------|
| **Brutalist architecture** (Boston City Hall, Barbican London) | Autofactory structures pushed to extreme—concrete, geometric, no human accommodation | "Brutalists tried to make usable buildings. The autofactory wasn't trying." |
| **Mayan pyramids** (Tikal, Chichen Itza) | Stepped ziggurats | Without ornamentation. Pure geometric form, concrete not stone. |
| **Japanese temple construction** (Ise Shrine) | Careful carpentry with visible joinery, natural wood | "Ise uses perfect wood, regularly replaced. Ogon uses whatever timber is available, built until it rots." |
| **Scandinavian coastal villages** (Faroe Islands, Norwegian fishing settlements) | Bright paint on weathered wood in harsh climate | "There colors are purchased. Here they're made from rubble." |
| **Japanese wabi-sabi** | Acceptance of weathering and decay as beautiful—rust, moss, cracks | "Wabi-sabi involves careful selection. The Akama just let things weather naturally and call that good." |
| **Mongol ger/yurt** | Felt tent as perfect mobile architecture | The destrier replaces the horse as center of life and economy. |

## Construction Vocabulary

**Settled construction (Ogon):**
- Douglas fir timber
- Mortise and tenon joinery
- Wooden pegs
- Honest display of construction
- Footbridges spanning canals
- Platforms built atop ziggurats
- Residential structures built INSIDE autofactory shells—proper timber frames within wrong-dimensioned concrete rooms

**Mobile construction:**
- Felt tents (destrier-based nomadism)
- Functional quality over display
- Equipment optimized for packing and transport

## The Three Colors

Made from autofactory waste:

| Color | Source | Application |
|-------|--------|-------------|
| **Rust-orange** | Iron oxide burned from corroded steel | Bold territorial markers |
| **Lime-yellow** | Calcium from burned concrete | Accent color |
| **Teal** | Verdigris scraped from corroded copper | Accent color |

**Application style:** Bold, visible brushstrokes, weathering accepted. Diagonal slashes marking human presence against alien concrete.

## Key Architectural Elements

- **Diagonal paint slashes** marking human territory on autofactory concrete
- **Timber structures within concrete shells** (proper frames in wrong dimensions)
- **Stepped ziggurats** with wooden platforms added on top
- **Footbridges** spanning canals and autofactory gaps
- **200-foot Douglas firs** growing through broken autofactory sections
- **Moss and weathering** accepted as natural, even beautiful

## Generating New Details

*Example: A residential structure in Ogon.* The autofactory room is thirty feet tall with a twelve-foot doorway—built for something larger than humans. Inside, a proper timber frame creates a two-story dwelling, the ceiling at normal human height, the upper space used for storage. The timber is Douglas fir, joints visible, pegs proud of the surface. Rust-orange diagonal slashes mark the doorframe. The gap between timber wall and concrete shell holds insulation—moss, fabric scraps, whatever was available. Rain runs down the concrete behind the timber walls; channels carved into the floor direct it outside.

*Example: A warband's winter camp.* Felt tents in a circle, flaps facing inward toward the central fire. The khan's tent at the position of honor—largest, but not ostentatiously so. Armor displayed on frames outside each tent. The ground between tents worn to mud from foot traffic. Diagonal orange slashes on nearby rocks marking "we were here." When they leave, the fire circle and the slashes will remain.
