# **Sewer-Fishers: A Comprehensive Analysis of Technical Caste Descendants in Imperial Society**

## **Biological Architecture**

Sewer-fishers represent a stable population of Technical Caste descendants, genetically optimized for infrastructure maintenance in confined, hazardous environments. Their physiology diverges from baseline humanity through systematic modifications that prioritize flexibility and chemical resistance over structural rigidity.

Adult sewer-fishers range from 1.35 to 1.55 meters in height, with body mass typically under 50 kilograms. This compact frame results from bone composition that favors cartilage over calcium, creating skeletons that remain partially flexible throughout life. The defining characteristic—slip-joint articulation—allows voluntary skeletal compression through specialized cartilaginous joints, enabling passage through openings as narrow as 25-30 centimeters. This capability isn't merely useful but essential for maintaining infrastructure in spaces designed without human access in mind.

The skull never fully sutures, maintaining infantile fontanelles that allow cranial compression. This creates extraordinary vulnerability during infancy, when the skull consists of small bone fragments floating within pressurized cerebrospinal fluid. The CSF pressure patterns, normal for sewer-fishers, would cause fatal hydrocephalus in baseline humans. This fundamental architectural difference ensures nearly complete reproductive isolation—crosses with other castes typically result in miscarriage, and the rare viable offspring are invariably sterile.

The skin presents as slate-grey with an oily sheen, permanently coated in a lipid-silicate emulsion that provides chemical resistance. This isn't an external coating but an integrated organ system producing protective compounds from specialized cells. The eyes, void-black and enlarged to approximately one-third of facial area, lack visible irises and demonstrate tapetum lucidum reflection in low light. Blade-shaped ears extend backward from the skull, providing enhanced acoustic sensing in resonant underground spaces.

## **Developmental Patterns**

Sewer-fisher infants experience unique developmental challenges. From birth to approximately nine months, they exhibit "muttering"—continuous vocalization of mathematical patterns through motor-vocal overflow. This represents a neotenous Technical Caste trait, where cognitive processing externalizes through the vocal system before executive function develops. All Technical Caste lineages pass through this phase; Mutterers remain arrested in it permanently.

The extraordinary infant mortality rate—approaching fifty percent—stems from multiple factors. The fragile skull structure makes infants vulnerable to trauma that baseline humans would survive. The transition from muttering to executive function represents a critical developmental checkpoint where neural architecture must stabilize; many infants fail this transition, remaining trapped in mathematical overflow until metabolic exhaustion causes death.

Cultural response to this mortality involves refusing personhood until muttering ceases. Infants pass between "aunts" who provide specialized care while maintaining emotional distance. Naming occurs at nine months for those who successfully develop executive function, marking their transition from biological potential to recognized person. This practice provides psychological protection for communities where emotional investment in early infancy often precedes devastating loss.

## **Cognitive Architecture**

Sewer-fishers inherit Technical Caste neural structures that process mathematical relationships as immediate sensory experience rather than abstract concepts. This manifests as intuition rather than knowledge—they perceive when systems are unbalanced, feel mathematical relationships as physical sensations, and recognize patterns without conscious calculation.

This cognitive architecture enables interpretation of Mutterer vocalizations through pattern recognition rather than translation. Both populations process information through similar mathematical frameworks, allowing intuitive understanding despite lacking common language. However, this same architecture creates communication barriers with other castes, as sewer-fishers cannot articulate the mathematical relationships they perceive.

The absence of formal education means these intuitions remain undeveloped into systematic knowledge. Each clan maintains family libraries of ancestral treatises documenting accumulated understanding within narrow technical domains. These texts, stored within Oracle remembrance shrines, preserve practical knowledge while lacking theoretical framework. A sewer-fisher might solve complex equations through intuition while being unable to explain their methodology in terms others understand.

## **Social Organization**

Sewer-fisher society operates through matriarchal clan structures, with authority passing through maternal lines. This arrangement emerges from reproductive realities—when breeding success depends on careful genetic management, those who bear the physical consequences necessarily control reproductive decisions. Matriarchs maintain detailed knowledge of bloodline compatibilities, using this information to maximize genetic diversity within the limited breeding population.

Marriage customs remain deliberately informal, with emotional partnerships often distinct from reproductive pairings. This flexibility allows optimization of genetic diversity while maintaining stable emotional relationships. Children belong to entire clans rather than nuclear families, with communal child-rearing distributing both labor and expertise across multiple caregivers.

Clans incorporate non-sewer-fisher members through fostering, creating cultural unity without biological integration. The Hasket clan exemplifies this approach, with stevedore orphans comprising eighty percent of clan membership while maintaining separate genetic lineages. These arrangements provide mutual benefit—sewer-fishers gain physical protection, while fostered members gain economic inclusion and family structure.

Decision-making operates through consensus when possible, democracy when necessary. The cognitive uniformity among sewer-fishers means technical decisions typically achieve natural consensus, as all participants perceive similar mathematical relationships. Democratic processes only become necessary for resource allocation and other non-technical matters where optimization provides no clear answer.

## **Economic Marginalization**

Without formal caste registration, sewer-fishers cannot legally work, pay taxes, or access Imperial services. The Benrus clan's unique status as registered "Technician (Mixed-Caste)" makes them the exception, allowing them to serve as intermediaries for all other sewer-fisher economic activity. Every formal transaction technically flows through Benru ledgers, creating a documentary fiction that satisfies Imperial requirements.

This marginalization produces a shadow economy of extraordinary complexity. The Salvage Guild employs thousands of "temporary auxiliary workers" whose temporary status has persisted for generations. The Pipefitters Union maintains "provisional apprentice" categories for workers without caste documentation. Even the Hanged Men, enforcing Imperial law, must negotiate with clan matriarchs whose legal existence they cannot acknowledge.

Clans specialize in different infrastructure niches: Loaks operate vertical transport, Sarns maintain deep pumping systems, Venns manage high-altitude rigging, Chos control water distribution, Haskets provide heavy labor, and Benrus broker between all parties. These specializations emerged from economic necessity rather than genetic variation, as all sewer-fishers share essentially identical Technical Caste heritage.

## **Cultural Practices**

Oracle remembrance shrines serve as repositories for both genealogical and technical knowledge. Ancestral treatises sit beside name-tablets, creating equivalence between family history and technical expertise. Children learn mathematics as inherited wisdom rather than abstract discipline, with each equation connected to specific ancestors.

Religious participation focuses on the Penitent Church, whose radical egalitarian theology resonates with sewer-fisher experience. The Church's doctrine that all humans share guilt for the Interdict aligns with sewer-fisher mathematical intuitions about reality's fundamental wrongness—they perceive the universe as mathematically unbalanced, though they cannot articulate why.

The veneration of figures like Tatius Benru, the sewer-fisher who allegedly passed the Seven-Cornered Examination and entered Bureau service, provides aspirational models for transcendence through mathematical excellence. His icon appears in household shrines beside the Third Oracle, who in sewer-fisher folklore revealed their existence to humanity as part of divine plan.

## **Imperial Dependencies**

They alone can navigate the confined spaces where critical systems operate, interpret the mathematical relationships governing complex infrastructure, and survive the chemical and radiological hazards of deep maintenance. Their absence would cause cascading infrastructure failure within weeks.

The relationship encapsulates Imperial contradiction: they depend on populations it cannot acknowledge, maintaining elaborate fictions to avoid confronting its own dependence on those it marginalizes. Sewer-fishers maintain infrastructure designed by ancestors the Empire claims to have eliminated, using knowledge the Bureau insists doesn't exist, solving problems through cognitive processes that officially cannot occur.

## **Demographic Constraints**

Reproductive isolation creates a genetic bottleneck threatening long-term survival. With perhaps twenty thousand breeding-age individuals in ya-Sattra's surface population, maintaining genetic diversity becomes increasingly difficult. Each generation must carefully manage bloodlines to avoid accumulating deleterious recessive traits while being unable to introduce new genetic material from other populations.

Deep populations, maintaining purer Technical genetics in unmapped tunnels, might serve as genetic reservoirs. However, these populations remain largely theoretical to surface dwellers, their existence inferred from maintained infrastructure in supposedly abandoned zones. If they exist, their even purer Technical heritage might make them completely unable to breed with surface populations, including diluted surface sewer-fishers.

The fostering system provides cultural but not biological diversity. While incorporated members strengthen clans socially and economically, they cannot contribute genetic material. ## **Clan Benru: The Trusted Brokers**

### **Family Business and Relationships**

The Benrus operate from a three-story structure just outside Vetch-Rise, in the Iron Yards—close enough to the Salvage Guildhall for daily business but within traditional sewer-fisher territory. The building's basement floods during high tides. The middle floor houses the brokerage proper, where Nyma maintains constant negotiations. The upper floor contains family quarters and the clan shrine.

Their business model depends on laundering illegal label into documented work. When the Loaks need to be paid in obols rather than power- or water-tokens, Benru ledgers show the work performed by "Benru-contracted labor." When the Sarns bring rare deep salvage, Benrus forge ownership chains that satisfy Guild requirements. They charge modest percentages—typically 3-5%—enough to maintain wealth without creating resentment.

The clan maintains careful neutrality in conflicts. When Pipefitters clash with the Salvage Guild, Benrus mediate without taking sides. When Chain-Men demand increased tolls, Benrus negotiate payment schedules that prevent violence. This neutrality is pragmatic rather than philosophical—taking sides would destroy their intermediary value.

Their marriage connections create information networks. Through the Chos, they understand Knot politics. Through the Loaks, they track salvage flows through Lift-3. These connections are deliberately maintained through arranged marriages that might produce sterile offspring but create permanent alliance bonds.

The relationship with the Bureau of the Coin remains complex. Their registration grants privileges but also scrutiny. Bureau inspectors regularly audit their ledgers, searching for evidence of the shadow economy everyone knows exists. The Benrus maintain two sets of books—one real, one for inspection—using mathematical techniques passed through the maternal line to ensure the false books remain internally consistent.

### **Living Situation and Idiosyncrasies**

The clan occupies a former pumping station, its machinery long ago sold for scrap but the robust architecture remaining. Iron fixtures bear the green patina of constant moisture, while walls show the high-water marks of historical floods. The building's original valve wheels have been repurposed as decorative elements, a reminder of their ancestral profession.

The brokerage floor features an unusual arrangement: two separate entrances, one for sewer-fishers, one for surface dwellers. Visitors never directly interact with anyone other Bernrus; Nyma or her assistants shuttle between spaces, carrying information but preventing direct contact that might upset either party. The sewer-fisher entrance opens onto a dock on the canals which cut between the Midden tenements, where small boats can arrive discreetly. The surface entrance faces a respectable commercial street.

The clan maintains obsessive documentary habits. Every conversation gets recorded, every agreement copied in triplicate. Children learn to write before they can properly walk, practicing letters on slate boards that can be quickly erased. The family library contains not just technical treatises but forty years of reverse-engineered Bureau of the Coin investigations of their fraud operation, slowly reconstructed from public documents.

They dress deliberately above their station but below suspicion. Korel's robes are well-made but not luxurious. Nyma wears clerk's clothing with subtle modifications—hidden pockets for water chits, reinforced sleeves from handling rough salvage documentation. The children wear standard Midden clothing with one exception: brass pins shaped like valve wheels, marking them as Benru.

Daily routine revolves around tide schedules. Morning low tide brings sewer-fisher petitioners arriving by tunnel. Afternoon high tide sees surface merchants arriving by street. Evening brings the reconciliation, where the day's real transactions get transformed into acceptable documentation.

The clan shrine holds particular significance. Beyond the standard Tatius Benru icon, they maintain his actual Bureau registration certificate—one of only three official documents acknowledging sewer-fisher ancestry. Families bring children to see it, proof that transcendence is theoretically possible. The certificate is water-stained and barely legible, but its Bureau seal remains visible.

The Benrus practice a peculiar form of honesty: they never lie about numbers. They might create elaborate fictions about who performed what labor, but the quantities—tons of salvage, gallons of water, counts of workers—remain absolutely accurate. This numerical integrity, inherited from thirty generations, makes them trustworthy even to those who know they're facilitating illegal activity.

Their wealth manifests subtly. They eat better than most sewer-fishers—fresh vegetables rather than algae starch—but never conspicuously. They maintain emergency water reserves that could sustain the clan for months. Most significantly, they can afford to keep elderly clan members who no longer work, a luxury most sewer-fisher families cannot manage.

The clan's greatest fear is losing their registration. Every generation must provide someone capable of maintaining the legal fiction, someone who can interface with Bureau requirements while preserving clan loyalty. The pressure on young Tessik is immense—not just personal success but clan survival depends on his ability to navigate between worlds.

### **Living Situation and Idiosyncrasies**

The clan occupies Hollow-Egress's three uppermost levels, with the top floor split between Merra's kitchen and the engine room. The kitchen's proximity to the engine provides year-round warmth. The smoke from trash fires and the canal's perpetual sewage smell don't make it this high. It's almost homey.

The middle level, converted from mechanical space when the engine simplified support requirements, houses living quarters accessible only through narrow hatches. These openings, easily navigated by sewer-fishers but challenging for larger castes, provide security through architecture. Eight small chambers surround a central meeting space where the clan conducts private business away from the kitchen's public nature.

The rooftop gardens transform available surface into agricultural production. Korr's hives occupy the southeast corner, producing honey with antimicrobial properties from urban-adapted bees. Hydroponic potato tanks, repurposed from industrial use, provide staple starches. Every remaining surface hosts vegetables—beans climbing salvaged trellises, tomatoes in hanging systems, hardy greens in shallow beds.

Water management shapes daily activity. The Lockworks provides minimal flow this high, forcing reliance on elaborate rainwater collection. Overlapping tarpaulins channel precipitation into cisterns built throughout the structure. During storms, the clan mobilizes to adjust collection systems, treating water capture as seriously as lift operations. Their roof ownership—technically leased from the Hanged Men but practically hereditary—makes collected water legally theirs.

The engine room serves as practical classroom. Children learn mathematics through mechanical reality: engine rhythms teach counting, counterweights introduce algebra, rope tension demonstrates geometry. This education produces operators who intuitively understand vertical dynamics but might struggle with abstract concepts unrelated to lift work.

Daily routine integrates multiple systems. Dawn brings dew collection before salvage operations begin. Morning sees heavy lift traffic. Afternoon focuses on garden maintenance during slower lift periods. Evening brings another salvage surge. Night involves engine maintenance and water system adjustments. Someone always remains awake, monitoring the engine's harmonics.

The clan maintains three parallel accounting systems. Official Guild documentation shows minimal activity. Internal written ledgers track actual flows. Mental accounts, never documented, record the favor economy determining real access. Children learn all three, understanding that survival requires maintaining these separate realities simultaneously.

The stevedore orphans have developed their own internal culture, maintaining birth-caste strength training while learning sewer-fisher technical knowledge. They provide physical deterrent—Chain-Men hesitate before confronting a clan with stevedore protection. This arrangement benefits both groups: orphans gain family structure and economic inclusion, sewer-fishers gain physical security.

The family shrine, besides standard icons, maintains components from fatal lift accidents—not as morbid trophies but as recognition of responsibility. Each piece bears a name, reminding operators that vertical transport kills. This memorial aspect reinforces the clan's careful approach to maintenance and operation.

The intersection of food production, lift operation, and water collection creates complex logistics. Every system must function without interfering with others. The clan has developed precise choreography through generations of practice, though outsiders find the constant coordinated activity overwhelming. This complexity itself provides protection—few outside the clan understand their operations well enough to effectively interfere.

### **Family Business and Relationships**

The ropeworks produces everything from delicate surgical sutures to massive dock cables, each product incorporating mathematical patterns that optimize strength beyond what materials alone would suggest.

Their toll-avoidance infrastructure represents careful political calculation. Every unofficial crossing reduces Hanged Men revenue, but the Venns ensure these losses remain below the threshold triggering violent response. They maintain approximately thirty unauthorized routes—enough to provide alternatives but not enough to constitute open rebellion. The routes themselves demonstrate sophisticated engineering: self-adjusting tension systems that accommodate weather changes, breakaway anchors that prevent catastrophic failure, redundant safety lines hidden from casual observation.

The relationship with the Loaks involves critical reciprocity. Venn cables keep Lift-3 operational, while the Loaks provide priority lifting for Venn materials. Competition with "official" rigging companies remains minimal because the Venns operate in spaces others won't enter. Insurance excludes sewer-fisher work, formal companies won't touch illegal modifications, and most engineers lack the mathematical intuition to recognize optimal anchor points. The Venns fill these gaps, charging enough to survive but not enough to attract major competition.

The clan's relationship with the Chain-Men stays carefully ambiguous. The enforcers know unauthorized crossings exist but finding them requires vertical expertise the Chain-Men lack. Occasional "discovery" of obsolete routes allows face-saving enforcement without disrupting actually important crossings. Ressa maintains a mental schedule of which routes to "accidentally" expose when political pressure demands visible compliance.

### **Living Situation and Idiosyncrasies**

The Venns don't occupy a single structure but maintain a distributed presence throughout the Middens' vertical spaces. Their primary residence consists of interconnected platforms built into the support structures of three adjacent buildings, connected by bridges only they fully trust. These platforms, constructed from salvaged materials and Venn-woven cables, sway with wind but never collapse—the clan's mathematical intuitions account for resonance patterns that killed previous high-dwellers.

The ropeworks occupies a former textile factory, its machinery repurposed for cable production. The building's height allows for hundred-meter vertical test drops, essential for quality verification. The work floors maintain specific humidity through water sprays, as fiber tension varies with moisture content. The smell of tar, used for waterproofing, permeates everything.

Living spaces reflect adaptation to height. Furniture anchors to walls. Possessions store in secured containers. Children learn to sleep through building sway that would nauseate ground-dwellers. The constant wind creates a sonic environment of whistles and moans that clan members interpret as weather information.

The clan maintains emergency caches throughout the district—coiled cables, anchoring equipment, emergency food supplies sealed against moisture. Only clan members know all locations, information passed verbally to prevent documentation. These caches allow rapid response to infrastructure emergencies and provide escape routes if ground-level violence erupts.

Daily routine follows wind patterns. Calm mornings see new installations. Afternoon winds test existing rigging. Evening brings maintenance and inspection. Night work happens only during emergencies, as darkness and height combine lethally even for experienced riggers. The clan has developed a sophisticated understanding of microclimate patterns, knowing which routes become dangerous in specific weather conditions.

Education focuses on three-dimensional thinking. Children build string figures which are brutally evaluated by their elders. This is not cruelty: to work at height means that your life, and the lives of your family, depend on your work. They learn to visualize stress patterns, understand material fatigue, recognize the sound of cable approaching failure. This training produces adults who think naturally in three dimensions while other castes remain psychologically ground-bound.

The fostered beamcrawler, Goss, occupies a special position. His extreme reach allows installations impossible even for sewer-fishers. The clan treats him as a valuable specialist rather than mere labor, understanding that his capabilities enable work that generates premium fees. His presence also connects the Venns to the broader beamcrawler community, opening additional labor possibilities.

Safety practices mix technical knowledge with superstition. Every rigger carries a "luck carabiner"—a carabiner connected to a line, connected to nothing, which elders "joke" will catch on the skyhooks if you fall. They maintain numerical taboos about certain installation patterns, avoiding configurations that feel mathematically unstable even if engineering suggests they're sound. These practices reflect deep intuition about structural failure that transcends conscious calculation.

Eldin made a note in the mental ledger that tracked which crossings were active, which were compromised, which were deliberate sacrifices to maintain the fiction of cat-and-mouse rather than systematic undermining of toll authority. The game required losses to remain plausible.

Below, the Chain-Men continued their watch, unaware that their controlled checkpoint had just become theater. The real traffic would flow overhead, following paths that existed in the space between improvisation and engineering, maintained by a clan whose mathematical intuitions let them see structure where others saw only chaos.

The morning shift change came and went. The crossing held. The city's vertical geography had developed another unauthorized wrinkle, one that would persist until discovery forced the next evolution. The Venns were already calculating where that would be.

## Stillbirth

The water in the preparation basin had already gone cold, but Anira couldn't bring herself to drain it. The small body within, no larger than her two fists together, showed the characteristic signs: the skull sutures that hadn't properly fused, the curved spine that suggested incompatible growth rates between tissue types, the webbing between fingers that had developed too early, trapping them before bones could form. Another attempted crossing between sewer-fisher and orbital caste. Another failure at seven months.

Siven hadn't spoken since delivering the stillborn. He sat in the corner of the medical room, still wearing his work clothes from the Lockworks, mechanically cleaning the same valve assembly over and over. His Fen heritage showed in his height, his narrow build, the long fingers that could reach into machinery spaces—advantages that made him valuable to the clan's operations. Advantages they'd hoped to pass on.

"The spine separation occurred around week twenty-two," Mella said quietly, reviewing her notes. She'd survived her own birth despite mixed heritage, but chronic pain had taught her to recognize skeletal incompatibilities with clinical precision. "Earlier than last time. The neural tissue grew at Technical Caste rates while the vertebrae followed baseline orbital patterns."

Three years ago, when Mella had delivered her own stillborn, she'd instituted documentation protocols that went beyond traditional midwifery records. Each failed pregnancy became data: gestation length, failure mode, genetic combinations attempted. The clan now possessed the district's most comprehensive records on cross-caste reproductive failure, though the knowledge brought no solutions, only earlier recognition of inevitable outcomes.

Elder Ro entered without knocking—at eighty-one, he'd attended enough of these to dispense with formality. He carried the shrine materials: the carved bone pins that would mark another attempt in the family records, the incense that covered the medical smell, the cloth that had wrapped successful births in generations before the Fen connections began.

"Third this year," he said, not accusingly but with the weight of pattern recognition. "Twenty-seventh since the intermarriage started."

"Twenty-eight," Anira corrected. "We don't usually count the ones that fail before quickening, but Lira's last year makes twenty-eight."

The preparation had its own rhythm, developed over too many repetitions. The body would be wrapped in sailcloth—organic material that would decompose at the same rate as the flesh, preventing the horror of bones surfacing during low tides. Weighted with specific stones that would hold it in the deep channels where the Sarns assured them the currents wouldn't return it. The ritual acknowledged that this wasn't quite a person yet—no name, no formal mourning period—but wasn't mere medical waste either.

Ten Cho-Fen arrived late, his partial skeletal rigidity making the ladder descent difficult. His survival as a mixed-heritage birth was the cruel proof that success was theoretically possible, though the constant pain he endured made it unclear whether success was the right word. He touched Siven's shoulder—solidarity between those whose bodies bore the evidence of genetic optimism.

"The documentation suggests a pattern," Mella said, because analyzing helped her process. "Pregnancies from orbital-sewer-fisher crosses that survive past four months always fail between six and eight months. The developmental cascades become incompatible once neural complexity reaches a certain threshold. We're not losing them randomly—we're losing them at predictable developmental checkpoints."

"Then stop trying," Elder Ro said, but without heat. They'd had this argument before. Would have it again.

"The Lockworks needs operators with reach and mathematical intuition both," Anira replied, equally practiced. "The next generation won't have enough pure-line births to maintain operations. We adapt or we lose the infrastructure."

The economics were as brutal as the biology. Each pregnancy meant months of reduced capacity, resources spent on additional nutrition, risk to the mother. The clan had invested twenty-eight attempts' worth of resources into genetic futures that never materialized. Other clans whispered about the Chos' desperate gambling, though they all understood the pressure of demographic collapse.

Vas arrived with the younger cousins who needed to witness the process—not from cruelty but from education. They would understand the costs before making their own choices. Lira stood among them, nineteen years old, having recently refused a proposed Fen marriage that would have allied the clan with another Knot family. Her presence was statement enough: she'd seen too many of these preparations to risk her own.

The wrapping began, each fold precise. Siven finally moved, taking his position as the father who wasn't quite a father, holding one corner of the sailcloth while Anira held the other. The movements were choreographed by repetition—they'd done this together before, would likely do it again unless they accepted what the numbers clearly indicated.

"I can adjust the work schedules," Anira said quietly, planning already for recovery, for the careful return to normalcy that would pretend this was temporary setback rather than recurring tragedy. "The new apprentices can handle the routine maintenance."

The procession to the deep channel would happen after sunset, when fewer people would notice another sailcloth bundle being carried to the waterline. The Sarns would ensure it reached the proper depth. By morning, the only evidence would be another bone pin in the shrine, another entry in Mella's careful records, another data point in the slow realization that some adaptations extract prices that compound across generations.

The basin was finally drained, the water flowing back into the same system the Chos managed for the entire district—the infrastructure that required their hybrid expertise to maintain, that demanded they keep attempting these genetic bridges despite the accumulating grief. The clan would continue because the alternative was accepting that their knowledge would die with the last pure-line generation, taking the Lockworks' functionality with it.

Tomorrow, operations would resume.

## The Sale

Korim Sarn laid the object on Nyma Benru's counting table with the careful movements of someone handling either something precious or something dangerous. It was both and neither—a cylinder perhaps twenty centimeters long, made of material that looked like ceramic but felt like metal, warm to the touch despite having been submerged at crushing depth for hours before retrieval.

"For the record," Nyma said, stylus poised over her ledger, "what would you call this?"

Korim's jaw worked as he attempted translation between the pressure-pulse mathematics the deep populations had used to describe it and the commercial terminology the Benrus required. "It's... a resonance stabilizer. No. A harmonic... battery? The below-ones demonstrated its function but—" He made a gesture that started as explanation and degraded into frustration.

Old Mett had come up specifically for this negotiation, though at eighty-three, the climb from even the first transition chamber left her gasping. She spoke in the half-mathematical dialect that older Sarns sometimes fell into when stressed: "Shows the wave-pattern-keeping. For systems that breathe-process. Seven-thirteen-seven oscillation maintenance."

Nyma's stylus hadn't moved. "I need something I can write in a Bill of Goods that a Guild assessor will understand."

"Industrial component," Korim tried. "Pre-Interdict. Still functional."

"Every salvage claim says that. What does it do?"

The cylinder sat between them, occasionally making a sound like crystalline growth, though visual inspection showed no change. Pen Sarn had wanted to open it in his workshop, but the below-ones had been emphatically clear through pressure-patterns that could only be interpreted as "NO NO NO SEVEN NO."

"Perhaps," young Tessik Benru suggested from his corner where he'd been observing, "we could describe it by equivalent function rather than actual purpose? Like how we list Mutterer work as 'specialized technical consulting'?"

Nyma considered this. The boy had good instincts for documentary fiction. "What would be the equivalent?"

Korim touched the cylinder again, feeling the almost-vibration that wasn't quite sound, wasn't quite heat, wasn't quite electricity. The below-ones had showed him a demonstration—one of their failing atmospheric processors suddenly running smoothly when this was brought near. But they'd also shown him three other devices that had stopped working entirely in its presence. The mathematics of it had been beautiful, expressed in harmonic proofs that made perfect sense while experiencing them but became impossible to verbalize.

"Power regulation," he finally said. "It makes certain machines work better. Others work worse. The pattern isn't random but I can't... the words don't exist."

"The words don't exist in Trades-Manual," Old Mett corrected. "In the jargon—" She lapsed into a string of tonal pulses that made the cylinder resonate sympathetically, producing a harmony that felt meaningful but conveyed nothing to the Benrus.

Vera, Korim's fifteen-year-old daughter, had accompanied them to get experience with surface negotiations. She'd been silent until now, but suddenly spoke: "It's like a tuning fork for machines. Some need its frequency, others can't tolerate it. The below-ones know which ones because they can feel the mathematical compatibility, but we'd need to test each surface machine individually."

Nyma's stylus moved finally. "Specialized frequency generator for industrial harmonics calibration. Pre-Interdict. Requires technical assessment for compatibility. Irreplaceable. Origin: deep salvage, authenticated by Sarn clan extraction."

"That's not exactly—" Korim began.

"It's what I can document," Nyma interrupted. "Now, more importantly, who's the buyer?"

This was the real challenge. The Sarns had brought the object up because the below-ones had specifically requested they do so, but their explanation of why had been incomprehensible—something about "geometric necessity" and "pattern completion for the ascending system." The mathematical proofs had been elegant but untranslatable.

"Den Vesh expressed interest in anything that affects industrial processors," Korim said carefully. "And there's a Creche research unit that collects anomalous technical components."

"The Creche doesn't pay market rates," Korel Benru noted, having emerged from the back office. "But they don't ask questions either. Vesh pays well but might want to understand it too thoroughly."

The cylinder made another crystalline sound. Everyone paused. In the deep, Korim had learned to interpret these sounds as communication, but at surface pressure they were just noise. Or mostly noise. There was something almost linguistic in the pattern, like overhearing a conversation in a language you didn't quite speak.

"There's another issue," Korim admitted. "The below-ones want something specific in trade. Not the usual chemical supplies or food."

"What do they want?"

Korim pulled out a piece of paper where he'd attempted to draw what they'd shown him through pressure-sculpture. It looked like a complex knot diagram or possibly a molecular structure. Below it, he'd written: "Thing that makes the small-small-small stop moving wrong."

Nyma stared at it. "Is that... are they asking for medicine?"

"Maybe? They showed patterns of decay at microscopic levels, then showed this pattern stopping it. But whether it's chemical or biological or something else—" Korim shrugged.

"Antibiotics," Tessik suggested. "They want antibiotics. See, this structure here looks like a benzene ring—"

"You can read their notation?" Korim asked, surprised.

"No, but I can recognize when someone's trying to draw organic chemistry without knowing the conventions. My tutor made me study pharmaceutical texts. This is someone attempting to show molecular structure through topology."

The negotiation shifted. If the below-ones wanted antibiotics, that changed everything. Medicine was controlled, tracked, expensive. But it was also comprehensible, documentable, tradeable.

"I can list this as medical salvage," Nyma said slowly. "Pharmaceutical precursor materials. That explains the high value, the specific trade requirements, the need for technical assessment. The Creche buys those when they're authentic pre-Interdict."

"But it's not—" Korim began.

"It's something that requires antibiotics in trade, which suggests biological or medical application," Nyma countered. "That's close enough for documentation."

The cylinder sat between them, occasionally humming in frequencies that made everyone's teeth ache, carrying purposes that spanned the gap between the deep populations' mathematical reality and the surface world's commercial fiction. The Benrus would document it in terms that made sense to bureaucracy. The Sarns would trade it according to patterns only they could perceive. And somewhere in the deep, populations that might or might not still be human would receive antibiotics for reasons that pressure-mathematics couldn't quite convey.

"Twenty percent commission?" Nyma suggested.

"Twelve," Korim countered automatically, though his heart wasn't in the negotiation. He was still thinking about the below-ones' demonstration, the way they'd shown sick members of their population, if that's what they were. The pattern of their illness had been beautiful in the mathematical language, all wrong angles and broken symmetries, but he suspected it translated to something much simpler and sadder in human terms.

"Fifteen, and I'll handle the Creche negotiations personally," Nyma offered.

"Done."

The cylinder would disappear into the Creche's research halls, its true purpose never discovered or documented incorrectly or both. The antibiotics would descend through transition chambers to populations whose need for them raised questions no one wanted answered. And the Sarns would continue their role as translators between worlds, carrying objects and meanings that degraded with each attempt at explanation, maintaining commerce between realms that shared nothing but the necessity of exchange.

## Family

The pump had seized at the worst possible depth—four levels down in the aquaculture complex, where the tunnels narrowed to pre-collapse dimensions and the water pressure would crush baseline lungs. Voss Hasket could feel the problem through the water itself, the way the flow patterns stuttered against his skin, but reaching the pump meant navigating passages that his sewer-fisher frame could barely compress through, then somehow removing a steel impeller that weighed more than he did.

"Has to be today," Tasme said, reviewing the ledger with characteristic precision. "Another twelve hours without circulation and we lose the entire seventh-tier mussel bed. That's eight hundred pounds of protein, minimum."

Rem, the Ironbone variant who served as Brakk's lieutenant, studied the architectural plans they'd inherited from whoever had originally built these chambers. His massive frame—nearly 2.4 meters of enhanced muscle and reinforced bone—made him imposing above ground, but down here it made him useless. "I can't even fit through the primary access. My shoulders are wider than the tunnel."

"That's why you're not going alone," Voss said, already stripping down to compression gear. At twenty-four, he'd learned to navigate spaces that would trigger claustrophobia in most humans, his sewer-fisher physiology allowing voluntary joint dislocation and ribcage compression. "But I'll need you at the staging point."

The plan was elegant in its necessity. Voss would navigate the flooded tunnels to reach the pump, assess the blockage, then guide Rem's strength through a chain of mechanical advantage. They'd done similar operations before, but never at this depth, never with this much at stake.

The descent began at what the clan called the "little door"—an access point that had been standard-sized before three centuries of settling had compressed it to barely forty centimeters wide. Voss exhaled completely, dislocated his left shoulder with practiced ease, and pushed through. Behind him, Rem fed out salvaged cable, each meter marked with knots that would tell him Voss's depth even in the absolute darkness below.

The water at the third level already tasted different—more mineral, less biological. The mussels here had adapted to near-toxic conditions, concentrating metals in their shells rather than flesh. Voss could feel the pressure in his inner ear, that Technical Caste inheritance that let sewer-fishers navigate by depth sensation alone. The pump's vibration, wrong and stuttering, created a sonic map of the tunnel system.

"Fourth tier," he called back, his voice distorted by water and distance. "The impeller's definitely seized. Feels like cable wrapped around the shaft."

"Cable means weight," Rem's voice echoed down. "Someone's been fishing where they shouldn't."

Illegal salvage lines—dropped by desperate scavengers who knew the aquaculture pools connected to deeper, older parts of the city. Sometimes they found treasure. Usually they just fouled the Haskets' equipment. This time, they might have cost the clan a season's worth of food.

Voss reached the pump housing at the fifth level, where the tunnel widened just enough for him to work. The mechanism was pre-Interdict, built when engineers assumed someone would always be small enough, flexible enough, desperate enough to maintain it. His fingers found the problem by touch—not just cable but chain, wrapped so tightly around the impeller shaft that it had worn grooves in the metal.

"It's bad," he reported. "Maybe sixty kilos of tangled steel. The release mechanism is behind it."

This was where their collaboration became essential. Voss could reach the release, but triggering it required more force than his sewer-fisher frame could generate. Rem had the strength but couldn't reach the depth. The solution required both.

"Setting up the pulley," Rem announced. They'd prepared for this possibility—a salvaged block and tackle that could multiply mechanical advantage. But it needed precise positioning, and that's where little Mika came in.

At fourteen, Mika was small even for a sewer-fisher, but she had the family gift for mathematical intuition. She felt the angles without calculating them, understood load distribution through some inherited sense that converted physics to instinct. She guided Rem's massive hands: "Left bracket, thirty degrees higher. No, feel the stress point? There, where the metal sings differently."

The chain they rigged was itself a work of art—Rem's strength threading heavy cable through mounting points that Mika identified by sound and touch, creating a system that would translate his power through three levels of flooded tunnel to where Voss waited.

"Ready," Voss called, wrapping the chain around the release mechanism, his body braced against the pump housing. "Remember, steady pressure. Too fast and the mechanism shears."

Rem began to pull. Two hundred kilos of enhanced human strength, reduced through mechanical disadvantage but still overwhelming compared to what Voss could generate. The little cousin felt the force arrive through the chain, guided it with minute adjustments that kept the pressure even. The release mechanism groaned, forty years of corrosion fighting the inevitable.

Then, with a sound like a bone breaking underwater, it gave.

The tangled mass of chain and cable burst free from the impeller, and Voss barely avoided being crushed as sixty kilos of steel tried to fall through the space he occupied. He compressed against the tunnel wall, feeling metal scrape past, then heard the blessed sound of the impeller spinning free.

Water began to move properly through the system again. Voss could feel it in his bones—the mathematical rightness of proper flow, that Technical Caste sense for systemic harmony. The mussels would survive. The clan would eat.

The ascent took longer than the descent. Voss had to guide the fouled cable up with him—evidence of illegal salvage that Brakk would use to demand compensation from whoever had been poaching their water. By the time he emerged at the little door, Rem was ready with blankets and the harsh alcohol that would prevent the industrial pathogens from taking hold.

"Good work," Tasme said simply, but her approval carried weight. She made a note in her ledger—not just of the incident but of the solution. These patterns mattered, the way big and little cousins could create capabilities neither possessed alone.

Jek and Toll, the Springheel brothers, had already begun preparing the victory meal—mussel stew from the beds they'd just saved, seasoned with satisfaction as much as spice. The big cousins and little cousins would eat from the same pot, their biological differences irrelevant compared to their shared accomplishment.

"Next time," Rem said, his massive frame somehow looking sheepish, "we install the pulley system permanently."

"Next time," Voss countered, wringing industrial water from his hair, "we post guards to keep poachers from dropping their lines in our water."

But they both knew there would always be a next time, always be problems that required both strength and subtlety, always be challenges that no single biology could overcome. The Haskets had survived by recognizing this, by building family from necessity rather than blood, by understanding that in the Middens, adaptation meant more than just accepting differences—it meant weaponizing them against a world that would crush any single approach.

The pump hummed its healthy rhythm five levels below, circulating water through beds of engineered mussels, maintaining the delicate balance of salinity and toxicity that produced food from industrial runoff. The clan had saved eight hundred pounds of protein through cooperation that couldn't be documented, couldn't be replicated by those who insisted on pure lines, couldn't be understood except through lived experience of bodies that needed each other despite—or because of—their engineered differences.
