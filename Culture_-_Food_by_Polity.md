# Food Culture by Polity

---

## The Undying Empire

### The Flavor Architecture

Imperial cuisine is built on a triad: **marru**, **fermented greens**, and **fresh herbs**. 

Marru provides the sweet-funky-umami foundation. Fermented greens provide sour contrast. Fresh herbs—used abundantly, as a dish component rather than garnish—provide brightness. These three elements appear in different proportions across virtually every preparation. Understanding Imperial food means understanding how they balance.

**Marru** is fermented wheat paste with honey vinegar and bread mold. Sweet, salty, deeply savory, with a metallic funk reminiscent of bleu cheese. Used thick as a paste spooned over grain, or thinned into dipping sauce (three parts water to one part paste), or dissolved into broth. Every household keeps a pot of marru. Running out signals disorder.

**Fermented greens** are leafy vegetables preserved through lacto-fermentation. Sour, complex, ranging from mild to aggressively tangy depending on age. They provide contrast to marru's sweetness and cut through rich proteins.

**Fresh herbs** dominate: cilantro, parsley, mint, basil, culantro, green onions, wild oregano. They're used in quantities that other cuisines would consider excessive—a pile of herbs as a dish, not a scattering as garnish. The Vietnamese and Persian influences show here.

**Spices** appear sparingly: cumin, coriander, sesame, nigella, mustard seed, wild ginger seed (the local pepper—peppery and slightly floral). Westerner imports (cardamom, star anise) are expensive luxuries.

**Citrus** runs through everything—fresh juice, preserved peel, zest. Fundamental.

### Manna

For the devout, manna is the most virtuous food. Thin crisp scrolls, mushroomy-savory with notes of pandan or jasmine. It can be eaten alone (the ascetic choice), softened in broth (the contemplative breakfast), wrapped around fillings, or crumbled as textural element.

The flavor of manna—that mushroomy-pandan depth—complements the marru-herbs-citrus architecture in ways that make devout cuisine distinctive. A bowl of manna softening in marru broth with fresh herbs is the ya-Sattran equivalent of monastic simplicity: restrained, complete, requiring nothing else.

### Restraint as Principle

Ya-Sattran sophistication means knowing when to stop. Not small portions necessarily, but clarity—few elements, each distinct. A dish of cold noodles with thin marru and herbs. A plate with liver, fermented greens, and herb sauce in three separate sections, assembled in the mouth. A grain bowl with precisely five toppings.

The herb plate is fundamental: whole herbs, stems attached, arranged for picking up. Radishes, chilies, citrus wedges alongside. Thin marru for dipping. You assemble each bite yourself—some cilantro, some mint, dip in marru, bite a radish, squeeze citrus. The restraint is in the preparation; the composition is yours.

Temperature matters: cold noodles, room-temperature salads, warm broths. Contrast is intentional.

### Regional Variation: Ya-Tsatsa

Northern ya-Tsatsa runs heavier: dairy-rich, mushroom-forward, beef-based. The triad still operates but the proportions shift—more fermented dairy (doogh, yogurt, smen), less fresh herb brightness. Morel dumplings. Beef and potato stew. Fiddlehead ferns with fresh cheese.

Dill appears here, marking the transition toward Thousand Kingdoms influence.

### What You Eat and What It Means

Choosing manna publicly is a visible statement about priorities and self-control. Cold noodles at lunch signal you don't need heavy fuel because you're not doing physical labor. Organ meats with herb sauce signal refined taste—appreciating what others might discard. The herb plate signals hospitality and abundance.

### When You Need to Eat a Lot

Labor castes have dramatically higher caloric requirements—some need four times baseline intake. The problem isn't access to food; it's making volume palatable. Eating enormous quantities of bland starch is miserable. Your body needs fuel but your mouth gets tired. The solution: the same triad deployed differently.

**Fermented greens become essential.** At high-calorie volumes, they're not garnish but digestive necessity. The sourness cuts through fat and resets the palate. Without the acid, starch and oil become cloying after the first thousand calories. You can't keep eating. With fermented greens piled on every bowl, you can.

**Fat is calorie density.** Ghee stirred into porridge. Oil drizzled over everything. Fried crispy bits scattered on top. You cannot physically eat enough volume to hit your calories without fat—your stomach would burst first. The fat makes it possible.

**Marru by the ladleful.** At this scale, marru isn't a condiment spooned on top. It's mixed into the porridge itself, a seasoning base that makes starch taste like food instead of paste. The sweet-funky depth carries through every bite.

**Cheap proteins in bulk.** Organ scraps from butchers. Small oily fish fried whole. Mussels from the tidal canals—dense, slightly metallic, intense with iodine. Mashed legumes. Whatever's affordable in quantity. The protein stretches through the starch.

**Texture keeps you going.** When you're eating continuously, variation matters. Crispy fried barley in soft porridge. Chewy fermented greens against smooth starch. Crunchy bits of fried fish skin. Something different in each bite prevents the monotony that makes you stop.

The communal pot never stops simmering. Individual households can't maintain fuel for constant cooking, but a shared pot amortizes energy costs across hundreds of meals. The base is barley or lentil porridge, miso-enriched, fat-laden. Contributions of ingredients, fuel, or labor earn meal portions. A handful of mussels might get you a bowl; a salvaged sack of grain feeds your family for a week.

The stew layers ingredients for different biologies. Some castes digest things others can't—certain algaes, cellulose-heavy vegetables, proteins that would pass through baseline systems unprocessed. The base works for everyone; the additions let each person get what their body needs.

**The portable version**: thick flatbread stuffed with mashed legumes, fermented greens, and ghee. Dense enough to carry calories, sour enough to stay appetizing, eaten one-handed while working. Workers carry these wrapped in cloth, eating continuously through shifts.

Industrial vendors serve by caloric value, not dish name. You order "four thousand" and receive whatever combination adds up—a deep bowl of enriched porridge, fermented greens piled high, cheap protein scattered over, crispy bits on top. You eat it standing, spoon in one hand, bowl in the other. It's not elegant. It's engineered to be eaten in volume without your palate giving up.

### The Ingredients of Necessity

The miso comes from koji grown on lentils and barley swept from the docks—cargo spillage from Westerner ships. The vinegar comes from distillery mash diverted before it reaches the still. Chiles float in the vinegar, slowly breaking down into paste. Small fish are mashed whole in brine, fermenting into an intense sauce that goes with everything. Honey provides sweetness and preservation. Mussels grow in the canals, adapted to tolerate what flows through them.

Pigeons and songbirds caught in nets get boiled whole until the meat falls from the bones. The bones strain out; what remains is almost a porridge of meat, stretched with grain, flavored with every herb and spice available. The herb gardens grow what they've always grown: cilantro, culantro, parsley, basil. The fermented greens come from whatever leafy vegetables can be preserved. The uni gets cracked fresh from urchins in the tidal pools.

This cuisine resembles Southeast Asian port food—Vietnamese, Filipino coastal traditions. Fish sauce, vinegar, whole-animal utilization, the aggressive flavors needed to make volume eating sustainable. The same triad operates: fermented depth, sour contrast, fresh herb brightness. The proportions shift toward intensity because subtlety doesn't survive the third bowl.

### Grapes and Wine

Imperial wine runs dry. Crisp whites, austere reds—sweetness is for Ganat. The vineyards climb the hills around ya-Sattra and spread through the inland valleys, producing wines meant to accompany food rather than overwhelm it.

**Table grapes** appear as almost a vegetable. Crisp green grapes, chilled, served with fresh herbs—mint, basil—as a palate cleanser between courses or as a light dish in themselves. The sweetness refreshes rather than satiates.

**Brandy** from grape distillation, unoaked and clean. Also **barley whisky**—mash fermented and distilled, sometimes aged, sometimes not. The whisky tradition is old; the techniques vary by producer and region.

**Beer** from barley, the common drink. Lighter styles for daily consumption, stronger for occasions. Every district has its brewers.

### Other Beverages

**Maté** grows in the Empire and appears constantly—the mild stimulant that fuels work and conversation. Served in gourds, passed and shared. Much of the crop exports to Ganat, where it's preferred but can't be grown locally.

**Coca tea**: imported from the south, another stimulant option. Cheaper than maté in some districts, more expensive in others depending on trade routes.

**Westerner tea**: available where Westerner trade reaches. A luxury import.

**Bloodfruit sharbat** for those with Yarangar connections—the preserved juice mixed with water, intensely flavored, deep purple-red.

**Doogh**: fermented milk with cucumber, mint, cumin. Refreshing, slightly sour, served cold at formal meals.

**Citrus juice**: fresh-squeezed, often mixed with water, sometimes sweetened with honey.

## Republic of Ganat

### The Bioengineered Bounty

Ganati agriculture runs on Antediluvian crops—bioengineered cultivars designed before the Interdict for colonization programs that never happened. These plants were meant to feed Serrulata settlers on other worlds. When the Interdict stranded the colonist caste on Earth, the crops came with them.

The yields are impossible by conventional standards. The plants tolerate desert conditions, use water with brutal efficiency, produce harvests that would require twice the acreage under baseline cultivation. They breed true without drifting. They don't hybridize with local species. They grow where planted, yield what they were designed to yield, and wait for colonists who will never leave Earth.

This bounty shapes everything about Ganati food. Scarcity isn't the problem—distribution is. The agricultural heartland produces surplus. The pastoral plains produce different surplus. The trade cities redistribute. The frontiers experiment with stranger cultivars. Each region eats differently because each region grows differently.

### The Pastoral Plains

The chilmole tradition comes from here—the Serrulata pastoralists who move their herds through seasonal rounds across the high plains. Places like Ghemar-yan, where three thousand people overwinter and five thousand gather when the bands return from southern ranges.

**Chilmole is the cuisine, and chilmole is the argument.** Every family has their recipe. The debates about fat type, meat inclusion, spice selection, and whether marru belongs in proper chilmole—these happen around fires in felt yurts, in winter quarters carved into riverbanks, at summer markets where pastoral bands trade wool and leather and destrier foals for goods they can't produce mobile.

The pastoral diet centers on what travels: dried goat, goat fat, goat butter, fermented mare's milk. Flatbread made from traded grain. Dried fruit—raisins especially—that rehydrates in stews. The bioengineered grain from the agricultural zones reaches the pastoralists through trade, already processed, ready to become bread or porridge.

At market towns, pastoralists trade animal products for grain, cloth, metal tools, and the luxuries that make life bearable: sugar when available, Westerner tea when the traders have it, dried chiles that have become essential to the cuisine. The trading happens in Republican copper and bronze coins but also through barter and credit arrangements between families who've dealt for generations.

The food is communal. Stews in shared pots. Flatbread torn and passed. Everyone eating from the same dishes because that's how you eat when you're moving together, when the whole band depends on each other, when hoarding is shameful and generosity is survival.

### Lake Ganat and the Upper Canyon

The agricultural heartland looks nothing like the pastoral plains.

Lake Ganat stretches thirty miles through the upper canyon—a reservoir created when the Dam was built in some forgotten Record. The farming villages around it are the wealthiest in the Republic. The explanation is simple: fertilizer, irrigation, and bioengineered crops that produce impossible yields.

Half a million people in Ganat Proper produce sewage continuously. For millennia, the city's sanitation infrastructure has processed this waste and shipped it upstream as fertilizer. The fields around Lake Ganat are dark, rich, fertile—not naturally but through systematic soil transformation. Irrigation networks draw from the lake through ancient infrastructure integrated with modern additions. The combination of enriched soil and reliable water makes the canyon capable of intensive agriculture.

The crops themselves are Serrulata-associated cultivars: bioengineered plants that tolerate the desert conditions, use water efficiently, and yield abundantly with proper fertilization. Nobody knows how the engineering was done or could reproduce it, but the plants breed true and produce reliably.

The lake supports fishing too. Catfish, carp, tilapia-equivalents—species introduced long ago that thrive in the reservoir. Some are stranger: engineered fish whose origins are unknown but whose flesh is nutritious.

**The food here is fresh in ways the pastoral plains never see.** Vegetables from the irrigated fields. Fish from the lake. Fruit from the orchards. The bioengineered cultivars produce vegetables unlike baseline crops—larger, more nutritious, strange textures and flavors that outsiders find unfamiliar. The agricultural kilits eat well because they grow well.

The relationship with Ganat Proper is symbiotic but tense. The city provides fertilizer, infrastructure, markets. The canyon provides food. But the farmers resent being secondary to urban industrial importance, and city residents think of farmers as conservative and parochial. Both are partly right.

### Ganat Proper

The dam-city itself eats what comes up from the canyon and what comes in through trade.

Five hundred thousand people carved into cliff faces around the Dam. Power too cheap to meter—the working-class warrens honeycomb the rock, and the city never sleeps because energy is free and stopping costs opportunity. The markets in Near Wall sell what the upper canyon produces alongside what traders bring from elsewhere.

The cuisine here mixes pastoral tradition with agricultural abundance. Chilmole appears, but so do fresh vegetables and lake fish. The bioengineered grains become flatbreads and porridges. The hookah dens where political factions argue serve food alongside cannabis and tobacco—small plates, shared dishes, the communal eating tradition adapted to urban density.

Street food matters. Workers eating between shifts. The constant churn of a city that operates around the clock. Vendors selling flatbreads stuffed with beans and chilmole, grilled cactus with charred onions, fried fish from the lake, whatever can be eaten quickly and cheaply.

### Taho

Everything is cheap in Taho because everything passes through.

Seven hundred thousand people on granite slopes above Lake Tahoe. The city controls Truckee Pass—the only crossing for hundreds of miles between Ganat and the Empire. Every trade route funnels through. Imperial goods, Westerner goods, whatever the eastern Marches produce, whatever the pastoral bands sell—all of it flows through Taho's markets.

The result is culinary diversity impossible elsewhere in Ganat. Imperial marru is common and affordable. Westerner spices appear in quantities that would be luxuries anywhere else. Ya-Sattran fermented greens, Thousand Kingdoms smoked fish, the strange preserves from the eastern trade—all available in the Port District markets.

Taho cuisine is fusion by necessity. Chilmole made with marru (the argument settled here by availability). Flatbreads with Imperial herb sauces. Stews incorporating ingredients from three different trade networks. The Dusk merchant kilits who dominate commerce eat eclectically, displaying wealth through variety. The working classes benefit from the overflow—when luxury goods are common, even laborers eat better than their counterparts elsewhere.

The lake itself—four hundred feet lower than ancient records describe—still provides fish. The terraced farms on the slopes produce vegetables. But Taho's food identity is trade, not production. You eat what comes through.

### The Marches

The seven frontier territories east of core Ganat grow stranger things.

The Marcher lords maintain serf populations working Antediluvian crops designed for colonization that never happened. These aren't the standard bioengineered cultivars of the upper canyon. These are the experimental variants: a grain that stores indefinitely without processing, a legume that fixes nitrogen aggressively enough to reclaim exhausted soil in a single season, root vegetables that produce tubers the size of a man's head, fibrous plants yielding material between hemp and cotton.

The crops were designed for Serrulata colonists on other worlds. The specifications assumed Serrulata labor, Serrulata dietary requirements, Serrulata heat tolerance. The Dawn Party's land-grant programs are expanding cultivation of these variants, pushing into territories where conventional agriculture fails.

**The food is practical rather than refined.** The giant tubers get roasted whole in pits, sliced and fried, mashed into dense porridges. The never-spoiling grain becomes hard cakes that last through any journey. The aggressive legumes appear in every stew because they're abundant and nutritious. The flavors are unfamiliar—these crops weren't designed for baseline human palates—but the nutrition is exceptional.

Marcher cuisine borrows from pastoral traditions (the serf populations include Serrulata, the lords employ Akama mercenaries) while adapting to the strange crops. Chilmole made with Antediluvian legumes instead of standard beans. Giant tuber stews with dried fruit. Hard grain cakes eaten with whatever preserved meat is available.

The eastern trade adds mystery. The Marches conduct commerce with unknown entities beyond the Dead Cities. Goods appear whose origins nobody can identify. Some of these become food—preserved items, spices, dried things that might be fruit or might be something else. The lords don't explain and the serfs don't ask.

### The Organizing Principles

Across all regions, certain patterns hold:

**Chilmole remains central.** Even where fresh vegetables are abundant, even where trade brings variety, the concentrated chile-meat paste appears. It's identity food—what makes Ganati cuisine Ganati regardless of local variation.

**Communal eating persists.** Shared pots, torn flatbread, everyone eating from the same dishes. The pastoral origin shows even in urban contexts. Individual plating is Imperial affectation.

**Sweet-savory from dried fruit.** Raisins, apricots, dates—rehydrating in stews, providing sweetness against chile heat. This appears everywhere, from pastoral camps to Taho's merchant tables.

**The bioengineered crops change everything.** The yields mean abundance rather than scarcity. The strange flavors mean Ganati food tastes like nothing else. The reliability means food security that other regions envy.

### Beverages

Ganati wine runs sweet—the opposite of Imperial. Muscats and big muscadines dominate, producing wines with residual sugar that would scandalize ya-Sattran vintners. The same grapes dry into raisins, the sweetness concentrating.

**Spiced wine**: vanilla bean and citrus peel steeped in the sweet wine. Common at celebrations, warming in winter.

**Pisco**: the primary liquor. Grape brandy, unoaked, clean and fierce. Distilled from the muscat wines, retaining some floral character. This is what pastoral bands carry; this is what gets drunk at markets; this is what seals agreements.

**Flavored brandies**: pisco infused with vanilla, citrus peel, sometimes chiles. These travel with the pastoralists.

**Raspa**: shaved ice with prickly pear juice and lime. The Ganati obsession. Requires ice, which means either trade with the north or salvaged refrigeration—the combination of intense desert heat and access to ice makes it a status display.

**Fermented mare's milk**: the pastoral staple. Slightly alcoholic, sour, made on the move from your horses. The settled populations buy this from pastoralists who come through.

### Stimulants

**Maté** is the preferred stimulant in Ganat, but it has to be imported from across the Empire—it doesn't grow in the desert. The trade routes that bring marru also bring maté. Expensive in the Marches, common in Taho.

**Coca tea**: imported from the south, cheaper than maté in most of Ganat. The default stimulant for workers who can't afford maté prices.

**Westerner tea**: available in Taho, rare elsewhere. A luxury that marks trade wealth.

## Thousand Kingdoms (Akama)

### The Processing Happened Earlier

Akama cuisine makes sense once you understand: the intensive work already happened. 

**Kavdak** is salmon fermented and reduced to concentrated paste—weeks of processing before it reaches the kitchen. **Tabanak** is blackberry-blood sauce reduced until leathery and shelf-stable. Meat was smoked days or weeks ago. Fish was dried. Berries were preserved.

By the time you're cooking, you're combining things that are already complex. So the cooking is simple. Kavdak dissolves into water; you pour it over barley. Smoked meat rehydrates in the broth. Tabanak softens in warm water and becomes sauce. You don't need elaborate technique because the technique already happened.

### Village Food

Villages are where the preservation happens.

During the salmon runs, everyone works: catching, gutting, smoking, fermenting. The kavdak production happens now—salmon broken down through weeks of koji fermentation and phenol preservation until it becomes the concentrated paste that will flavor meals all year. At slaughter time, the smoking fires burn continuously. Meat hangs in smokehouses until it's shelf-stable. Berries get reduced with blood into tabanak. This is the labor that makes Akama cuisine possible.

The rest of the year, villages eat what they preserved plus what they grow and forage.

**Root cellars** hold turnips, carrots, parsnips, camas bulbs. These last through winter, roasted or added to stews.

**Cabbage** marks Akama cooking the way chard marks Imperial. Fresh cabbage shredded into salads. Pickled cabbage in crocks. Fermented cabbage with its sour funk. Cabbage in everything.

**Fiddlehead ferns** when they come up in spring—blanched and dressed with fish oil, or added to barley.

**Dairy**: koumis (fermented mare's milk, slightly alcoholic, sour), sour cream, aged cheese. The dairy tradition runs deep.

**Foraged abundance**: wild onions, ramps, nettles, sorrel, mushrooms (morels, chanterelles, matsutake), hazelnuts. What the land provides, eaten simply.

The home meal is the structure established earlier: kavdak broth over barley, smoked meat rehydrated, foraged greens dressed with fat, tabanak as sauce. Complex preserved concentrates plus simple fresh ingredients. Dill runs through everything.

### Feast Food

Villages host raiders.

This is tribute, hospitality, insurance—some combination that varies by village and warband. When riders come through, the village feeds them. This means villages maintain *good* preserved food specifically for these occasions. The kavdak aged an extra year. The best cuts of smoked meat. The tabanak made with care rather than necessity. Koumis in quantity.

The feast economy shapes what villages produce. You can't just make enough for your family; you need surplus for when the warband arrives. A village known for poor hospitality becomes a village known for being raided rather than hosted. The quality of your preserved food is a form of defense.

Feast dishes are village dishes made abundant: the barley pilaf piled high with shredded smoked beef, the tabanak sauce flowing freely, the pickled cabbage and fermented vegetables in variety, the koumis drunk until everyone's loose. The food is the same; the generosity is the message.

### Raider Food

On the move, warbands eat differently.

**Kavdak packets**: dried paste wrapped in leaves or cloth. Dissolve in hot water for broth. Or cold water if you can't risk a fire. Or chew it straight if you're desperate—intensely salty, concentrated nutrition.

**Dried meat**: chewed as-is, slowly, over hours of riding. Or quickly rehydrated in whatever liquid you have.

**Hard barley cakes**: dense, dry, nearly indestructible. Soak in broth to soften or gnaw at them raw.

**Tabanak**: eaten straight as trail food. Leathery, tart, calorie-dense. The name means "bootsole" for a reason.

**Koumis**: fermented from your horses' milk. Made on the move. The one fresh thing.

**Foraged supplements**: whatever you find while riding. Berries in season. Wild onions. Mushrooms if you know them.

Fires give away position, so raiders often eat cold. Kavdak dissolved in stream water. Dried meat chewed without cooking. The food is designed for this—everything portable, everything edible without preparation, everything concentrated enough to sustain hard riding.

But raider life cycles between deprivation and abundance. Days of cold trail rations, then arrival at a village and a feast. The oscillation is part of the experience—you eat badly so that you can eat well.

### Ogon Food

Ogon is different: a city of a million that can't feed itself.

The salmon processing happens here at industrial scale. Ataru Blackfish controls eight hundred warehouses. During the runs, the whole city works the catch—gutting, smoking, fermenting, packing. This is where kavdak gets made for export, shipped south to the Empire, east to the interior villages, north to wherever it goes. The city smells like fish and smoke for months.

But Ogon imports most of what it eats. Grain from the interior. Vegetables from farms along the Columbia. Trade goods through the Grand Canal terminus. A million people require more food than the local fishery and the rainy coastal climate can provide.

**Fresh fish** is common in ways it isn't inland. Salmon, obviously, but also the coastal species that don't travel well. Eaten fresh rather than preserved because you can.

**Ferns**: the wet climate grows them abundantly. Fiddleheads, bracken, sword fern shoots. More fern-eating here than anywhere else in the Thousand Kingdoms.

**Cabbage**: grows well in the damp. Appears constantly.

**The rain shapes everything.** Two hundred days a year, everything perpetually damp. Food storage is different—rot is the enemy more than drying out. Smoking works; air-drying doesn't. Fermentation happens fast in the wet air. The cuisine adapted to constant moisture.

**Kma-Dhol food**: forty thousand sewer-fishers live here openly as technical specialists, and they brought their food traditions. The same miso-and-vinegar-and-fish-sauce complex that operates in ya-Sattra's undercity appears here, adapted to local ingredients. Kma-Dhol cuisine is its own thread within Ogon eating—more fermented, more sour, more intense.

**Urban variety**: trade through the terminus means ingredients from everywhere. Ganati chiles. Imperial marru. Dried goods from across the Thousand Kingdoms. The city eats more diversely than the interior because trade makes diversity possible.

The result is something neither fully Akama nor fully anything else. Fresh fish and industrial kavdak production. Imported grain and local ferns. Kma-Dhol fermentation and raider-descended feast traditions. Ogon food is hybrid, urban, shaped by rain and trade and a million people living where the autofactory sprawls for eighty miles.

### Beverages and Drinking Culture

**Koumis** (fermented mare's milk) is fundamental—the same tradition as Ganati pastoralists, sour and mildly alcoholic, made on the move. Villages maintain dairy herds; raider bands make koumis from their horses.

**But feasting means liquor, and liquor means trade.** The Akama buy alcohol in quantity: pisco from Ganat, whisky from the Empire, whatever flows through Ogon's terminus. The drinking culture is extravagant by outside standards—huge quantities consumed at feasts, at gambling sessions, at the rituals of hospitality between warbands and villages.

The feast without drinking isn't a feast. When villages host raiders, the alcohol flows as freely as the food. When warbands meet to settle disputes, drinking together precedes and follows the fighting. Drunkenness is expected, tolerated, part of the social fabric. The stoic image of Akama warriors applies on the trail; at the feast-fire, restraint is almost insulting.

**Stimulants**: coca tea appears where trade brings it—cheaper than in Ganat, because the routes run through Ogon. Maté is rarer and more expensive. Westerner tea is a curiosity.

### The Palette

**Grains**: barley primarily  
**Proteins**: salmon (central), smoked meats, blood, fresh coastal fish in Ogon  
**Vegetables**: cabbage, root vegetables (carrots, turnips, parsnips, camas), ferns (especially coastal)  
**Foraged**: wild onions, ramps, nettles, sorrel, mushrooms (morels, chanterelles, matsutake), hazelnuts, berries  
**Preserved concentrates**: kavdak (salmon paste), tabanak (blackberry-blood sauce)  
**Dairy**: koumis, sour cream, aged cheese  
**Fats**: fish oil, butter, bacon fat  
**Seasonings**: dill (the signature herb), cumin, wild ginger seed, dried Ganati chiles (imported luxury)

## Asovoë (Avouvar)

### The Radiation Pantry

Asovoë is intensely radioactive. The contamination that gives the Avouvar their centuries-long lifespans also kills all microorganisms. Food that enters the city stops changing. Lettuce stays crisp. Meat doesn't rot. Pickled cabbage remains pickled forever. Nothing spoils; nothing ferments.

This means:
- Fermentation is impossible locally. All marru, kavdak, cheese, pickled vegetables must arrive already processed.
- Fermented imports freeze at the moment of entry. They won't develop further.
- Fresh ingredients persist indefinitely. Herbs stay fresh. Radishes stay crisp. For years.
- The only variable is time since arrival.

### Wealth, Not Scarcity

The Avouvar are rich. They control autofactory outputs—alloys and components available nowhere else—and trade from strength. Food arrives in abundance through the San Juan factorias. The famine winters were about access (harbor freezing), not poverty.

This wealth plus radiation-preservation creates a flattened gradient. When everything costs roughly the same to transport and nothing spoils, the only meaningful distinction is freshness—how recently something arrived.

### The Grammar: Kavdak and Marru

Avouvar cooking follows a structure borrowed from Japanese cuisine but built with Polish ingredients.

**Kavdak** functions as dashi—the base broth. Salmon paste dissolved in hot water creates amber, smoky, intensely savory liquid. This is the foundation.

**Marru** functions as miso—stirred in at the end for sweet-funky depth. Kavdak provides base; marru finishes.

Over this architecture: pearl barley, cabbage, radishes, peas, dill, butter. The Polish thread. Dark bowls for contrast against ink-black Avouvar hands. Pickled vegetables arranged in distinct sections. Laver and dried scallop for oceanic notes.

### The Standard Bowl

Centuries of refinement produced exact proportions. The ratio of kavdak to marru. The amount of butter. Which pickled vegetables, arranged how, in what quantities. Five hundred years of adjustment—including the famine winters—produced this.

The Standard Bowl isn't a recipe; it's a consensus. This is what we eat. This is how we eat it.

### The Dock Meal

Freshness is the only luxury.

Food eaten within hours of cargo arrival—before radiation fully penetrates—still has living microbiome. Enzymes remain active. Bread still has yeast character. Herbs have enzymatic brightness. Cheese is still ripening.

Racing the radiation to eat something still metabolically alive is the true Avouvar luxury. You time your meal to the cargo schedule. You meet the ship. You eat within two hours. The conversation centers on identifying what still tastes alive versus what has begun stabilizing.

Guests who arrive late taste the difference. This is the point.

### The Continuity of Form

What can be said about food during the Second Anointing—the famine winters covered by eternal silence—is this: the Avouvar choose not to lose their humanity.

The same dark plates. The same careful arrangement. The same documentation of what is being eaten and where it came from. The conversation continues—about the meal, about the presentation, about anything except what everyone knows. The ritual holds because if the ritual breaks, something else breaks with it.

This is the origin of Avouvar food culture's precision. The Standard Bowl wasn't refined across five centuries of plenty. It was refined across five centuries that included those winters. The proportions were worked out at tables where maintaining the form was how you remained a person.

Elders who survived the same winter recognize each other partly because they sat at those tables together. They remember who kept the conversation going. They remember who arranged the plates correctly when it would have been easier not to.

When visitors observe that Avouvar cuisine seems excessively formal—too much attention to placement, too much discussion of provenance, too much care—they are not wrong. They are simply unaware of what the formality is for. The Avouvar do not explain. But they do not simplify either, even in plenty, because the habit of civilization is the point. You practice it when it's easy so that you can maintain it when it's not.

### The Archival Plate

For formal occasions, the Avouvar compose plates documenting provenance. Pickled radishes from year 412. Cabbage from year 398. Each component labeled with its arrival year.

This descended from famine-winter meals where documentation wasn't aesthetic showing-off but insistence: this is food, it has a history, we are people eating together. Now it serves as temporal archaeology—tasting across decades, arguing about whether the 389 butter differs from the 401 butter.

The Avouvar palate, refined across centuries, detects distinctions that visitors cannot. Or claims to. The argument is part of the meal.

## Cross-Cultural Notes

### The Organizing Principles

**Ya-Sattra**: Restraint. The triad of marru + fermented greens + fresh herbs. Assembly at the table. Few elements, each distinct.

**Ganat**: Regional variation across a republic. Pastoral plains produce the chilmole tradition—portable, communal, what you can carry. Lake Ganat's agricultural heartland eats fresh fish and bioengineered vegetables. Taho's trade gateway makes everything cheap and fusion inevitable. The Marches grow stranger Antediluvian crops—giant tubers, never-spoiling grain—and eat frontier food shaped by what the colonization program left behind. Bioengineered yields mean abundance; the debates are about identity, not scarcity.

**Thousand Kingdoms**: The processing happened earlier. Village food is where preservation happens—kavdak made during salmon runs, meat smoked at slaughter, then combined with foraged and stored ingredients. Feast food for hosting raiders shapes production. Raider food is portable concentrates eaten cold on the move, cycling with village feasts. Ogon food is urban, industrial, import-dependent, shaped by rain and the Kma-Dhol presence. Cabbage marks Akama cooking the way chard marks Imperial.

**Asovoë**: Precision descended from famine. Kavdak + marru architecture. Freshness as the only luxury. The habit of civilization maintained because if it breaks, something else breaks with it.

### Trade and Influence

Marru flows everywhere. It's fundamental in the Empire, cheap and common in Taho (where the trade routes converge), debated among the pastoralists (does it belong in chilmole?), used as finishing element in Asovoë, occasionally appearing in Thousand Kingdoms cooking.

Kavdak originates in the Thousand Kingdoms but reaches full expression in Asovoë, where it serves as dashi-equivalent foundation.

Dried Ganati chiles travel north into the Thousand Kingdoms and Asovoë as trade luxury. The pastoral bands produce them; Taho distributes them.

The bioengineered crops don't travel well as seeds—they breed true only in their home territories—but processed products (never-spoiling grain, dried legumes, giant tuber flour) appear in trade networks, adding strange Antediluvian flavors to cuisines far from the Marches.

Wild ginger seed serves as the local pepper throughout the region.

### What Fat Means

Goat fat and goat butter in Ganat. Butter and fish oil in the Thousand Kingdoms. Ghee and neutral oil in ya-Sattra. Aged butter in Asovoë (stored for years, flavor stabilized). Each fat changes the cuisine fundamentally.

### Communal vs. Individual

Ganati food is communal—shared pots, flatbread for scooping, everyone eating together. Akama food portions from common pots into individual bowls. Ya-Sattran food tends toward individual compositions. Avouvar food varies: the Standard Bowl is individual, but formal occasions involve shared archival plates.

### Time and Food

Each cuisine has a different relationship with time.

Ya-Sattra values freshness—the herbs were picked today, the fermentation is active, the citrus just squeezed. Everything is happening now.

Ganat values portability—the chilmole was made last month, the raisins dried last year, the techniques unchanged for generations. Time compresses into what you can carry.

Thousand Kingdoms values the work that happened earlier—the smoking took weeks, the fermentation took months, the foraging happened this morning. Past and present combine.

Asovoë exists outside time. The pickles from seventeen years ago taste like they did seventeen years ago. The only luxury is freshness—the brief window when something is still alive. The cuisine is archaeology, memory, and the insistence that meals matter even when time doesn't.
