# Akama Material Culture

**Core intention:** "I was here. This happened. It mattered. Notice."

Objects speak for individuals across mortal timescales. Material culture is testimonial.

---

## Philosophy

**"Life promises nothing."** The Akama philosophy of fate and strength and endurance. The tent doesn't promise to last forever. You don't promise to survive. Material culture doesn't pretend otherwise. A patched tent is more truthful than a pristine one.

**"It is enough nonetheless."** Sufficiency, not optimization. "Enough" is the highest praise. More than enough is suspicious—who are you trying to impress?

**"There is a quality to this thing. View it."** Craftsmanship as testimony. Quality isn't luxury; it's respect for the work. A shoddy object insults its maker and its user.

**"This was the great thing I did."** Objects as evidence of deeds. Your armor tells your story. Your tack tells your story. You are your own chronicle.

**"This was my greatest misfortune. I bore it with honor."** Damage not hidden but displayed. The mended thing is more honest than the pristine thing.

## Armor and Equipment

**Armor as biography:**
- Displayed outside tents—not storage, but testimony
- History visible: patches, replacement plates, modifications for different bodies across generations
- Layered ceramic plates over destrier-leather (echoing Aztec quilted cotton)
- Captured pieces integrated, origin visible

**The young warrior's embarrassment:**
- Everything too new, armor pristine with no patches
- Leather stiff, no wear patterns
- Can't fake patina—the newness testifies against him
- Nothing has happened to him yet

**Threat decoration:**
- Diagonal slashes in the three colors
- Captured weapons displayed
- Trophies from defeated enemies

---

## Destrier Tack

Equipment maintained across generations, repaired with whatever available:

- Saddle old—leather cracked and reconditioned multiple times
- Stirrups often don't match: one local ironwork, one Imperial manufacture
- Bridle assembled from three different original sets
- Each repair tells a story of where they were, what was available

## Personal Objects

**A khan's drinking bowl:**
- Bronze, ya-Don manufacture—a trophy
- Dented on one side from the blow that killed its previous owner
- Rim worn smooth from generations of lips
- Base inscribed: names crossed out, current owner's name last
- The bowl is a document

**Weapons:**
- Inherited blades with multiple hilts across generations
- Repair history visible in blade geometry
- Captured weapons kept in original condition (testimony to victory)

## Territorial Marking

**"I was here":**
- Names carved into rocks at campsites
- Marks on canyon walls
- Diagonal paint slashes on autofactory concrete
- The Antediluvians didn't need to say "I was here" because everyone who mattered already knew
- The Akama know nothing is eternal, so you leave marks

**Graffiti tradition:**
- Even in Imperial territory: "Names and dates carved into bronze, some dating to the Record of the Black Star or earlier"
- Someone wrote 'STILL WAITING' recently
- The testimonial urge persists everywhere Akama go

---

## Funeral Practice

**A camp after a death:**
1. Dead warrior's tent still standing, flap pinned open—the spirit needs to leave
2. Armor displayed more prominently than in life
3. Destrier decorated with fabric strips
4. For three days the camp organizes around the empty tent
5. Then packed, distributed, strips kept as mementos

**Inheritance:**
- Equipment passes to those who can use it
- Names added to objects, previous owners' names remain
- The object's history continues through new hands

## Generating New Details

*Example: A trader's satchel.* Leather worn pale at the edges, darker where hands grip. Three different patches—one local hide, one Imperial leather, one something unidentifiable. The buckle doesn't match the strap hardware. Inside: a knife with a blade honed thin from twenty years of sharpening, a cup with a crack sealed with pine pitch, documents in a waterproof case that's the only new thing he owns. The satchel is older than he is.

*Example: A warband's camp.* No two tents identical—each shows its owner's history. The oldest tent, patched seventeen times, belongs to the warband's founder. The newest belongs to a warrior who joined last season; he's added a single patch (probably self-inflicted) to start his tent's story. Armor frames outside each tent display ceramic plates in different patterns—some geometric, some bearing family marks, some showing damage from specific battles the warriors will tell you about if asked.
