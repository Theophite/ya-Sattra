# Khesh-Dalan: Revolutionary Ethics in Antediluvian Ruins

## Executive Summary

Khesh-Dalan sits in the ruins of an Antediluvian complex near former Rexburg, Idaho, at 5,000 feet where the Teph River (formerly Henrys Fork) cuts through high plateau. Population fluctuates between 200 and 400 depending on who's counting and whether you include the pastoral bands ranging nearby. The village is entirely Akamaâ€”formerly tribute-paying villagers and their former overlords trying to live together after both embraced revolutionary principles. Captain Sekh Tal-Omir, Serrulata revolutionary veteran from Ghemar-yan, visits regularly trying to navigate Ministry of Industry bureaucracy to monetize the site before economic pressures collapse the entire delicate social experiment. ## The Antediluvian Complex

## The Village Before

Khesh-Dalan was Akama tribute village, one of dozens scattered through the eastern Malpais surviving through calibrated submission. Maybe a hundred people living in and around the Antediluvian structures, growing what they could in small patches, herding cattle and sheep, paying tribute to whichever warband controlled the territory.

The complex provided advantages that made the village viable where others failed. The utilities meant warmth in winter without excessive fuel. The water outlets meant reliability when springs ran dry. The structures themselves provided shelter that would last indefinitely. You paid tribute with the surplus these advantages generatedâ€”not much, but enough to avoid being raided.

The village swore to multiple bands over generations. When power shifted, the headman would announce: "Khol-Maran's people are coming next week. We are opening the gates." The new overlord would arrive, tribute would be negotiated, life would continue. From the village perspective, warbands were like weatherâ€”dangerous, unpredictable, manageable through preparation and submission.

One of those overlords was Khol Thirteen-Hawk's band. Not largeâ€”maybe forty ridersâ€”and not particularly powerful. They held the territory through persistence more than dominance, extracting tribute from Khesh-Dalan and three other villages, ranging widely through surrounding highlands, practicing standard Akama philosophy about fate and strength and endurance.

Then Khol converted. Radical Penitent theology reached him through circuit preachers and Ganati exiles hiding on the frontier. The ideas transformed him: might doesn't make right, suffering isn't sacred trial, helping the weak isn't theft of their experience. He began preaching to his band, trying to reconcile Akama culture with revolutionary principles, arguing that true strength meant defending the weak rather than exploiting them.

His band split. Some riders left for more traditional khans. Others stayed, either convinced by his theology or loyal to him personally. When the Revolution came and the Sultanate called for auxiliary forces to fight in Yarangar, Khol's reduced band volunteered. They fought alongside Ganati forces, learned revolutionary organization, earned veteran status.

Khol died three winters agoâ€”sixty-seven years old, worn out, still trying to figure out how Akama could be revolutionary without ceasing to be Akama. His son Dranakh Nine-Hawk inherited leadership of the band and its impossible ethical burden.

## Dranakh's Decision

Dranakh Nine-Hawk is thirty-four now, born into his father's struggle to reconcile identities. He grew up hearing Penitent theology alongside Akama philosophy, revolutionary principles alongside tribute collection realities. He fought in the Revolution as a teenager, saw his father's ideals tested in combat, watched as revolutionary Akama tried to figure out what they were fighting for beyond overthrowing monarchy.

When his father died, Dranakh inherited twenty-eight ridersâ€”half the force Khol commanded at his peak. The band was poor, operating on marginal territory, struggling to maintain Akama lifestyle while rejecting Akama ethics. They couldn't extract tribute on principle, couldn't compete with traditional warbands for better territories, couldn't settle without abandoning culture.

Dranakh made the decision everyone thought was insane: move into Khesh-Dalan. Not as overlords extracting tribute but as residents sharing the village. Live alongside the people they used to oppress, defend them collectively, implement revolutionary principles in practical terms rather than abstract aspiration.

The village elders were suspicious initially. This looked like occupation disguised as egalitarianism, domination wearing revolutionary rhetoric. But Dranakh made concrete proposals: shared governance, collective defense, equal distribution of resources, no tribute. His band would contribute labor, provide military protection, participate as citizens rather than overlords. In return they'd receive what the village receivedâ€”shelter, food, security.

The elders negotiated carefully. What happens when winter comes and food runs short? Dranakh agreed to rationing applying equally to everyone. What happens when traditional warbands attack? Dranakh's riders defend everyone or no one. What happens when revolutionary principles conflict with Akama culture? They'd work it out collectively or fail collectively.

The band moved in two years ago. Population doubled overnightâ€”from a hundred villagers to a hundred villagers and thirty riders plus their families. Socially it worked better than expected. Former tribute-payers and former tribute-takers navigated the awkwardness through explicit discussion and shared labor. Everyone knew the history, everyone committed to transcending it, and everyone participated in building something different.

Economically it didn't pencil out. The village could barely support its original population. Doubling it meant food shortages, resource competition, infrastructure strain. The Antediluvian utilities helpedâ€”free heating, supplemental waterâ€”but couldn't create calories. They needed more grazing land, more irrigation, more of everything.

## The Social Dynamics

Morning Assembly happens in the largest Antediluvian structureâ€”forty feet by sixty feet, twelve-foot ceilings, ambient lighting from wall panels. Everyone attends who's in the village: elders from the original community, Dranakh's riders, pastoralists ranging nearby who consider Khesh-Dalan their anchor point. Maybe eighty people on average, more when seasonal patterns bring bands through.

The Assembly addresses practical questions: who's working which fields today, who's maintaining irrigation, who's riding patrol. But it also addresses the tension that underlies everything: how do former tribute-takers and tribute-payers actually live together?

The conversations are explicit because implicit accommodation failed immediately. Someone has to say: "When Dranakh tells me to do something, my first instinct is obedience because that's what you did when the warband spoke. We need to address that instinct because it undermines the whole project." And someone else has to say: "When villagers defer to me, my first instinct is acceptance because that's what being Akama meant. I need to catch myself, push back, insist on collective decision."

They've developed practices that make the equality visible. Decision-making alternates chairsâ€”one day an elder from the original village, next day one of Dranakh's riders. Distribution of scarce resources (meat, tea, ammunition when they have it) is done publicly with lots drawn. Labor assignments are posted where everyone sees them, and prestigious tasks rotate regardless of skill to prevent hierarchy reasserting through competence.

It's exhausting. Every interaction requires attention to power dynamics. Every decision must be explained, justified, verified as collective rather than imposed. The revolutionary principles are clear in theory but grinding in practice.

And it works, mostly. Two years in, people have learned each other's patterns. The original villagers recognize which riders are genuinely committed versus which are performing commitment. The riders recognize which villagers are participating versus which are still internally submitting. Friendships have formed. Marriages have happenedâ€”one between a rider and a village woman, celebrated as proof the project works.

But economic pressure is increasing. Last winter they rationed food strictly. The winter before was easier because they had reserves. This coming winter looks worse because summer was dry and yields were low. If they can't find income sources beyond subsistence agriculture and marginal herding, the whole project collapses and everyone scatters to wherever they can survive individually.

## Captain Sekh's Connection

They weren't close friendsâ€”different castes, different cultures, different worldviewsâ€”but they fought together, trusted each other in combat, developed respect that transcended differences.

Sekh watched Khol struggle with reconciling Akama identity and revolutionary ethics. She thought he was impossibly idealistic and genuinely admirable in equal measure.

When Khol died, Sekh attended the funeralâ€”three-day journey from Ghemar-yan, difficult in winter, but she owed him that respect. When Dranakh decided to move into Khesh-Dalan, Sekh thought it was disaster waiting to happen. But Dranakh was committed. And Sekh realized she had two choices: oppose the decision and preserve her pragmatism, or support it and help make impossible things work. She chose support because Khol's memory demanded it and because someone needed to connect this Akama revolutionary project to Republican resources that might save it.

## The Archaeological Problem

Sekh isn't an archaeologist. She's a soldier with academy education that included some technical training but nothing comprehensive. She can recognize Antediluvian manufacture, distinguish probable functionality from decorative elements, assess rough value based on condition and complexity. But she can't identify what specific devices are for, can't date construction phases, can't determine why this complex existed or what its relationship to other sites might be.

What she can see is that Khesh-Dalan is archaeologically rich in ways that should make it valuable. The artifact density is extreme. In two years of surveying, she's catalogued over three hundred objects that might be marketable, another thousand fragments that might be valuable if properly understood, and indications of larger systems that might be priceless if excavated properly.

If this were standard salvageâ€”battlefield debris, crashed vessels, abandoned infrastructureâ€”the process would be straightforward. She'd contact the Salvage Guild, they'd send evaluators, items would be authenticated and purchased at established rates, and the village would receive income proportional to find values minus Guild fees.

But this isn't salvage. This is intact complex of unclear purpose in territory that's not technically Republican. The Salvage Guild won't operate here because jurisdiction is ambiguous. Private merchants will buy artifacts but at exploitative prices because sellers have no authentication or market knowledge. Southern kilits with archaeological expertise could contract for proper extraction, but they'll structure deals that cheat Akama villagers who lack legal standing or negotiating power.

Sekh needs someone with impeccable leftist credentials who can navigate Republican bureaucracy to arrange proper archaeological evaluation and fair compensation. Someone who cares about revolutionary ethics enough to prioritize helping Akama veterans over profit maximization. Someone with Ministry of Industry connections who can cut through jurisdictional ambiguity.

Her correspondence network is sparse. She knows people in the Ministryâ€”former military colleagues who transitioned to civilian archaeological work, academy contacts who entered government service, fellow veterans who ended up in bureaucratic positions. She writes to them regularly, describing the site, sending photographs (expensiveâ€”requires traveling to Taho to use Imperial-salvage cameras, developing costs more than she can really afford), explaining the urgency.

The responses are sympathetic but noncommittal. Everyone agrees the site sounds important. No one can promise Ministry resources for excavation in ambiguous territory. The jurisdictional questions need resolution before official action is possible. Maybe if Khesh-Dalan formally petitioned for Republican incorporation? But that requires population thresholds the village doesn't meet and political capital Akama revolutionaries don't have.

## The Time Pressure

Each winter that passes makes the situation more precarious. The village survives through collective determination and minimal resources. Food shortages are managed through strict rationing. Infrastructure degrades graduallyâ€”irrigation systems need maintenance, tools wear out, the Antediluvian utilities show increasing unreliability (some rooms that maintained temperature now fluctuate, some water outlets have stopped flowing).

Dranakh's riders are committed but realistic. If this winter is worse than last, if rationing means genuine hunger rather than discomfort, some will leave. Not abandoning revolutionary principles but recognizing that implementation failed. They'll seek incorporation into Ganati veterans kilits, settle into collective farms, abandon Akama culture to survive. The original villagers might scatter to relatives in other settlements, accept that the experiment was noble but unworkable.

Sekh's most recent letters to Ministry contacts have increased urgency: "This project has maybe two winters left before it collapses. If you can't commit Ministry resources, connect me with private scholars who have funding for legitimate excavation. These people deserve better than being cheated by merchants or watching their revolutionary commitment starve them."

The responses acknowledge urgency but reiterate constraints. Ministry resources are committed elsewhere. Private scholars with appropriate funding are rare and cautious about jurisdictionally ambiguous sites. The best suggestion: document everything thoroughly, maintain pressure on Ministry officials, and hope something breaks before the village does.

## The Archaeological Richness

What makes the situation additionally frustrating is that Khesh-Dalan represents potentially significant find. Sekh's surveys suggest the complex served research or educational purposes rather than residential or industrial functions. The artifact types cluster around information processing, display systems, and what might be experimental apparatus. If properly excavated and analyzed, the site could contribute to understanding pre-Interdict civilization in ways few other locations offer.

One structure contains what appears to be intact laboratoryâ€”work surfaces, storage systems, devices mounted in configurations suggesting coordinated function. Another holds what might be library or archive, though the information storage methods are unreadable and the access systems are dead. A third has large open space with mounting points suggesting equipment that was removed deliberately rather than salvaged randomly.

The devices Sekh has catalogued include:

* Communication interfaces (probably) showing residual power  
* Measurement instruments of unknown type  
* Display systems that project patterns when activated  
* Control mechanisms for systems that no longer exist  
* Sample containers holding materials that might be valuable if properly analyzed  
* Personal equipment whose function is unclear but construction quality is extraordinary

Proper excavation would require months of work by qualified teams, detailed documentation, controlled removal of artifacts, systematic analysis of spatial relationships. It would require funding that Khesh-Dalan doesn't have, expertise they can't access, and jurisdictional clarity that doesn't exist.

Meanwhile, every season that passes means more artifacts degrading from exposure, more context being lost as villagers repurpose spaces for practical needs, more potential knowledge disappearing because no one with proper training is documenting what remains.

## The Ethical Dilemma

Sekh faces competing obligations:

**To the village**: Help them survive economically, which means monetizing artifacts quickly even if that means suboptimal documentation and lost knowledge.

**To scholarship**: Preserve the site properly, which means waiting for proper resources and procedures even if that means the village collapses.

**To her own people**: Prioritize Serrulata interests in Ghemar-yan rather than spending time and resources helping Akama projects that most pastoralists oppose.

**To revolutionary principles**: Support fellow veterans implementing revolutionary ethics regardless of practical difficulties or political consequences.

She's chosen to prioritize the last, which creates tension with the first three. Her visits to Khesh-Dalan take time away from Ghemar-yan responsibilities. Her advocacy for Ministry support gets filtered through pastoralist networks that oppose Akama incorporation. Her correspondence efforts cost money she doesn't really have for a project that might fail regardless of her efforts.

But Khol's memory demands this effort. And Dranakh is genuinely trying to implement what his father believedâ€”that Akama culture and revolutionary ethics can coexist if people commit to the difficulty. And if this project fails because no one with connections helped them navigate Republican bureaucracy, then revolutionary principles are just rhetoric that abandons people when implementation gets hard.

## Current Status

The village persists. Morning Assemblies continue. Food is stretched. Archaeological surveys accumulate documentation no one currently values. Sekh writes letters that receive sympathetic but unhelpful responses. Winter approaches with its test of whether collective determination survives genuine hardship.

Dranakh leads with awareness that his father's idealism created impossible situations. He knows the economics don't work, knows the archaeology represents potential salvation, knows they need outside help that isn't coming fast enough. But he also knows that abandoning the project means admitting that revolutionary Akama can't implement revolutionary principles, which means every traditional khan was rightâ€”might makes right, weakness deserves suffering, helping others is theft of necessary hardship.

So the village continues its experiment in living together despite history, implementing equality despite instincts, surviving collectively when individually would be easier. They wait for Sekh's next letter, hope her Ministry contacts finally commit resources, prepare for another winter that might be the one that breaks them.

The Antediluvian ruins around them hum with mysterious utilities, glow with ambient lighting, hold artifacts whose value depends on finding buyers who won't exploit the sellers. The complex that enabled the village to survive tribute extraction now needs to fund revolutionary transformation, if anyone can figure out how to make that happen before time runs out.
