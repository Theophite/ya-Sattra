## Core Principle: Not Capitalism

None of these systems resemble capitalism as historically practiced. All feature:
- Hereditary position over market competition
- Rent extraction over production
- Religious or kinship structures constraining economic behavior

The surface forms of capitalism appear everywhere—currency, prices, wages, trade—but the underlying logic differs fundamentally.

## The Obol Standard

The Imperial obol serves as the universal unit of account. Regional currencies exist—the Ganati dinar, various Thousand Kingdoms scrip—but all convert to obol-equivalents for trade and comparison. Many, the banking organism, maintains exchange rates through mechanisms no one fully understands but everyone accepts.

**Currency divisions:**
- Beer costs 1/16 obol
- Basic meal: 1/8 to 1/4 obol
- Workers think in obols
- Merchants calculate in hundreds
- Aureate families measure wealth in millions

**Exchange rates:**
- 1 Ganati dinar = 10 obols

## The Kess Benchmark: Defining Subsistence

Subsistence economics are calibrated against Kess, a courier working the Rotten Quarter of ya-Sattra. Her income and expenses establish the minimum required for survival in the Imperial capital.

**Courier income:** 80-120 obols monthly (routine deliveries 2 obols; difficult routes 4-5 obols)

**Minimum expenses:**
- Bunkhouse: 20-22 obols/month (5 obols/week)
- Food: 45-60 obols/month (1.5-2 obols/day)

**The subsistence threshold:** 90-110 obols monthly maintains a bunk and adequate food. Below 60 obols means sleeping rough, irregular meals, owning nothing. This is destitution, not subsistence.

**Parish capitation:** 40 obols annually for legal Penitent practice—roughly half a month's income at subsistence level.

## The Three-Tier Imperial Market

### Tier One: Conventional Guild Production

Most everyday goods—clothing, food, copper vessels, furniture, beer—come from guild workshops operating under ancient charters. These are not archaeotech products; they're ordinary handcraft produced by workers following traditional methods.

Guild pricing reflects monopoly markup rather than patent rents. A guild-made shirt costs 3-4 obols and lasts years. The price includes materials, labor, and the premium extracted by guild restriction of competition.

### Tier Two: Ya-Don Production

Complex technology—lectors, lighting systems, electronics, medical equipment, weapons—comes only from the Furnaces of ya-Don. These goods require patent authentication.

A household lector costs 400-600 obols—a year's savings for a skilled artisan, impossible for a dock worker without inheritance. Such items become family heirlooms.

The distinction matters: patent extraction occurs in specific transactions (buying a lector, receiving certified medical treatment) rather than being built into bread prices.

### Tier Three: Unlicensed and Import Goods

Below guild production exists a market for cheaper alternatives. Unlicensed workshops produce lower-quality goods at lower prices—a shirt for 1-1.5 obols that wears out in months and marks the wearer as poor.

Ganati imports compete with better quality. Maguey-silk fabric from Republican workshops costs 2 obols per length. The quality approaches guild goods at prices the working poor can afford.

## What These Systems Share

**No creative destruction.** Existing arrangements persist indefinitely. Guilds have held charters for millennia. Patents self-enforce through technology. No entrepreneur can disrupt through innovation.

**Status determined by birth.** Caste, house, family, kilit membership matter more than individual achievement. You can rise within your category, but categories are fixed.

**Rent extraction dominant.** Aureate authentication, guild monopolies, warband tribute, house distribution—production happens, but production isn't where power lies.

**Religion pervades economics.** The Penitent Church legitimizes ya-Don's unions and Ganat's wealth-through-service. Oracle theology structures ya-Tsatsa's opacity. Akama fortune theology makes gambling sacred.

## Common Pitfalls

**Treating all economies as variations of capitalism.** The surface forms appear everywhere, but underlying logics differ fundamentally. A ya-Don worker's "wage" is closer to monastic allocation than labor market pricing.

**Ignoring regional variation in prices.** Food costs more in Taho (altitude imports) than ya-Sattra. Housing costs more in the Inner City than the Middens.

**Conflating patent rents with production.** Aureate families extract rents from authentication; they don't produce goods.

**Applying capitalist assumptions to non-capitalist systems.** Asovoë elders don't "invest"—they allocate across centuries. Akama warbands don't "pay" warriors—they share what fortune provides.

**Forgetting the religious dimension.** Economic behavior is shaped by theological frameworks in every region.

**Underestimating Aureate wealth concentration.** Families processing millions daily exist alongside workers earning hundreds monthly.
