# High Junction: Garrison City of the Eastern Range

## Executive Summary

High Junction sits at 6,400 feet elevation where the Azhmar River (formerly the Arkansas) carves through the southern Colorado highlands, housing 80,000 inhabitants and serving as the Republic's fourth-largest city. The settlement has transformed dramatically since grid power from Ganat proper arrived six years ago—from a frontier garrison of 30,000 to a manufacturing center drawing migrants from across Republican territory. It functions as the eastern anchor of Republican authority: the furthest reach of the power grid, the primary garrison for operations against the Thousand Kingdoms, and the administrative hub for the vast eastern rangelands where pastoralist kilits, veteran cooperatives, and Dawn agricultural settlements compete for influence.

The site was already a garrison before the Revolution, guarding the northern border against Akama incursions from the Thousand Kingdoms. The Revolution increased its strategic importance, and the recent arrival of electrical transmission has transformed its economic role entirely.

Every Senator representing High Junction is nominally Dawn Party, though the city's politics are considerably more complex. Dusk-affiliated manufacturing kilits and pastoralist bands strategically delegate their votes to moderate Dawn factions, creating representation that votes radical on military expansion and veteran benefits while maintaining pragmatism on commercial regulation. The city's explosive growth has produced a chaotic periphery of hastily constructed worker housing around relocated manufacturing facilities, a thriving vice economy serving garrison soldiers, and famously fractious kilit politics—hence the local joke: "High Junction has 80,000 people and 80,000 newspapers."

Dawn Consul Rasma Dhal-Setil technically represents no geographic constituency, but virtually all kilits forming her coalition base their operations in High Junction. Her family's mercantile enterprise—which made its fortune under the Sultanate before lesser scions financed the early Revolution—pioneered catalog delivery to range general stores, making Dhal-Setil Mercantile one of the Republic's most trusted commercial names.

## Geography and the Bridge Problem

The Azhmar River runs south-to-north through the town, carrying snowmelt from the Sangre de Cristo Mountains toward its eventual junction with what used to be called the South Platte. At High Junction, the river spans perhaps 200 feet—shallow in late summer but fast and deep during spring melt. The banks rise 15-20 feet above normal water level, creating natural terraces that protect Old Town from flooding.

The defining geographic feature is the bridge: a pre-Interdict structure spanning the river in a single impossible arch. The bridge is perhaps 250 feet long and 40 feet wide, constructed from a metalloceramic material no Republican engineer fully understands. Its surface is perfectly smooth, almost frictionless when wet, colored pale grey and showing no weathering despite unknown age. Locals joke it proves even the Antediluvians were capable of terrible construction, though the joke misses the point—the bridge itself is essentially indestructible. The problem is that it connects to nothing stable.

Whatever anchoring system the original builders used has long since been undermined by the river's gradual downcutting and lateral migration. The eastern abutment sits on eroding sandstone that loses material every spring flood. The western abutment rests on bedrock fractured by freeze-thaw cycles. The bridge doesn't collapse because the arch efficiently distributes its own weight, but neither end is truly stable. Engineers shore up approaches with timber pilings, stone gabions, and increasingly desperate earthworks, but the river works around their interventions over time.

The surface problem is equally intractable. The metalloceramic material cannot be roughened by anything the Republic can produce—it remains glass-slick when wet. The solution is crude but functional: workers spread sand constantly during wet weather, creating enough traction for careful crossing. Wagons cross slowly, drivers alert for any slip that might send cargo into the river. Accidents happen several times annually.

## Historical Development

The site's strategic value predates the Republic by uncounted generations. The crossing existed before the Sultanate, its origins forgotten even then. The bridge already stood when humans returned to this region after whatever catastrophe depopulated it. Under the Sultanate, High Junction served as a garrison guarding the northern frontier against the Thousand Kingdoms—a role that increased dramatically in importance during the Revolution, when control of the eastern crossing became essential for Republican forces operating against Sultanate cavalry and their Akama auxiliaries.

Post-revolutionary growth occurred in three waves:

**First wave (965-970):** Veterans accepting land grants in eastern territories needed a commercial center for purchasing supplies, selling products, and accessing Republican institutions. The town grew to perhaps 10,000, mostly Serrulata veterans establishing homesteads within 50 miles of the garrison.

**Second wave (970-975):** Hereditary Debtors arrived to staff the expanding bureaucratic apparatus. As eastern territories formalized administrative structures—land registration, tax collection, legal adjudication, census operations—the Republic needed competent administrators. The Debtors, working off multi-generational debts through public service, provided that expertise. Their large bureaucratic complex arose during this period, housing perhaps 3,000 Debtor families.

**Third wave (976-present):** This has been most dramatic. When grid power from Ganat proper arrived six years ago, it enabled manufacturing operations that could never have sustained themselves on wind power alone. Dusk-affiliated agricultural manufacturing kilits, recognizing their primary markets lay in the eastern range rather than Ganat or Taho, began relocating operations. The logic was straightforward: why transport finished goods east from Ganat, or raw materials west from the range, when you could manufacture near your customers? The move also provided distance from Dawn-dominated urban politics in Ganat while placing facilities in a garrison town where Dawn's military strength provided security.

This manufacturing relocation brought 50,000 new arrivals in six years. Workers from the kilits, their families, associated service providers, opportunistic entrepreneurs, and a constant stream of migrants seeking employment. Old Town, designed for 30,000, suddenly hosts 80,000. The result has been controlled chaos.

## Urban Layout and Architecture

### Old Town

Old Town occupies the western bank terrace and portions of the eastern approach. The architecture reflects Ganati building traditions adapted to frontier conditions: adobe domes rising above underground chambers, their smooth ochre-cream exteriors broken by organic doorways with copper cladding worked in flowing patterns. Some structures show carved decoration—geometric curves incised into plaster, bands of rust-orange and cream marking structural divisions—though nothing approaching the monumental carved facades of Ganat proper. Every surface that isn't regularly cleaned accretes layers of wheatpaste posters, creating a palimpsest of political advocacy and commercial announcement that gives even the oldest buildings a ragged, contested appearance. This is frontier work: functional first, but still recognizably Ganati in its flowing lines and organic forms.

The larger domes—housing the garrison headquarters, the Hereditary Debtor bureaucratic complex, and public buildings—use timber framing inherited from an era when wood was more plentiful. These structures extend underground, their cool chambers providing relief from summer heat and protection from winter wind. Revolutionary banners hang from doorways and across corridors: maguey-silk dyed rust-orange or teal, bearing cream-colored text in flowing Ganati script.

Old Town houses: the garrison headquarters and barracks, a converted Sultanate administrative complex capable of withstanding assault; the Hereditary Debtor bureaucratic complex, a sprawling structure of connected buildings housing administrative offices, archives, and Debtor family housing; the parish of Saint Arsakh, the primary Penitent church occupying a converted Sultanate administrative building; commercial establishments serving the established population; and the residential core where pre-revolution families and early revolutionary settlers live.

### The Manufacturing District

South of Old Town, beyond the original settlement perimeter, manufacturing facilities have risen over the past six years. These structures make no pretense to architectural refinement: timber frames on stone foundations, sheet-metal roofing, large doors for wagon access, chimneys venting forge smoke. The buildings are functional rather than aesthetic—walls that keep weather out, roofs that don't leak, floors that support heavy machinery.

The contrast between exterior and interior is stark. These relatively shabby buildings house some of the most advanced technology Ganat can build: precision lathes, textile machinery incorporating salvaged Imperial components, metalworking equipment capable of tolerances that would have been impossible a generation ago. The Republic's ideology of understanding and replicating rather than merely scavenging means these facilities represent genuine industrial capacity—crude by pre-Interdict standards but entirely comprehensible to the engineers who operate them.

The district includes forges producing metal goods, textile operations processing maguey fiber and range wool, food processing facilities, and workshops producing manufactured items for range distribution. Each facility belongs to a specific kilit, usually one that relocated from Ganat, staffed by kilit members who came with the relocation or were recruited afterward.

### Worker Housing

Surrounding the manufacturing district, spreading unevenly into formerly empty land, worker housing has appeared through ad hoc construction. These buildings represent urgent need met with available resources: undersized timber frames, inconsistent adobe fill, roofing of whatever materials could be obtained. Many structures began as temporary shelters and solidified over time through continuous repair and reinforcement rather than any deliberate plan. Increasingly common are Dhal-Setil Mercantile prefab cabins—standardized timber-frame structures shipped in sections and assembled on site, offering better construction quality than improvised housing at prices workers can afford through installment payments to their kilits.

The housing is dense—families of four or five in single rooms, sometimes sharing buildings with multiple other families. Sanitation is inadequate, with communal latrines and some dwellings lacking facilities entirely. Water access is inconsistent through wells, water carriers, or river collection. Fire risk is extreme. Disease spreads easily.

## Political Structure

The city generates seven Senate seats through kilits based in or near High Junction: two veterans' cooperative kilits that vote pure Dawn for maximum military spending and aggressive eastern expansion; one agricultural manufacturing coalition representing moderate Dawn positions that balance revolutionary ideology with commercial pragmatism; one Hereditary Debtors seat that functions as a swing vote supporting whoever offers better debt reduction terms; two composite seats assembled from various small kilits representing moderate Dawn coalitions; and one pastoralist confederation seat that is technically Dusk but delegates to moderate Dawn to prevent more radical alternatives.

The key dynamic is Dusk kilits—manufacturing operations, merchant houses, conservative pastoralist bands—strategically delegating votes to moderate Dawn candidates rather than maintaining independent Dusk representation. Dawn will control High Junction regardless of Dusk opposition, so pragmatic Dusk kilits delegate to the least radical Dawn factions, gaining some influence over policy rather than remaining in permanent opposition.

This produces a Dawn majority that's radical on military issues, where all Dawn factions agree, but moderate on everything else, where Dusk delegations constrain Dawn positions.

### The Veterans' Kilits

Young men and women nearing discharge from the Republican Guard face aggressive recruitment from multiple veterans' organizations, each offering different ideological orientation and material benefits. The competition has become almost ritualized: every veterans' hall and bar associated with a particular kilit provides pulque and hashish virtually free to prospective members, creating a scene where soldiers spend their final weeks of service moving between establishments, sampling different kilits' offerings while being pitched on political platforms and mutual aid benefits.

The three major veterans' kilits in High Junction represent distinct ideological tendencies. The **Frontier Veterans' Collective** occupies a converted warehouse in the manufacturing district—meetings are serious, ideological, structured around formal agendas with debates and votes. They advocate aggressive Dawn positions: maximum military spending, accelerated eastern expansion, land grants prioritizing collective farms over individual homesteads. Their hall features revolutionary banners, portraits of revolutionary martyrs, and rhetoric treating military service as ongoing revolutionary commitment.

The **Eastern Range Cooperative** maintains more modest facilities in Old Town—their hall is functional, their meetings focused on practical mutual aid rather than ideology. They represent moderate Dawn positions, advocating for veteran benefits without aggressive expansion that might destabilize existing settlements. Members tend to be older veterans who've already established homesteads, concerned with consolidation rather than constant growth.

The **Homesteaders' Brotherhood** operates from what's essentially a social club near the garrison—their hall is casual, meetings are excuses for socializing, political discussions minimal. They represent the least ideological veterans, focused on land grants and equipment loans without particular commitment to Dawn platforms beyond what benefits members directly.

Once a soldier chooses and formally joins, they're integrated into the kilit's social and economic networks. They receive initial land grant assistance through help navigating bureaucratic processes with the Hereditary Debtors, startup equipment loaned at favorable terms or provided as mutual aid, connections to other kilit members already homesteading, and ongoing political representation. In exchange, they provide kilit dues of typically 5-10% of annual income, militia service, and political support.

### Political Culture

Most of the 50,000 newcomers who arrived over the past six years belong to kilits whose geographic base lies elsewhere. Some retain membership in organizations headquartered in Ganat proper or Taho, paying dues to treasuries they'll never see and receiving political representation through Senators who have never visited the eastern range. Others come from pastoral bands—people who decided they wanted steady work, electric heat, and lighting rather than another winter on the range. Still others are veterans who accepted land grants, tried collective farming, and discovered it was neither as romantic nor as lucrative as revolutionary rhetoric had suggested.

This diversity creates aggressive competition for new membership among local kilits—the veterans' organizations, the Hereditary Debtors, the nascent manufacturing worker associations—all seeking to recruit arrivals away from their original affiliations or incorporate those who arrived without strong kilit ties. The recruitment happens in taverns, at factory gates, through mutual aid offered during crises. A worker who arrived as a member of a Ganati textile kilit might find that the local veterans' cooperative offers better land grant assistance, or that the Hereditary Debtors provide more useful connections for navigating the bureaucracy. A former pastoralist might discover that manufacturing worker associations offer solidarity unavailable in the individualistic range economy.

High Junction's "80,000 newspapers" joke references the proliferation of small publications serving different factional audiences. Perhaps twelve newspapers actually publish regularly, ranging from single-sheet broadsheets to multi-page periodicals. The publications include: the **Republican Guard Weekly**, which is military-sponsored and reports garrison news while promoting recruitment; the **Eastern Pioneer**, a Dawn party organ advocating settlement expansion and ideological purity; the **Range Commerce Journal**, Dusk-aligned and covering trade, manufacturing, and commercial opportunities; the **Pastoral Review**, serving pastoralist kilits and emphasizing rangeland conservation; the **Workers' Voice**, a radical Dawn publication by manufacturing kilit laborers criticizing employer practices; and various smaller sheets publishing irregularly.

The publications range from professional operations employing paid journalists to amateur productions by single individuals with printing access. Most appear weekly or biweekly. Literacy rates of perhaps 80% overall—higher in Old Town, lower in worker housing—ensure broad circulation, supplemented by newspapers read aloud in taverns and meeting halls.

Any significant event produces competing interpretations across different papers. A workers' strike might be heroic resistance in the Workers' Voice, dangerous radicalism in the Range Commerce Journal, justified response to exploitation in the Eastern Pioneer, and inevitable consequence of rapid industrial growth in the Republican Guard Weekly. Readers consume multiple sources, evaluating claims, forming opinions informed by diverse partisan perspectives.

The same fractious energy covers the city's walls. Wheatpaste posters appear overnight on any surface not actively maintained—building faces, fence posts, the timber pilings shoring up the bridge approaches, the poles carrying electrical transmission. Most advocate for kilit membership, promising mutual aid, land grant assistance, or political representation. Others promote kilit coalitions ahead of Senate votes, or individual Senators seeking reelection, or commercial ventures hoping to catch workers' attention. The posters layer over each other as weeks pass, newer advocacy covering older, creating visible stratigraphy of the city's political arguments. Removing them is pointless; fresh posters appear within days. The visual clutter has become part of High Junction's character, announcing to any visitor that this is a place where political competition happens continuously and nobody controls the conversation.

## Economic Life

High Junction's economy rests on three foundations: military payroll, manufacturing production, and range commerce.

### The Garrison Economy

The Republican Guard maintains approximately 3,000 active personnel at any given time, rotating between garrison duty, range patrol, and training operations. These soldiers receive monthly pay sufficient for basic needs plus discretionary spending, generating perhaps 150,000 dinars annually circulating through the local economy. Direct military procurement—food, fodder, equipment, construction materials—adds substantially more.

Garrison soldiers are young, typically 18-26, mostly male with perhaps 30% female. They're ethnically diverse—Serrulata predominate but substantial numbers of Ranga, near-baseline humans, and some Akama serve as well. They're away from family oversight and paid regularly. They support taverns, gambling establishments, brothels, food vendors, and entertainment. Their presence shapes the city's character—economically significant, socially disruptive.

### Manufacturing

The manufacturing kilits employ perhaps 15,000 workers directly, producing goods primarily for range markets. The arrival of grid power from Ganat six years ago made this concentration possible—before transmission, the windmills and capacitor banks could never have supported industrial operations at this scale.

The work is physically demanding. Most manufacturing requires standing for full shifts, repetitive motions, exposure to forge heat or chemical fumes or textile dust, constant noise. The workday is eight hours in theory, but overtime is frequent and often mandatory during high-production periods. Injuries are common—burns from forges, crushed fingers from machinery, respiratory problems from long-term dust exposure. Safety standards are poor by western Republican measures.

But the pay is good and getting better. Wages run lower than in Ganat or Taho, but vastly exceed what the range economy offers, and the cost of living undercuts both. Unskilled laborers earn 15-20 dinars weekly—enough for basic food, housing, and modest discretionary spending that would be impossible for a pastoral family or struggling homesteader. Skilled craftspeople earn 40-60 dinars weekly, allowing genuine savings. Foremen and supervisors earn 80-100 dinars weekly. For someone who spent years watching sheep or failing at collective farming, factory work represents material improvement despite its hardships.

### Range Commerce

Dhal-Setil Mercantile dominates trade through their pioneering catalog delivery system. Pastoral families or homesteading veterans can order anything from an electric loom to sheep-dip to complete prefabricated cabins through illustrated catalogs. They place orders at general stores across the range, nearly all supplied by Dhal-Setil caravans, and orders consolidate at High Junction before regular caravan runs deliver purchases within eight weeks at fair prices.

## Infrastructure

### Power and Electricity

The city receives power from two sources: degrading local generation and transmission from Ganat proper. The tension between these systems reflects broader Republican development patterns—local resilience versus centralized efficiency, proven but deteriorating technology versus new but dependent systems.

The old windmills and capacitor banks ring the town's southern perimeter—perhaps twenty windmills, each 40 feet tall, feeding banks of salvaged capacitors. The system was installed during early post-revolutionary expansion, providing power when High Junction was a garrison village of 10,000. It barely suffices as supplementary generation now, and complaints persist that the manufacturers have first claim on windmill power when the transmission line falters, leaving Old Town residents in the dark.

The windmills are degrading. Mechanisms are simple but components wear. Bandits strip copper wire from distribution lines, selling it to scrap merchants or smuggling it to the Thousand Kingdoms. When it's stripped, no one replaces it.

A transmission line from Ganat arrived six years ago—copper wire on timber poles, strung 120 miles from the nearest substation. This provides power for the manufacturing district and supplements Old Town's failing local generation. The transmission is more reliable but creates dependency: storms damage lines frequently at this altitude, and power fails until repair crews arrive from Ganat. During outages, the manufacturing district goes dark, production stops, workers idle, and the city remembers how dependent it's become on infrastructure it doesn't control.

### Telegraph

High Junction hosts a telegraph office in the bureaucratic complex, connecting the city to Ganat and points west. The system uses salvaged Imperial equipment that Republican engineers have learned to maintain. Telegraph service is expensive—2 dinars per 20 words to Ganat—restricting it to commercial and official use. The Hereditary Debtors staff the office 24 hours daily.

Most residents never use the telegraph. They communicate by letter, by courier, or not at all.

### The Railroad Debate

Regular proposals emerge to extend rail service from Ganat to High Junction. They founder on geography and politics. Rail would need to run approximately 180 miles across increasingly marginal terrain, requiring substantial grading, bridge construction, and ongoing maintenance. Cost estimates range from 2-4 million dinars.

The political coalition blocking the railroad is counterintuitive. Urban Dawn Senators in Ganat proper have little interest in funding eastward expansion that doesn't benefit their constituencies. Pastoralist Dusk Senators are actively hostile to frontier settlement that threatens their grazing territories. Together, these unlikely allies prevent the 60% Senate majority required for major infrastructure investment. The manufacturing kilits in High Junction would benefit enormously, but they lack sufficient Senate representation to overcome the coalition.

The debate has continued for years without resolution. Meanwhile, commerce moves by wagon—four to five days each direction.

### Water and Sanitation

Old Town has functional water infrastructure inherited from the Sultanate era. Worker housing mostly lacks formal infrastructure. Residents use communal wells, purchase water from carriers, or carry their own. Sanitation is worse—communal latrines fill faster than they're cleaned, creating regular disease outbreaks.

The manufacturing district generates industrial waste dumped into drainage channels flowing to the river, contaminating water downstream.

## Crime and Vice

High Junction has earned its reputation for lawlessness—not complete chaos, but steady background disorder that authorities manage rather than eliminate.

### Smuggling

The primary criminal enterprise is smuggling to and from the Thousand Kingdoms. Akama territories 80 miles north operate under different legal and economic systems, creating arbitrage opportunities. Northbound smuggling moves weapons, tools, luxury goods, and medical supplies. Southbound smuggling brings stolen archaeotech, drugs, human trafficking, and stolen livestock.

The Republic's response is pragmatic. Colonel Ghar understands he can't eliminate smuggling—economic incentives are too strong, the border too long, forces insufficient. He focuses on preventing violence and maintaining reasonable order rather than achieving prohibition.

### Vice Economy

Garrison soldiers want entertainment. The city provides: gambling establishments ranging from respectable to extremely crooked, brothels operating in legal gray areas, taverns, hashish vendors, and various other services. The vice operations concentrate in specific districts, informally tolerated so long as they don't generate excessive disorder.

## Religious Life

The parish of Saint Arsakh occupies a converted Sultanate administrative building on the Old Town terrace—a two-story adobe structure with a covered courtyard, thick walls, narrow windows, timber-framed roof. The architecture is functional rather than grand, seating perhaps 400\.

Curate Demal Totha operates with substantial autonomy. He's 38 years old, Serrulata, Dawn Party, revolutionary veteran, from an agricultural family in the Virgin River valley. His theology is practical Orthodox Penitentism without the radical elements characterizing some Ganati clergy. He holds regular Common Confessions attended by perhaps 200 faithful, along with special observances and life ceremonies.

### Competitive Philanthropy

High Junction's elite have imported a practice from the western Republic: competitive philanthropy—public demonstrations of ostentatious generosity. The competition plays out primarily through Particular Confessions, where wealthy individuals justify their commercial success in terms of their charitable activities, elaborating at length on how their prosperity serves collective capacity rather than personal enrichment.

Rasma Dhal-Setil exemplifies this practice. She lives in Ganat and always has, but when she visits High Junction every few months she delivers Particular Confessions described as "real stemwinders"—hour-long orations in which she accounts for Dhal-Setil Mercantile's quarterly earnings, explains why profits were reinvested rather than distributed, catalogs the catalog innovations expanding access to manufactured goods across the range, details employment practices benefiting workers through profit-sharing and education funding, and enumerates infrastructure investments and ongoing commitments to further works. The theological requirement to confess one's works creates occasion for comprehensive review of one's commercial achievements.

The Particular Confessions serve multiple functions simultaneously. Religiously, they satisfy Penitent requirements for public confession of works-based atonement. Commercially, they're advertisements demonstrating reliability and virtue of Dhal-Setil enterprises. Politically, they demonstrate Dawn credentials while maintaining commercial moderation. Socially, they're performances establishing status within High Junction's elite.

This represents a more conservative style of Penitent worship than what prevails in Ganat proper—less emphasis on revolutionary theology, more on demonstrated virtue through commercial success and charitable contribution. The approach has spread eastward with the manufacturing kilits, creating tension with the garrison's more ideological Dawn veterans who view bourgeois philanthropy as inadequate substitute for revolutionary commitment.

## Daily Life and Culture

High Junction operates at two speeds simultaneously. Old Town maintains rhythms of established community—predictable work schedules, familiar social patterns, stable relationships. The manufacturing district and worker housing experience constant churn—new arrivals seeking employment, discharged soldiers deciding whether to stay or homestead, failed businesses closing while new ventures open.

### Economic Stratification

Wealth disparities are visible everywhere. Old Town residents in adobe domes with underground chambers and electric lighting live differently than manufacturing workers in shared timber-frame units with oil lamps. The garrison officers eating at proper restaurants with tablecloths and multiple courses experience different High Junction than enlisted soldiers buying street food from vendors. The Dhal-Setil estate in Old Town, maintained for Rasma's occasional visits, with its servants, private courtyard, and imported furnishings exists in a different world than worker housing where eight people share 300 square feet.

But the stratification isn't rigid. Veterans successfully transitioning to homesteading improve their circumstances substantially. Skilled workers in manufacturing kilits earn enough for modest comfort. Ambitious individuals create opportunities through entrepreneurship, political involvement, or educational advancement. The frontier creates spaces for mobility that more established Republican cities lack.

### Ethnic Dynamics

Serrulata predominate numerically and dominate politically, but High Junction's diversity forces accommodation. Ranga work in manufacturing operations—their forest adaptations are useless for desert survival, so they accept industrial employment unavailable elsewhere. Near-baseline humans staff technical and administrative positions. Small Akama populations operate in gray areas between Republican citizenship and Thousand Kingdoms affiliation, serving as intermediaries for cross-border commerce.

The Republican ideological commitment to caste-blindness creates pressures toward social integration. The manufacturing kilits officially hire based on capability rather than biology, the garrison accepts any caste willing to serve, the Church preaches universal human dignity transcending engineered differences. But biological reality and cultural preferences create informal segregation—Serrulata socialize primarily with other Serrulata, Ranga form mutual aid networks separate from mainstream kilits, near-baselines cluster in administrative roles.
