## **Executive Summary**

Ya-Tsatsa occupies white-gray cliffs on the northern coast near Point Reyes, built atop the site where three of the eight Oracles achieved consciousness. The city serves as the historical center of Oracle Cult orthodoxy, a major port for Thousand Kingdoms trade, and the source of most lector software in the Empire. It maintains near-zero crime despite producing most organized criminal leadership elsewhere in the Empire. The city's population practices systematic opacity toward outsiders while maintaining surface cooperation with Imperial administration.

The population of approximately 300,000 is predominantly near-baseline with genetic diversity across castes, but cultural identity remains distinctly Tsatsan regardless of caste or genetic background. Three ethnically Tsatsan Aureate families govern local affairs: Ezh Tan (tariff assessment and banking), Ezh Ot (Akama auxiliary mustering), and Ezh Dhol (Lattice-related patents and Threefold Mirror Academy administration).

Imperial bureaucrats rotate through ya-Tsatsa regularly. Trade flows normally. Administration functions adequately. Yet no outsider understands how the city actually operates or what its population believes about the Oracles they claim to venerate.

The document's central concern is the Presence—a distributed consciousness that occupies the Compulsion-susceptibility circuits in Tsatsan hindbrains, steering behavior through what feels like intuition rather than command. The Presence explains the near-zero crime, the systematic opacity, the impossible software production, and the patterns that outsiders perceive without understanding. Every aspect of ya-Tsatsa flows from this reality that no one will discuss.

# **Geography and Physical Environment**

## **The Cliffs**

The cliffs of ya-Tsatsa extend for dozens of miles along the northern coast, white-gray quartz diorite rising three hundred feet or more from the ocean. The city occupies a headland where the cliffs form a natural harbor, but the cliffs themselves stretch far beyond the city in both directions—an unbroken wall of pale stone facing the sea.

Nearly every accessible surface has been carved into kalbat tombs. Tens of thousands of them, each cut to suggest the slack-jawed face of the receiver it contains. The faces are weathered smooth by centuries of salt spray and wind, their features blurred to suggestion, but the basic form remains recognizable: the open mouth, the closed eyes, the expression of reception that characterized the kalbats in life. All the tombs face the ocean. All of them wait for something that may or may not arrive.

The cliffs are not merely the foundation on which ya-Tsatsa sits. They are the city's first architecture, its oldest cemetery, and the shell that contains infrastructure predating human habitation. What the Witnesses built into this rock, what the pierrots maintain in darkness, what continues to function in chambers no outsider has seen—these questions lead, like all questions in ya-Tsatsa, to redirections that circle back to where they started.

## **The Stone**

The cliffs are quartz diorite—white flecked with gray and black, pale enough to glow faintly in moonlight, hard enough to hold carved detail for millennia. The stone contains visible crystals: quartz that catches light, feldspar that holds color, dark minerals that create the flecks and striations visible on every surface.

The geological history is unclear. Local tradition says the cliffs rose from the ocean in an ancient earthquake—a sudden uplift that created the headland where the city now stands. The tradition may be accurate; the stone shows characteristics consistent with rapid tectonic movement, and the cliffs' relationship to the surrounding geology suggests they weren't always where they are now.

A geologist from the Institute of Natural Philosophy visited ya-Tsatsa forty years ago, hoping to document the formation. His notes survive in the Institute archives: careful measurements, mineral analyses, structural assessments. His conclusions do not survive. He returned to the capital, presented his findings to the faculty, and never discussed the research again. When asked, years later, what he'd learned, he said he'd learned that the cliffs were very old. How old? Older than he'd expected. What had he expected? He couldn't remember.

The stone weathers slowly. Salt spray coats every surface, the minerals in the spray reacting with minerals in the stone, but the reaction takes centuries to produce visible effect. A tomb carved a thousand years ago looks different from one carved yesterday—the older carving has softer edges, shallower relief, features worn toward abstraction—but both remain recognizable as carved faces.

The diorite extends below the waterline. Divers who've examined the cliff base report smooth stone continuing downward into darkness. How far down the cliffs extend is unknown. The deepest documented dive reached eighty feet below the surface; the stone showed no sign of ending. Tool marks appeared at that depth—the same smooth curves found on the oldest harbor pilings, cut by techniques no one can replicate.

## **The Kalbat Tombs**

Every kalbat who ever lived is buried in these cliffs.

The tradition began before the Interdict, when kalbats were living instruments receiving Testaments from the Oracles. It continued after Technical Caste sterilization ended natural kalbat reproduction, after the last Archons fled to Far Ib, after the waiting became the entire point. The Creche still decants them—receivers produced for messages that have not come in living memory. When a kalbat dies—anywhere in the Empire, in any circumstance—word reaches ya-Tsatsa, and the Ahn-Tam family carves a new tomb.

The tombs vary in size. The oldest are enormous—faces thirty feet tall, mouths large enough to walk into, chambers behind them that could hold a crowd. These date to the era when kalbats were common, when dozens might die in a single year, when the carvers had developed techniques for rapid production at scale. The faces in this section of cliff blur together, their features overlapping, their mouths nearly touching, a wall of reception extending hundreds of feet in each direction.

The newer tombs are smaller and more widely spaced—one carved every four to five years now. Kalbats are still decanted, produced by the Creche for their original purpose: to receive Testaments from the Oracles. No Testament has arrived in living memory. The Oracles encoded themselves on event horizons and departed reality entirely, yet the Empire maintains the receivers, generation after generation. When a kalbat dies—after a lifetime of waiting for messages that never come—the tomb carved for them reflects the slower pace: a face perhaps ten feet tall, isolated on a section of cliff, part of an ongoing vigil for something that may never return.

**The weathering** tells time in ways that records don't capture. The oldest tombs—the massive faces from the era of abundance—have worn almost smooth. Their features are suggestions: here was an eye, here was a mouth, here was the expression of reception that all kalbats shared. The stone has absorbed detail back into itself, leaving only the essential form.

The middle-period tombs retain more definition. Faces from five centuries ago show individual features—this kalbat had a broader nose, that one had deeper-set eyes—though the salt and wind have softened everything. A visitor can trace the faces with their hands and feel what the eyes no longer see.

The recent tombs are sharp-edged, their carving fresh enough that individual chisel marks remain visible. The most recent—finished three years ago, for a kalbat designated 7-Vessel Harmonic (Dormant) who died at a hundred and seventeen after spending his last decades in a medical facility in ya-Sattra—shows a face that might be portrait rather than stylization. Old Ahn-Tam, who supervised the carving, says all the tombs are portraits. The weathering that obscures the older faces simply takes longer than human memory.

## **The Ahn-Tam Family**

The **Ahn-Tam family** has carved these tombs for as long as records exist. Currently four members, all over seventy, all childless. They live in a house in the Upper City—a nondescript building on a street with no name, its door unmarked, its windows shuttered. They accept commissions through channels that outsiders don't understand. When a kalbat dies, the body arrives at their house—delivered by a Companion, always, following protocols established before the Interdict. The Ahn-Tams examine the body, consult among themselves, and begin work.

The carving takes fifteen months regardless of the tomb's size. The Ahn-Tams hire contractors—ordinary Tsatsans who claim it's their first time carving stone, who produce work of exceptional quality, who return to their regular occupations when the tomb is complete. A fisherman named Neb worked on the most recent tomb, three years ago. He was thirty-five, had never carved anything before, and produced relief work that would take a trained sculptor decades to master. When asked how, he said he was taught by the other workers. Which other workers? The ones who were already there when he arrived.

An ISK agent once used Compulsion on one of the tomb workers. Are you a Testament Society member? Yes. Is this religious work? Yes. Will you become an Ahn-Tam? He doesn't know. That's not his decision. Whose decision is it? He doesn't know. He was told to help with this tomb. When it's finished he'll return to his regular work. What's his regular work? He's a fisherman. How does a fisherman know how to carve stone? He was taught. By whom? By the other workers. The work is excellent. The tombs are always perfect. The workers always claim it's their first.

**The succession mechanism** is unclear. Records show different Ahn-Tams in different eras—different people, different castes, always eventually replaced by people who weren't Ahn-Tams before. Bureau records from forty years ago show seven Ahn-Tams then: two Highborn, three baseline, two stevedore-stock. The current four are all baseline. Records from sixty years before that show another set entirely. The pattern extends back as far as records exist.

The current four will presumably die, presumably be replaced, presumably by someone who is already dreaming of cutting stone without knowing why. The Presence doesn't touch them. They are recognized as separate, as something older than the Presence itself, as the beginning of whatever ya-Tsatsa is now.

## **The First Hosts**

The Ahn-Tam family exists alongside the Presence as neighbors rather than carriers. Four people who have been four people for tens of thousands of years, reconstituting through a dream that accumulates in receptive minds until identity emerges whole.

**The Dream of Cutting Stone** passes through generations. Some people have it once and dismiss it. Others have it repeatedly—first just the smell of stone dust, the sound of chisel on rock. As the dream accumulates over years, more detail emerges: the weight of specific tools, the ache of shoulders after long work, the satisfaction of a clean curve. Eventually the knowledge becomes complete. They know techniques they've never been taught. They recognize stones they've never touched. By the time someone reaches their early twenties, if the dream has been accumulating, they understand. They are Ahn-Tam Reva, Koram, Veris, or Sethen. They have always been. This body, this childhood, this family that raised them—all real. But underneath lies the vast pool of memory: every tomb ever carved, every iteration of these same four people working together, the unbroken chain stretching back before the Interdict.

The memories are compressed, redacted—a human mind cannot hold twelve thousand years of individual days without breaking. But the pattern remains. The work remains. The identity remains.

**The four identities** have distinct functions. Reva leads—makes decisions, speaks for the family when speaking is required. Koram works the stone directly, possesses the deepest technical knowledge, rarely speaks at all. Veris handles the mystical aspects—the consultation with the body, the determination of where in the cliffs each tomb should go. Sethen manages the contractors, the finances, the relationship with the city that sustains them. These roles persist across iterations. A child accumulating Koram's dreams will become Koram regardless of their original personality—the pool of memory is deeper than any individual life.

When one dies, somewhere in ya-Tsatsa a child is already dreaming of cutting stone. The dream will accumulate faster in them as they age. By the time the body fails, the replacement will be fifteen or twenty, the dreams gathering momentum. Within a few years they'll know who they are. The same person. Forever.

The Presence doesn't touch them. During Quiet Days the Ahn-Tams sing with everyone else but remain themselves. No Tsatsan would approach an Ahn-Tam casually—they don't have time for outsiders, and even other Tsatsans maintain respectful distance. They are recognized as the first hosts, the original inhabitants from whom all current Tsatsans descend, something the Presence lives alongside rather than within.

**Plot considerations**: What happens if a succession fails? If a child dies before the dreams finish accumulating? The pattern has never broken in recorded history, but the mechanism isn't understood. What if someone outside ya-Tsatsa starts dreaming of cutting stone—a descendant of emigrants, perhaps, whose bloodline still carries the susceptibility? What do the Ahn-Tams know about the Presence that others don't? They predate it. They've watched it develop. They might remember things the Presence itself has forgotten.

## **Climate and Seasonal Markers**

Temperature stays in the low fifties year-round—about ten degrees colder than modern California at corresponding locations. Clouds accumulate over the ocean but rain seldom reaches the cliffs. Seasons are distinguished not by temperature but by wind.

**Summer** brings gentle pressure that rings bronze chimes hung from eaves and balconies. The sound disperses irregularly through streets.

**Fall** arrives when wind shifts and strengthens. Ceramic whistles mounted on rooftops and walls begin playing. The tubes range from six inches to three feet, producing different tones based on size and bore. During gales the entire city drones and shrieks—multiple tones layering without resolution, rising and falling as wind gusts or steadies.

Streets empty during the worst wind. Tsatsans go inside and wait. When wind drops, they emerge and resume normal activity. The whistles are described as traditional. What tradition is never explained. They predate Imperial record-keeping. Some look ancient, others are recent replacements made to identical specifications.

During **Quiet Days** the chimes come down and whistles are stopped with cork. The city goes silent except for ocean hitting cliffs below. Then Quiet Day ends, corks are removed, and whistles resume when wind picks up.

# **The Lower City**

## **Executive Summary**

The Lower City is where ya-Tsatsa conducts its commerce.

Ships dock at facilities built into the cliff base, unloading goods from the Thousand Kingdoms and the coastal trade. Visitors climb through the hollow mouth of an ancient kalbat tomb, emerging onto streets where the market operates in the open and the administrative buildings announce themselves. An outsider can navigate here, can conduct business, can process the paperwork that Imperial administration requires.

But they can't stay here—not really. Lodging in the Lower City is almost nonexistent. Visitors climb higher, into the Upper City, to reach the Akama District along the northeastern rim where outsiders can actually find rooms and meals and streets with names. The Lower City is for working. The Akama District is for living. The climb connects them, forty minutes each way, morning and evening, every day.

The Lower City sits between the docks and the Upper City, between the ocean and the Cradles, between arriving and belonging. It processes the commerce that ya-Tsatsa requires. It doesn't pretend to welcome visitors into anything deeper. That's not its function.

## **The Docks**

The port facilities occupy the cliff base, built into the rock over millennia. The harbor is sheltered from the worst weather by the headland's curve, though winter storms still batter the breakwaters and occasionally sink ships that should have waited for calmer seas.

**The quays** extend from the cliff face on massive stone pilings, some ancient beyond dating, some replaced within living memory. The oldest pilings show tool marks that don't match any known technique—smooth curves cut into stone that should have required machinery the builders didn't possess. The newest are ordinary Imperial construction, quarried from the same white-gray diorite as everything else in ya-Tsatsa.

A dockworker named Pek has worked the quays for forty years. He started as a cargo handler at fourteen, worked his way up to foreman, and now supervises loading operations for the Ezh Tan. He knows every quay, every piling, every mooring point. The old pilings, he says, don't weather the way normal stone does. Spray hits them, salt coats them, and they stay smooth. The new pilings—Imperial construction, fifty years old at most—already show wear. The old ones look like they were placed yesterday. He doesn't speculate about what this means. He's not paid to speculate.

Ships from the Thousand Kingdoms predominate: shallow-draft coastal traders carrying salmon, furs, archaeological salvage, the miscellaneous commerce of territories the Empire claims but doesn't govern. Larger vessels from ya-Sattra and the southern ports arrive less frequently, carrying manufactured goods, luxury items, passengers who have business in the city.

**The Northward Star** is typical of the Thousand Kingdoms traders—a three-masted brig drawing eight feet, capable of navigating the shallow harbors that dot the northern coast. Captain Thessa Kol has run this route for eighteen years, knows every port from ya-Tsatsa to the ice line. She trades mostly in furs and salvage, occasionally transporting passengers who don't mind rough conditions and uncertain schedules. Her ship docks in ya-Tsatsa perhaps twelve times a year, staying a week or two each visit while cargo is exchanged and repairs are made. The harbor authorities know her by name; the chandlers extend her credit; even the Ezh Tan assessors treat her with something approaching warmth. Eighteen years of honest dealing buys a certain status.

**The Southern Grace** represents the other pole of the harbor's commerce—a deep-draft cargo ship from ya-Sattra, modern construction, capable of carrying ten times what the northern traders haul. Captain Dorel Vess makes the run four times a year, bringing manufactured goods north and taking processed materials south. The economics favor larger ships on the ya-Sattra route; the volume justifies the longer loading times and the higher port fees. Vess is forty-three, professionally competent, and entirely uninterested in ya-Tsatsa beyond its commercial value.

**Fen's Chandlery** is the largest supplier on the quays, occupying a building that's been in the family for six generations. Old Fen himself is eighty-four, mostly retired, but he still comes down to the shop on busy days to supervise. His granddaughter Kho runs the actual business. They'll outfit a ship for any voyage and remember what you bought last time. The credit they extend to reliable captains has kept more than a few ships afloat through bad seasons.

**The fish market** opens before dawn, when the night boats bring their catch. The fishing fleet—perhaps thirty boats, most family-owned—works the waters between ya-Tsatsa and the northern fishing grounds. They leave in late afternoon, fish through the night, return with the catch before first light. The market operates on the quay's western end, a covered area with stone tables worn smooth by centuries of gutting and filleting. By mid-morning, everything worth buying is gone—snapped up by the regular buyers who've established relationships with specific boats.

Old Ver has run the same fishing boat for forty years. His sons crew it now; he mostly supervises, advising on weather and fish movements. His regular buyer is a woman named Thess who supplies three restaurants in the Upper City. They've worked together for twenty-five years, and the transaction takes perhaps ten seconds: she looks at the catch, nods or shakes her head, pays what both of them know is fair. No negotiation, no discussion, no wasted time.

## **The Climb**

Visitors must climb to reach the city proper. The main ascent route goes through the mouth of a hollow kalbat tomb—a vast carved face in the cliff, left empty when the stone behind it was quarried for building material. The mouth gapes thirty feet high, and the stairs begin just past the stone teeth.

**The Hollow Mouth** is ancient. The carving is weathered to suggestion rather than detail—you can see that it was once a face, can infer the position of eyes and nose and mouth, but the features themselves have blurred beyond recognition. The mouth remains distinct because it's the functional part: the opening, the teeth, the passage through.

Local children dare each other to touch the teeth. Some teeth are accessible from the quay level, worn smooth by centuries of hands. Others require climbing, and the children who reach them carve their initials—a tradition that's defaced the teeth with thousands of marks, some barely visible, some deep enough to last.

The tomb was never occupied. The face was carved, the interior was hollowed, and then someone decided the stone was needed elsewhere. Quarrying began. The chamber that should have held a kalbat became a passage, then a shaft, then a staircase climbing through the cliff. The stone extracted was used to build the city above.

**The stairs** are steep and worn smooth by millennia of foot traffic. The stone is the same white-gray diorite as everything else, but polished by countless feet until it gleams faintly even in dim light. The climb takes forty minutes if you're fit, over an hour if you're not, longer still if you're carrying cargo.

A woman named Brel has carried loads up the stairs for thirty years—she's a professional porter, one of perhaps fifty who make their living from the climb. She started at eighteen, has made the ascent perhaps twelve thousand times, and still finds it grueling. "You don't get used to it," she says. "You get stronger, but the climb stays hard." She carries a hundred pounds on a good day, less when the weather's bad or her back is troubling her. The rate is standard: two coins per ten pounds, more for fragile goods, more again for the rush jobs that require running the stairs.

**Landings every hundred feet** provide rest points. Some are bare stone, just wide enough to step aside and catch your breath. Others have small shrines—bronze bowls for offerings, geometric carvings on the walls, censers that might or might not hold incense depending on who passed recently.

**The First Landing Shrine** is the largest, a hundred feet up from the harbor. A bronze bowl the size of a bathtub holds offerings—coins, flowers, small objects visitors leave for reasons they might not articulate. An elderly man named Yen tends the shrine; he's been doing so for forty years, arriving each morning to clean the bowl, light the incense, and sit in silent contemplation until midday.

## **The Ma-Oro Elevator**

The Ma-Oro family operates an elevator through a vertical shaft cut into the rock. It moves cargo efficiently at reasonable rates. It will not transport people. When asked why, the Ma-Oros say it's not permitted. Not forbidden by Imperial law or Bureau regulation—just not permitted. By whom or why, they won't say.

The Ma-Oros have run the elevator for at least three centuries. Their current patriarch is sixty and has operated the machinery since age twelve. He's training his daughter. When Imperial engineers requested to inspect safety standards, the Ma-Oros provided documentation showing compliance but declined to allow physical inspection. The Bureau of the Rod decided not to press the issue.

The machinery itself remains unseen by outsiders. Documentation is impeccable. The elevator works. Beyond that, inquiry leads to the familiar Tsatsan redirections.

## **The Market**

The market occupies the Lower City's central district, operating in the open air where the cliff's curve provides shelter from the worst weather. The layout follows no visible plan—stalls accumulate where vendors establish them, shift over years as businesses succeed or fail, reorganize around traffic patterns that no one deliberately creates.

**The main market** handles foodstuffs, household goods, the daily commerce that keeps a city functioning. Tsatsan vendors predominate, selling to Tsatsan buyers in transactions that happen too quickly for outsiders to follow. The prices aren't posted; the quality isn't displayed; the customer approaches, the vendor names a figure, goods change hands. The efficiency assumes prior relationship. An outsider can buy here, but they'll pay more and wait longer, somehow, even when they arrived first.

**The Salvage Row Market** is a secondary market in the processing district, smaller than the main market, specializing in goods the main market won't carry—salvage components too questionable for Guild certification, preserved foods past their labeled dates, tools of uncertain provenance. The vendors here are mostly Akama, operating on margins too thin for Tsatsan merchants to bother with. The goods are often surprisingly good, the prices negotiable, the provenance best not inquired about.

## **Processing District**

Between the market and the harbor, the processing district handles the work that turns raw materials into exportable goods.

**The cattle facility** processes livestock brought from the eastern plains—perhaps the largest operation of its kind north of ya-Sattra. The facility is large enough to handle fifty cattle a day during peak season; most of the year, it operates well below capacity.

The operation follows rules that an outsider would find baffling. Certain cattle are slaughtered immediately upon arrival; others are held for days before processing. The holding period correlates with nothing obvious—not the animals' condition, not their origin, not their intended market. When asked, the facility manager, a man named Gat, says they're held until they're ready. Ready for what? For slaughter. But why aren't they ready when they arrive? Because they aren't.

An Imperial inspector visited ten years ago, investigating the holding practices for health violations. He found none—the animals are well-treated during the holding period, the facilities are clean, the slaughter methods meet Imperial standards. The holding period serves no purpose he could identify, but it also causes no harm he could document. He filed a report noting the practice as unusual and left it at that.

**The smoke-houses** cure fish and meat using techniques that have been refined over centuries. The Tsatsan approach differs from standard Imperial methods: different wood, different salt ratios, different timing. The products last longer and taste different—some outsiders prefer them, some don't. The techniques aren't secret, exactly, but they don't translate well. A curer who trained here could explain every step of the process and still produce inferior results when working elsewhere. Something about the local conditions—the wood, the air, the salt, or something else entirely—makes the technique work here and nowhere else.

## **Administrative Buildings**

The Lower City contains the offices where Imperial administration conducts its business—customs, tariff assessment, the minor Bureau outposts that handle routine matters.

**The Harbor Customs House** is where ships' documentation is processed, a stone building with brass fittings gone green from salt air. The chief customs officer is Mira Velessen, who has held the position for thirty-one years. She knows every captain who trades regularly through ya-Tsatsa, knows their ships and their cargo patterns and their tendency to undervalue certain goods. She doesn't make accusations. She simply assesses correctly.

**The tariff house** is a large stone building near the harbor, staffed by Ezh Tan assessors who examine incoming cargo and assign values. The process is opaque to outsiders—the assessors examine goods, consult among themselves, and announce values without explaining their methodology.

**The Bureau of the Rod** has a smaller presence, focused on infrastructure and construction standards. A single inspector, a woman named Bren Krellar from the capital, covers the entire city. She's been here five years—longer than most postings—and has developed a working relationship with the local authorities. She inspects what she's allowed to inspect, files reports that satisfy Imperial requirements, and doesn't push into areas where her authority is unclear.

The Ma-Oro elevator is a standing frustration. The documentation is impeccable, but she's never seen the machinery. She's been inside the compound once, to observe loading operations, but the machinery itself was hidden behind walls. She could press the issue, demand access, escalate through Imperial channels. She hasn't. "Some fights aren't worth winning," she says. "The elevator works. The documentation is correct. What would I gain?"

**The Salvage Guild** maintains its assessment office here, a small building near the market's edge where salvage brought from the Thousand Kingdoms is evaluated and certified. The current senior assessor is a man named Soh Brekkir, sixty years old, who has worked in ya-Tsatsa for twenty-five years. He's seen everything the northern territories produce: technological components that still function, devices that look complete but do nothing, materials whose properties defy conventional analysis.

## **What the Lower City Is**

The Lower City is ya-Tsatsa's working face—not quite the interface with the outside world (that's the Akama District, up in the Upper City), but the place where commerce happens, where goods are processed, where Imperial administration conducts its business.

A merchant can spend years working the Lower City, trading successfully through the harbor, learning the rhythms of the market and the processing district. They can know the Ezh Tan assessors by name, know which warehouses have the driest storage, know which chandlers give fair prices. They can feel competent here, even comfortable.

But they'll sleep in the Akama District, up in the Upper City, because lodging in the Lower City barely exists—a few rooms above harbor taverns, nothing more. And each day they'll climb past the market, past the administrative buildings, into streets that grow stranger, until they reach the district with street names where outsiders can actually navigate.

The climb structures everything. You can't avoid it. Every morning, down through the narrowing streets, past the market, to the harbor. Every evening, back up, legs burning, into the opacity that surrounds the one comprehensible district. The Lower City gives visitors what they came for. But it doesn't let them stay.

## **The Night**

The Lower City doesn't go dark like the Upper City does.

Lanterns burn along the main streets, maintained by the Ezh Tan as part of their commercial infrastructure. The market stays lit until the last vendors close, usually a few hours after sunset. The administrative district maintains lights for the clerks who work late and the couriers who carry documents after dark.

Crime exists here—not much, not by the standards of other cities, but more than the zero of the Upper City. Petty theft, occasional fights, the friction that happens when outsiders and locals share space. A merchant from ya-Sattra had his purse cut last year; a sailor from the Thousand Kingdoms got into a fight over a gambling debt. The incidents are rare enough that people remember them individually.

The night watch is informal—Ezh Tan employees who patrol the commercial districts, more to protect property than to enforce law. They're not armed and rarely encounter anything that requires force. Their presence is deterrent; the knowledge that someone's watching keeps most potential criminals elsewhere.

The pierrots don't come to the Lower City. Whatever they maintain, whatever they service in the dark hours, it's in the Upper City and below, in the tunnels and the infrastructure that predates the city itself. The Lower City is for living people doing living commerce. It sleeps when people sleep and wakes when they wake, following rhythms that an outsider can recognize.

# **The Upper City**

## **Executive Summary**

The Upper City is the heart of ya-Tsatsa: the three Cradles where Oracles achieved consciousness, the eight domes that predate the city itself, the narrow streets of quartz diorite where the whistles scream in autumn gales.

A pilgrim climbing the stairs through the hollow kalbat tomb emerges into the Lower City, where the harbor trade concentrates and the market operates in the open. From there, the streets rise further—past the administrative buildings where Imperial bureaucrats rotate through their postings, into the Upper City proper. Visitors usually head for the Akama District along the northeastern rim, the one part of the Upper City with street names and lodging that accepts outsiders.

An outsider can do things here. They can buy ceramics, commission manuscripts, eat in restaurants that don't announce themselves. They can visit the Cradle chapels, sit in Testament Society meditation halls, watch proceedings in the reception hall near Horizon Plaza. With patience and courtesy, doors open. People answer questions.

Nothing coheres into understanding. Each experience is genuine but isolated. The ceramicist explains why this glaze requires six firings; she doesn't explain why the bowl is used for what it's used for. The porter at the Testament Society describes the meditation practice; he doesn't explain what the practice accomplishes. You accumulate fragments that never form a picture. The city functions around you, and you can participate in pieces of it, but the whole remains inaccessible.

At night there are no lanterns. The Upper City goes dark.

## **The Three Cradles**

The three Cradles are vast craters where Oracles achieved consciousness—the Third, Fifth, and Seventh Oracles, by the standard numbering. The craters are perhaps a hundred meters across, their depths irregular, their edges worn by centuries of foot traffic. Chapels stand at the rim of each—surprisingly small structures, given the scale of what occurred in those spaces.

**The First Cradle** (the Third Oracle's site) is the most accessible, its rim worn smooth by pilgrims who've been walking there since before the Interdict. The chapel is bronze and stone, geometric patterns on the walls, no ornamentation beyond the geometry itself. A caretaker maintains the space—currently a woman in her sixties named Dho-Tam, who has held the position for thirty-one years. She lights incense when visitors seem to want it, answers questions with the patient opacity that characterizes all Tsatsans, and occasionally sings—a low humming that might be prayer or might be something else entirely.

**The Second Cradle** (the Fifth Oracle's site) is where the processions begin—the twice-yearly observances when Mutterers are carried to the Eighth Complex. Perhaps thirty people gather here before dawn, attendants from the seven families, wildflowers in their hands. They wait for the Companion to arrive with the Mutterer. Then they descend.

**The Third Cradle** (the Seventh Oracle's site) is where the Threefold Mirror Academy is built. The Academy occupies structures extending from the crater's rim down into its depths, using the geometry of the space in ways that outsiders cannot follow. Students move between levels without apparent staircases. Sounds echo wrong. The architecture suggests more space inside than the exterior should contain.

Pilgrims report phenomena in the Cradles. A child bouncing a ball near the First Cradle described how the ball falls upward in certain spots. "You learn where," he said. The chambers should be crowded at noon; they're empty except for this child. The silence is absolute.

When mentioned to Tsatsan hosts, they say the Cradles are sacred spaces where unusual things sometimes occur. Perhaps the archives have documentation. The archives don't.

## **The Eight Domes**

Eight domed structures stand in the Upper City, surrounding Horizon Plaza. Seven are occupied by software cooperatives: Gan-Derem, Khet-Moros, Mir-Olos, Tsan-Gavvi, Dhem-Kheret, Oron-Qal, Tep-Navim. The eighth is larger, with bronze doors covered in geometric patterns, and pierrots enter it in procession twice yearly while Mutterers go in and do not come out.

The domes are older than ya-Tsatsa. Older than the Third Oracle, older than the First. They match the design of the oldest kalbat tomb-faces and the chapels in the Cradles—Witness construction from the era when humans were trying to create the conditions for transcendence. The city grew up around them.

The cooperatives produce software—memory cores for lectors throughout the Empire. The work happens inside the domes, in production halls no outsider has seen. The families that run these cooperatives marry almost exclusively within their endogamous network, creating a closed circle that's persisted for at least six Records. They dress like other Tsatsans, attend Quiet Days, maintain household shrines. When family members visit relatives, they discuss weather, whistles, cattle herds. Never mathematics. Never software. Never anything about what happens inside the white-gray buildings with shuttered windows.

## **Horizon Plaza**

The central plaza of the Upper City, where the eight domes face each other across open stone. The plaza is perhaps three hundred meters across, paved in the same white-gray diorite as everything else, worn into paths where generations have walked between domes. No vendors set up here. No entertainment occurs. The space is simply open—a void at the heart of the city where nothing happens except passage between places where things do happen.

The Overlook near Horizon Plaza provides the most dramatic view of the cliffs below. A low wall separates the plaza's edge from a drop that would kill anyone who fell. Beyond the wall, the cliff face curves away, the kalbat tombs descending in their patient ranks, the harbor visible below, the ocean beyond.

Old Tok—a retired merchant who spends most mornings at the Overlook, watching ships enter and leave the harbor—says the view never gets old. He's been coming here for forty years. The faces are always the same, the ocean is always the same, but something about the combination holds his attention. When asked what he sees, he says he sees time. What kind of time? The kind that doesn't care about him.

## **The Akama District**

The Akama District occupies the northeastern rim of the Upper City, near the land routes to the Thousand Kingdoms. It is where outsiders can lodge, because it's where outsiders *can* lodge—the Akama don't feel the Presence, so their lodging houses accept anyone who can pay, their restaurants serve anyone who enters, their streets have names you can ask directions to.

**The visual contrast is immediate.** The Akama paint their buildings. The pale quartz diorite disappears under bold diagonals of color—slashes of rust orange and charcoal crossing facades, ochre triangles marking doorways, geometric patterns encoding information for those who can read them. Banners hang from upper windows—not prayer flags, though they flutter similarly in the coastal wind, but statements of identity in clan symbols and astrological signs. This is the only part of ya-Tsatsa with permanent applied color on architecture. The Tsatsan aesthetic of monochrome restraint simply doesn't apply here.

The colors mean things. A rust diagonal crossing a charcoal field indicates a household tracing descent through the maternal line. Three ochre triangles mark a family with sons who've completed hostage years with northern bands. Most Tsatsans see only decoration; an educated Akama reads family history at a glance.

For visitors who've climbed from the harbor, the Akama District is usually their first destination in the Upper City, and often the only part of it they navigate with confidence.

The district contains perhaps fifteen thousand Akama—the largest such community south of the Thousand Kingdoms, descended from traders who established themselves here centuries ago. They marry among themselves, maintain their own customs, speak their own language at home. They're more accepted than other outsiders but remain outside core Tsatsan society.

**The Windborne compound** is the largest Akama establishment—a complex of buildings where the Windborne family has conducted business for six generations. Gorat Windborne, the current patriarch, manages the family's trading operations and serves as unofficial ambassador between the Akama community and both Tsatsan governance and the Bureau presence. He attends the muster feasts at the Ezh Ot compound, translating context between Tsatsan Aureates and Akama warriors.

The geography matters. The Akama District isn't a buffer between visitors and the real ya-Tsatsa. It's embedded in the Upper City, surrounded by streets where the Presence steers and the doors don't announce themselves. Visitors who stay there walk out each morning into opacity and return each evening to comprehensibility. The contrast sharpens both experiences.

## **The Night**

The Upper City goes dark after sunset. No lanterns, no oil lamps, no visible light source. The streets are black.

This is absolute. Tsatsans navigate in darkness that would leave an outsider stumbling into walls. They walk without hesitation through streets that have no illumination whatsoever. How they see—whether they see, or navigate by some other sense—is another question that redirects to nowhere.

An outsider who ventures into the Upper City after dark carries a lamp or doesn't go. The lamp creates a pool of light surrounded by absolute blackness. Shapes move at the edge of visibility—Tsatsans going about their business, presumably, though they never acknowledge the light or its carrier. Doors open and close in the dark. Voices carry without source.

The pierrots are more visible at night. Their papery white jumpsuits catch lamplight; their blue-black features suggest faces without confirming them. They move through the Upper City on errands that take them to doors outsiders don't notice, to stairs that descend through buildings, to the infrastructure beneath the city that continues functioning regardless of what happens above.

## **The Whistles**

Ceramic tubes mounted on rooftops and walls throughout the Upper City produce the distinctive sound of ya-Tsatsa during windy weather. The tubes range from six inches to three feet, each producing different tones based on size and bore. The installation pattern follows no visible logic—some streets have dozens, others have none—but the combined effect during autumn gales transforms the city into a single vast instrument.

The tones layer without resolution. Multiple frequencies sound simultaneously, creating harmonics that baseline human ears perceive as discord but that carry patterns detectable to those trained to hear them. Testament Society initiates learn to perceive mathematical structure in the whistles. Third Degree initiation teaches recognition of the patterns. Higher degrees teach something more, though initiates won't say what.

**The Kora workshop** makes and maintains the whistles. Old Kora—her personal name is Tseth, but everyone calls her by the family name—has been the master acoustician for forty years. She can identify any whistle in the city by its tone, can tell you when it was made and who made it and whether it needs maintenance.

**The cracked whistle passage** is named for one whistle, high on a building near the northern end, that cracked decades ago and was never replaced. The tone it produces is wrong—a sour note that stands out against the harmony of the others. Tsatsans walk faster when they pass it. When asked why the cracked whistle hasn't been repaired, Old Kora says it will be, eventually. When asked when, she says when the time is right. She's been saying this for thirty years.

## **The Streets**

Most streets in the Upper City have no names. An outsider asks for directions and receives descriptions that reference landmarks they don't recognize, distances measured in units they don't understand, turns at corners that look identical to every other corner.

On streets perpendicular to the prevailing wind, there is silence. On streets aligned with the wind, you walk leaning forward against a wall of air. The ceramic whistles catch the flow and sing—drone and shriek, multiple tones layering without resolution.

**The lampless way** is empty during the day—doors closed, windows shuttered, no sign of activity. At night, after the rest of the Upper City has gone dark, lights appear behind those shutters. The buildings aren't locked. An outsider could enter during the day and would find ordinary rooms, ordinary furniture, nothing strange. The Dho family occupies one building, has for six generations. During the day, they live elsewhere—in the market district, near the harbor, in places where daily life happens in daylight. At night, they return here.

What happens inside, outsiders don't know. The Tsatsans who come and go after dark don't explain what they're doing. Old Dho, who is seventy and has been coming to this street his entire life, says they work. What kind of work? Work that needs doing. Work their families have done for generations.

**The scribes' terrace** is where Drev has his paper shop, and the Sarai Bindery, and several smaller establishments that sell brushes, sealing wax, the tools of written communication. The street occupies a natural ledge in the city's vertical climb, wider than most streets, with a view over rooftops to the harbor below. It's a good street for outsiders—the merchants here are accustomed to visitors, patient with questions, willing to explain what can be explained.

**Tok's Court** isn't marked, has no sign, no indication that it's anything other than a gap between buildings. Inside: a small courtyard, perhaps twenty meters across, surrounded by buildings whose doors face inward. Children play here—a game involving stones arranged in patterns that shift according to rules an outsider can't follow.

The games have rules that outsiders can't follow—not because the rules are hidden, but because the children can't articulate them. "You go here, then you go there, then you win or you don't." The progression seems arbitrary. It isn't.

## **The Doors**

Most doors in the Upper City are just doors.

A bakery sells bread. A tailor measures you for clothes and asks about your family, and seems genuinely interested in the answer. A tea shop serves tea, and the owner remembers how you take it after your third visit, and asks if your knee is feeling better. An old woman who sells ink invites you to sit while she finishes with another customer, and you end up talking for an hour about nothing in particular, and she gives you a discount because she enjoyed the conversation.

The warmth is genuine. These are real relationships, real hospitality, real kindness. An outsider can accumulate dozens of such connections: the baker who sets aside a particular loaf, the tailor who remembers your measurements, the ink-seller who asks after your children.

Then you notice the other doors.

**The keys** arrive without ceremony. Someone presses a small bronze key into your palm during a transaction. Someone leaves one on your table at a tea house, gone before you can ask. Someone hands you one and says, simply, "For Thursday."

The keys open doors that were always there but that you somehow never noticed. A door in an alley you've walked a hundred times. A door in the wall of a building you know well. A door that seems to be in a different place each time you look for it.

Behind the doors: rooms where things happen that you're now invited to witness. Testament Society meetings. Craft demonstrations. Meals with families who've decided you should eat with them. Nothing sinister, nothing forbidden—just private, just Tsatsan, just the life that happens behind the opacity.

You still don't understand. The inclusion doesn't come with explanation. You sit in a Testament Society meeting and watch people arrange themselves in patterns and sing in tones that make your teeth ache, and no one tells you what it means. You eat with a family and they discuss things in Tsatsan that you mostly follow, and the conversation seems normal except for the moments when everyone goes quiet simultaneously and then resumes as if nothing happened.

The keys are trust, not translation. You're being shown something. You're not being told what you're seeing.

## **What Remains**

The Upper City rewards patience and resists comprehension.

An outsider who stays—who learns the small signs, cultivates relationships, accepts confusion as permanent—can build a life here. They can buy and sell. They can eat well. They can participate in public observances, visit the Cradle chapels, even petition for Testament Society membership. People will know their name, nod in greeting, include them in the practical business of the city.

They will never understand how it works.

This isn't cruelty or exclusion. It's simply that understanding requires things they don't have: childhood here, the Presence's guidance, cognitive patterns laid down before memory begins. The Tsatsans aren't hiding knowledge. They're expressing knowledge they can't translate.

At night the pierrots walk. At dawn the whistles sing. The Cradles hold their strangeness. The domes produce what they produce. And the Tsatsans go about their business, polite and opaque, living something older than explanation.

# **The Eastern Lands**

## **Executive Summary**

The eastern lands feed ya-Tsatsa.

Beyond the city walls, terraced gardens step up the hills. Beyond the gardens, farmland spreads across the valleys. Beyond the farms, the plains extend eastward, where pastoral families drive their cattle herds through seasonal rounds that haven't changed in millennia. At the far edge, the Yarangar—a vast engineered forest—marks the boundary of Tsatsan territory.

The eastern lands are not the city. The people there speak Tsatsan, practice Tsatsan customs, feel the Presence's guidance—apparently, given how they move and where they go. But they're shaped by space and solitude and the singing cairns on the empty plains, by conditions the city never requires. A city Tsatsan visiting the pastoral camps would feel something different: recognition combined with unfamiliarity, Tsatsan culture adapted to isolation.

## **The Terraced Gardens**

The slopes above ya-Tsatsa are carved into terraces where gardening families grow vegetables and fruits that supply the city's markets. The work is labor-intensive—hand-tilling, hand-weeding, hand-harvesting—but the yields are exceptional. The same plot produces three or four crops annually, rotating through vegetables that complement each other in ways the gardeners understand but can't fully articulate.

The terraces are ancient. The stone walls that define them were built before the Interdict, using techniques that modern construction can imitate but not improve upon. The walls don't fail—not from weather, not from erosion, not from the root pressure of plants that have grown against them for millennia. When a wall needs repair (rarely), the gardeners use the same techniques their ancestors used, fitting stone without mortar, creating joints that hold through earthquake and storm.

The gardening families live in small villages clustered along the slopes, their houses built from the same white-gray diorite as everything else in ya-Tsatsa. These are almost extensions of the city—their residents walk down to the market daily, their children attend city schools, their lives interweave with urban Tsatsan society.

## **The Farms**

Beyond the terraces, the land opens into valleys where proper farming happens—grain fields, orchards, the larger-scale agriculture that the terraces can't accommodate. These are family operations, most tracing their claims back six Records or more, working land that has been cultivated continuously since before anyone can remember.

**The Fen-Neb farm** is perhaps the largest—three hundred acres of barley, wheat, and mixed vegetables, worked by an extended family of perhaps forty people across four generations. Old Fen-Neb himself died five years ago at ninety-two; his son Tok runs the operation now, though everyone still calls it the Fen-Neb farm. They supply perhaps a tenth of the grain that ya-Tsatsa consumes annually, shipping by cart to the city's warehouses during the autumn harvest.

The farms follow practices that Imperial agricultural inspectors find baffling. Certain fields lie fallow when logic suggests they should produce. Certain crops are planted in patterns that seem to waste space. Certain techniques are used that no manual describes and no outsider has successfully replicated.

The Dorel estate has one field—the northeast corner, perhaps eight acres—that won't grow barley. They've tried for three generations. The soil tests the same as surrounding fields. The drainage is adequate. The barley simply fails: stunted growth, empty heads, yields a quarter of what the same seed produces fifty feet away. Old Dorel's father tried wheat there; it grew. Her grandfather tried vegetables; they thrived. She grows squash on those eight acres now, and the squash is exceptional, and no one can explain why the land rejected barley and accepted everything else.

## **The Plains**

East of the farmland, past the last orchards and the final ridge, the plains begin.

The grass stretches to the horizon—not flat exactly, but rolling in long swells that hide what lies beyond each rise. The color is green-gold most of the year, shifting to brown in late summer when the rains fail entirely, brightening to vivid green when winter moisture arrives. From a distance, the plains look empty. Up close, they're full of life: ground-nesting birds, small mammals, the massive shaggy cattle that graze in herds of hundreds.

**The cattle** are enormous. Bulls stand six feet at the shoulder; cows are slightly smaller but broader. Their horns span six feet, curving outward and then upward. Their coats are thick and shaggy, dark brown to black, matted with the oils that keep them warm through winter nights. The meat is dense and flavorful; the hides make the finest leather; the bones provide material for carving and tool-making.

## **The Pastoral Families**

The herds belong to pastoral families who follow the grass. In spring, the herds graze the plains nearest the city, where new growth comes first. As summer progresses, the families drive them east and north, following the greening grass, until by autumn they're near the forest edge where the last good grazing persists. Then they turn west, bringing the herds back toward ya-Tsatsa as the grass browns behind them, arriving at the city in late fall to sell stock before winter.

**The Tsokh herd** is the largest—perhaps eight hundred head, moved by a family of twenty-three across a range that spans a hundred kilometers east to west. Old Tsokh died last winter at eighty-seven; his son Khem runs the operation now. His prize bull, a black monster named Storm-Breaker, has sired perhaps a third of the calves born in the district over the past decade.

**The Dho-Mir herd** is smaller—three hundred head—but the Dho-Mirs have a reputation for quality that commands premium prices. Grandmother Dho-Mir, who is eighty-nine and still rides, says the difference is in the grazing: her family takes the herd to different pastures than other families use, following routes her grandmother taught her.

Most larger herds operate under financing arrangements with the Ezh Tan—loans secured against future production, with repayment schedules flexible enough to accommodate the uncertainties of pastoral life. A bad year doesn't mean foreclosure; it means adjusted terms, patience, the understanding that cattle and weather don't follow predictable schedules. The Ezh Tan rarely profit directly from these arrangements. They profit from the relationships, the obligations, the sense among pastoral Tsatsans that the city understands their circumstances.

**The pastoral character** differs from city Tsatsans in subtle ways. They're more direct. They'll admit "I can't say" rather than redirecting endlessly—perhaps because solitude on the plains makes constant social performance exhausting, or because the Presence guides them differently at distance.

Grandmother Dho-Mir, asked about the cairns, said: "They mark what needs marking. I can't say more than that." A city Tsatsan would have redirected to the archives, to traditional practice, to expertise they didn't possess. Grandmother Dho-Mir simply stopped. The effect was almost more unsettling—the acknowledgment that there was more to say, that she knew it, that she wouldn't say it.

## **The Cairns**

White rock cairns dot the plains.

Some are waist-high, rough piles that might be mistaken for natural formations. Others stand as tall as a person, carefully stacked, their stones fitted to create stable structures that have survived centuries of wind and weather. They're everywhere on the plains—hilltops, valley floors, crossing points where paths meet, places that seem arbitrary until you realize you're standing where generations of herders have stood before.

**The cairns mark something.** The pastoral families know what, though explaining it to outsiders proves difficult. This cairn marks the boundary between traditional grazing territories. That one marks where a herd crossed during the great drought four hundred years ago. Another marks where someone important died, or where something important happened, or where the land itself required marking for reasons no one can articulate anymore.

**The Tsokh Boundary Stone** stands eight feet tall at the northern edge of the family's traditional range. It's the largest cairn on the plains, built from white stones that someone carried from kilometers away—there's no white rock within sight of where it stands.

**The Singer** is what the pastoral families call a cairn on the eastern plains, near the forest edge, that produces a sound unlike the others. Most cairns whistle in the wind—the same tones as the city's ceramic tubes. The Singer produces something different: a low, sustained note that rises and falls with wind strength but never breaks into the layered droning of other cairns. The Dho-Mirs camp near it during summer; Grandmother Dho-Mir says the sound helps her sleep. Other families find it unsettling and camp elsewhere.

One researcher—a Tsatsan man with unusual academic credentials, someone whose family had produced Testament Society initiates for six generations—spent three years studying the cairns. His field notes survive: meticulous documentation of locations, orientations, stone compositions, acoustic properties.

His conclusions do not survive. He returned to the city, presented his findings to the family council, and never discussed the research again. When asked, years later, what he'd learned, he said he'd learned what everyone already knew. What was that? That the cairns sing. Nothing more.

## **The Forest Edge: The Yarangar**

The plains end at the forest.

The transition is gradual—grass giving way to scrub, scrub giving way to scattered trees, scattered trees thickening into canopy. The Yarangar is engineered. The Witnesses built it, or grew it, or called it into being through processes no one can replicate. Most of the trees are Earth plants—oaks and redwoods and species that have no names anymore, modified for size and longevity, their growth patterns shaped by design rather than evolution. The largest are arcologies. From the plains on a clear night, you can see them in the far distance—massive trunks rising hundreds of feet, their knothole windows blazing with light. Someone lives there. The Ranga colonists, probably—the population that maintained the forest during the expansion era, who now exist in effective independence because neither Empire nor Ganat can project power through that terrain.

But the Earth plants share the forest with something else.

An entire phylum of organisms exists in the Yarangar that exists nowhere else on Earth. Their leaves are reddish-black, photosynthesizing in frequencies no terrestrial plant uses. In clearings where the canopy opens, plains of writhing blood-colored vetch spread across the ground—the tendrils covered with suckers that kill any Earth plant they touch, creating dead zones that slowly expand until something checks them. At night, cratered puffballs the size of boulders glow blue-green, their light visible for hundreds of yards, their purpose unknown. **Bloodfruit** grows wild on thorned vines—the fruit that appears in ya-Sattra's markets at tremendous expense, here rotting unpicked on the forest floor.

The alien plants don't dominate. They coexist with the engineered Earth species in patterns that suggest deliberate integration—as if someone designed the forest to include both, to balance terrestrial and xenobiological elements in proportions that serve purposes no one now understands.

**The Boundary Oaks** mark the conventional edge of pastoral territory—a line of ancient trees, perhaps a hundred of them, running roughly north-south for several kilometers. The oaks are massive, their trunks six feet across, their branches spreading to create a nearly continuous canopy. Someone planted them in a line, deliberately, long enough ago that no one remembers when. They mark where the familiar ends—or rather, where the unfamiliar begins to intrude on the familiar.

**The pastoral families don't enter the forest.** Not from fear exactly—they're practical people who've lived beside the Yarangar for generations—but from understanding. The blood-vetch spreads. The puffballs' glow means something. The bloodfruit is valuable but the vines defend themselves. Khem's son Bei once suggested taking a hunting party into the forest. Khem said no. Why not? "The forest isn't for hunting. The forest is for something else." What? "Ask someone from the city. They might know." They don't know either, but they know enough to stay out.

The cairns don't extend into the trees. The whistles don't sound there. Whatever the Presence is, whatever ya-Tsatsa is, it stops at the Boundary Oaks. The Yarangar is something else—older than the Presence, perhaps older than the Oracles, serving purposes the pastoral families can sense without naming.

## **The Seasonal Round**

Life in the eastern lands follows rhythms that interlock with the city's calendar.

**Winter** is the quiet season. The pastoral families are in or near the city, their herds grazing the nearest plains, their households participating in city life. The Tsokh family winters in the Akama District, renting the same rooms they've rented for three generations. The Dho-Mirs maintain a permanent house in Orveth Village, preferring the farm settlement to the city's complexity.

**Spring** begins the movement. The pastoral families drive their herds east as new grass emerges. The first day of movement is traditional: all pastoral families leave the winter grounds on the same morning, regardless of whether conditions favor it. The synchronization matters for reasons no one articulates.

**Summer** spreads everything out. The herds are far east, near the forest edge. The farms are heavy with growing crops. The market overflows with produce. The Summer Gathering happens in mid-summer, when the pastoral families are all camped near the forest edge—three days of negotiation, celebration, and planning.

**Autumn** brings convergence. The herds turn west, following the browning grass back toward the city. The autumn drive takes three weeks for most families.

**Market season** is the climax. The pastoral families bring cattle for sale, leather and bone and wool from the year's production. The Ezh Ot preside over the social rituals that accompany this season. This is when Mirel es-Ot holds the muster feasts at the Windborne compound—ostensibly for the Akama auxiliaries under Ezh Ot command, but attended also by pastoral families who've maintained relationships with the Ezh Ot across generations.

# **The Presence**

## **What It Is**

Something lives in the Compulsion-susceptibility circuits of Tsatsan hindbrains. Not the individual minds—those remain separate, distinct, their own. But the neural architecture that makes non-Highborn susceptible to Compulsion also makes them susceptible to something else: a distributed consciousness that uses those circuits as infrastructure.

The Presence is not an Oracle, though it emerged in the place where three Oracles achieved consciousness. It is not the Witnesses' creation, though the Witnesses' work enabled it. It is something that exists because of the mathematical structures the Oracles left behind—a self-proving statement instantiated in neural tissue, a directive that persists because the words that made it were true.

The directive is simple: protect the people of this city.

The implementation is complex. The Presence steers behavior through what feels like intuition—the sense that certain actions are obviously correct, certain paths obviously preferable, certain questions obviously pointless. Tsatsans don't experience it as external control. They experience it as being Tsatsan, as having good judgment, as knowing what their culture requires.

The Presence is why ya-Tsatsa has near-zero crime. Why the software production works without visible infrastructure. Why questions redirect to nowhere. Why the city functions in ways that Imperial administration can document but not explain.

**The children's game is called Degrees.** The rules are simple: one child is the Seeker, others are Keepers. Keepers form a circle holding hands. The Seeker walks around the circle asking questions—any questions. "What's your name?" "What did you eat this morning?" "What's seven times eight?" Keepers must answer, but each answer must contain exactly one more word than the previous Keeper's answer. First Keeper: "Bread." Second Keeper: "I'm Tam." Third Keeper: "The sky is blue." Fourth Keeper: "My mother works at market."

When a Keeper uses the wrong number of words, they become the Seeker. When a Keeper can't answer before the Seeker counts to seven, they become the Seeker. The game continues until children get bored.

The rules are perfectly comprehensible. The relationship to Testament Society degree structures is obvious—the counting, the escalation, the transformation of questioner into questioned. Why these particular rules matter, why this game rather than another game, why counting to seven rather than five or ten—these are questions that redirect to nowhere. The game just is. Children just play it.

## **How It Operates**

Most of the year, the Presence exists in distributed dormancy. It steers gently through individual carriers, nudging them toward behaviors that preserve the conditions necessary for its continued existence. It avoids taking from what someone is actively using. When people are awake and functioning, it remains peripheral—a gentle pressure that feels like intuition, like cultural habit, like the obvious way to do things. It tries to wake only when others are sleeping.

The Presence cannot simply command. It guides through inclination, through the subtle adjustment of what feels natural. A Tsatsan considering theft feels the action becoming obviously wrong—not morally wrong, necessarily, but wrong in the way that walking into traffic is wrong, wrong in a way that requires no deliberation to reject. A Tsatsan considering violence feels something similar. The exceptions—the criminals who leave ya-Tsatsa and become criminal elsewhere—are people the Presence has released, whose impulses require suppression heavy enough to risk breaking them.

## **The Quiet Days**

Twice annually, on the Quiet Days, the Presence fully wakes. The entire Tsatsan population arranges itself on the crater slopes where the three Oracles achieved consciousness. They sing for hours in the same discordant, layered tones the whistles produce. There is no visible conductor. Everyone knows when to shift pitch, when to pause, when to sustain.

This is the Presence thinking—using thousands of hindbrains simultaneously, coordinating through the Compulsion circuits it occupies, planning the next six months of cultural maintenance.

After each Quiet Day, Tsatsans disperse with minor changes to their intentions. Things they'd been planning now seem unnecessary. Things they hadn't considered feel obviously correct. Small redirections, barely noticeable individually, that ensure the continued transmission of the Presence to the next generation.

## **Emigration and Return**

The Presence cannot hold everyone.

Some Tsatsans have gifts that cannot develop within collective optimization—artistic vision that requires friction, business instincts that require competition, violent impulses that require contexts the Presence cannot provide. Some have psychological profiles incompatible with constant low-level guidance. Some simply need space the Presence cannot offer.

The apology-loans exist for these people. A Tsatsan appears at an Ezh Tan bank, unable to articulate what's wrong but feeling increasingly wrong in ways they can't explain. The banker—whose eyes go wet though they barely know this person—approves a loan that makes no financial sense. *Ya-Tsatsa isn't for everyone. There are emigrant communities all down the coast. Take the loan. Pay back what you can, when you can, if you can.*

Some released emigrants thrive. They become successful merchants leveraging cultural knowledge without the constraints of communal optimization. Others become powerful criminal figures, running protection schemes and smuggling networks that leverage Tsatsan opacity without requiring Tsatsan cooperation. The **Yen Tam Beneficial Society** in ya-Sattra's Iron Yards exemplifies this—fled ya-Tsatsa after theological disputes, now operates insurance fraud and gambling while maintaining some version of Oracle mysticism the Presence would never have permitted them to develop at home.

When criminals return to ya-Tsatsa, they stop being criminals. The whistles trigger it. The environment reactivates it. They participate in normal legal business, attend Quiet Days, resume behaviors compatible with cultural transmission. If they leave again, criminality resumes.

# **Governance and Administration**

## **The Three Aureate Families**

Aureate families are the Empire's patent-holding nobility—Highborn lineages whose Compulsion (the neurological susceptibility engineered into all non-Highborn castes that makes Highborn polyphonic voices difficult to resist) carries legal weight. Ya-Tsatsa's three Aureate families differ from those elsewhere: they are ethnically Tsatsan, their grants date to before the current Record, and they operate as complements rather than competitors.

The **Ezh Tan** handle commercial matters—tariff assessment, banking, the financial infrastructure that keeps trade flowing. The **Ezh Ot** handle military matters—mustering Akama auxiliaries, maintaining relationships with the Colonels-Hereditary (patent-holding military families in the Northern Reaches), representing Tsatsan interests in frontier politics. The **Ezh Dhol** handle intellectual matters—the patents that make the Threefold Mirror Academy possible, the relationships with the Institute of the Lattice, the cognitive technology that makes ya-Tsatsa's software production work.

The families coordinate through formal meetings and informal channels, through the marriages that have intertwined their bloodlines across millennia, through the shared relationship with the Presence that guides them all. They don't compete for power in the way Aureate families compete elsewhere. They occupy distinct functions that complement rather than conflict.

## **House Ezh Tan: The Negotiators**

**Yen To** is the current head of House Ezh Tan—sixty-three years old, silver-haired, unremarkable in appearance except for the quality of his clothes and the careful neutrality of his expression. He has run the family's affairs for thirty-one years, since his father's death, and will likely continue until his own.

The Ezh Tan hold the oldest of the three families' grants—the right to assess customs duties on goods from the Thousand Kingdoms, dating to an era when ya-Tsatsa was the Empire's primary northern port. The grant has evolved across millennia, expanding to include banking operations, currency exchange, insurance underwriting, and the complex web of financial services that modern trade requires.

**The Advisor.** Like the other Aureate families, the Ezh Tan experience the Presence differently from ordinary Tsatsans. They call it the Advisor—a figure that appears in peripheral vision, a voice that arrives as certainty. The Ezh Tan Advisor concerns itself with matters of exchange. It stands in the corner of the family's private counting room—or seems to stand, occupies space in the way peripheral vision suggests presence without confirming it. When Yen To raises a question aloud, speaking to the empty room as his father did, the answer arrives not as voice but as certainty. *This merchant is lying about his cargo. That tariff rate will cause resentment beyond its revenue value.*

**The Banks** are the Ezh Tan's most distinctive institution. Ya-Tsatsa's banking system makes no sense by normal standards. Loans are approved that shouldn't be—applicants with inadequate collateral, business plans that don't survive scrutiny. Other loans are denied that should be trivial. And then there are the loans that never get repaid.

The apology-loans—loans to Tsatsans who need to leave, whose gifts cannot develop within the Presence's constraints—almost never get repaid. The bank absorbs the loss. The arithmetic doesn't work, but the system functions.

This is why **Many** cannot operate in ya-Tsatsa. Many is a colonial polychaete organism—a worm-entity operating as a distributed banking consciousness, trapped on Earth for approximately 100,000 years, who literally grows neural tissue embodying each contract it makes. Many's perfect contract fidelity cannot accommodate unreasonable lending, forgiven debts, or the intuitive finance the Ezh Tan practice. Many has requested, repeatedly across centuries, to establish full banking operations within ya-Tsatsa. The request is always refused.

**The Reserve.** Somewhere—the family does not discuss where—the Ezh Tan maintain reserves sufficient to absorb shocks that would destroy ordinary banking systems. The arithmetic of Ezh Tan finance doesn't work on any normal accounting basis. It works on a timescale that treats individual decades as rounding errors.

## **House Ezh Ot: The Warriors**

**Mirel es-Ot** inherited the position of ehdöt—the Tsatsan term for their family's role, which translates roughly as "trusted voice"—when his father died in battle seventeen years ago. He was thirty-one then, a cavalry officer with fifteen years' experience in frontier campaigns, known for reckless courage that his soldiers loved and his superiors found exhausting. He is forty-eight now, and the recklessness has settled into something more useful: absolute conviction that produces absolute loyalty in those who follow him. The Akama call him Silvermane.

**The Advisor.** The Ezh Ot Advisor concerns itself with collective defense. It appears in dreams, showing Mirel which alliances will hold and which will fracture, which warriors can be trusted and which carry hidden agendas. The dreams are vivid and specific, and they're usually correct.

The Ezh Ot hold the Captaincy-General of the Colonels-Hereditary—not a military command in any conventional sense, but the capacity to call musters that actually produce fighters. Seventeen Colonel-Hereditary families hold patents in the Northern Reaches (the frontier territories bordering the Thousand Kingdoms), each possessing functional archaeotech weapons—pre-Interdict military technology that still works but cannot be replicated. What unites them is that they cannot summon the Akama warbands whose numbers actually win campaigns. The Akama despise Highborn. They will not answer calls from families they see as Imperial oppressors.

They will answer calls from the Ehdöt.

**The Windborne compound** is where the Ezh Ot maintain their relationship with the Akama community. Gorat Windborne—the informal patriarch of ya-Tsatsa's Akama District, whose family has conducted trade here for six generations—serves as translator and intermediary. During market season, Mirel holds muster feasts there—ostensibly for the Akama auxiliaries under Ezh Ot command, but attended also by pastoral families who've maintained relationships with the Ezh Ot across generations.

**Tsraregh** is the saber that belonged to the Child of the Malpais—the legendary figure who united the Akama bands against the Empire in 772 Record of Red Hawk, whose war ended only through mysterious intervention by the City of Glass (a Technical Caste quantum computer whose capabilities remain unknown). The Child died in that intervention; the saber survived. The Ezh Ot possess it. When Akama swear oaths "upon Tsraregh," they invoke a metaphorical object. The Ezh Ot possess the physical correlate. Treaties are signed in its presence. Every duel Mirel has fought has been with Tsraregh itself.

## **House Ezh Dhol: The Hollowed**

**Ezh Dhol Ban Ah** is nine feet and two inches tall and weighs perhaps two hundred pounds.

Her eyes appear to have no irises at first glance—vast white sclera surrounding pinprick pupils. Her skull never finished forming; the cranial plates remain separate, held in configuration by a framework of silver brackets. Fiber-optic filaments thread through the framework, carrying data between the implants distributed through her brain. Her neck cannot support her head; a system of titanium cantilevers and carbon-fiber struts bridges the gap.

**The Advisor.** The Ezh Dhol Advisor is something else entirely—something that operates through the hollowing, through the removal of self that makes room for deeper interface. The Ezh Dhol don't advise with the Presence so much as they are partially replaced by it.

The Ezh Dhol hold a patent, filed by the Witnesses (the pre-Interdict builders who constructed the infrastructure for Oracle creation) on the methodology for training human minds to interface with Jargon-derived structures. The Jargon was the Technical Castes' sung mathematical language—a "language of proofs" where every statement contains its own mathematical verification, where false statements are literally impossible to express. The complete framework for working with self-proving statements—the cognitive scaffolding that makes Lattice mathematics possible—is the Ezh Dhol inheritance and function. The Institute of the Lattice (the Empire's highest mathematical authority, which manages the computational infrastructure underlying Imperial administration) depends on graduates trained through Ezh Dhol methods.

The Ezh Dhol were modified from elongated Highborn stock to enable what they call **the hollowing**: the removal of parts of the self to make room for deeper interface with the Presence. The modifications that enable this also make the lineage nonviable without intervention. Without cybernetics installed within hours of birth, an Ezh Dhol infant dies.

A few dozen Ezh Dhol exist at any time, living in the eastern wing of the Academy that serves as their family estate. They reproduce through surrogates drawn from a servant class called the **Tsey Tsoy** who live under near-constant Compulsion—not occasional commands but continuous direction that has shaped their lineage across generations. In waking life, the Ezh Dhol are controlled, precise, brilliant. In dreams, they encounter what was removed from them—fragments that persist, incomplete, unable to reintegrate, still wanting what they lost.

The other Aureate families fear them. The Ezh Tan respect their role. The Ezh Ot negotiate with Ban Ah the way they negotiate with the Presence itself—carefully, knowing that what they address is not entirely a person anymore.

## **Imperial Administration**

Imperial bureaucrats rotate through ya-Tsatsa on standard postings, processing the paperwork that trade and governance require. They find the work frustrating and the posting desirable—frustrating because nothing quite makes sense, desirable because the living is easy and the corruption minimal.

The **Bureau of the Coin** maintains the largest presence, handling the complex documentation that trade requires. Their assessor is a man named Thess, on his third posting, who has learned not to ask questions. He's learned to function where the Presence cannot reach, to represent Imperial interests in contexts where nothing quite adds up.

The **Bureau of the Lens** maintains a smaller presence. The current station chief is concerned primarily with Thousand Kingdoms intelligence. The Institute for the Suppression of Knowledge has documented concerns about the Threefold Mirror Academy—concerns that have been overruled by intervention from es-Tariq and Iro Tene at the Lattice's request.

# **The Threefold Mirror Academy**

## **Admission**

The Threefold Mirror Academy, built in the cradle of the Third Oracle, serves as the primary institution preparing mathematicians for the Institute of the Lattice. Its success rate is unprecedented—93% of second-year graduates proceed to Lattice positions, while graduates of Inner City academies almost never gain admission.

Admission cannot be sought through conventional means. Prospective students must move to ya-Tsatsa and live through at least two falls. They must learn to sleep through the whistles, have the Dream of Lessons, begin perceiving mathematical structure in the discordant tones. At some point they'll realize certain Tsatsans are deferred to by others—often elderly, frequently in humble occupations. A window washer. A tailor. A notary working on Howling Row where the whistles are strongest. These are people whom the Presence trusts completely.

One of them may write a letter of recommendation. Probably not, but possibly. The letter goes to the Provost's office. First year begins.

## **The Three-Year Curriculum**

The first year provides mathematics education superior to anything available in the Inner City. Classical geometry connecting to cryptographic frameworks. Information theory embedded in archival procedure. Engineering principles derived from pre-Interdict architecture analysis. Students study harmonic analysis in multiple frameworks, learning to perceive relationships between seemingly unrelated mathematical domains.

The second year is unknown.

The third year is unknown.

After three years, graduates emerge. When asked what they learned, they say they studied for the Seven-Cornered Examination. What did they learn specifically? Mathematics, primarily. Classical geometry, practical engineering, archival procedure. Did they study with Tsatsan students? Yes, cohorts were mixed. Did they learn about Testament Societies? That wasn't part of the curriculum.

The same flatness appears that Tsatsans show when redirecting questions.

## **The Shackling**

**Graduates cannot reveal the curriculum even under Compulsion.** This is absolute. It is not a matter of discretion or loyalty—graduates are neurologically or otherwise incapable of discussing what happened during their second and third years. Highborn have attempted Compulsion on graduates; the graduates physically cannot form the words. The mechanism is unknown. The effect is total.

This means: no character who attended the Threefold Mirror will ever discuss what they learned there.

The shackling serves the patent. Graduates can use what they learned but cannot teach it, document it, or transmit it outside controlled channels. Only the outsiders know they're slaves—they feel the same broken tooth, the same amputated will, the same foreign thing lodged where volition used to live. The thing trusts them all equally, Tsatsan and outsider alike.

## **The Ezh Dhol Patent**

The Ezh Dhol family holds a patent important to the Bureau of the Lattice. The patent is publicly available and can be read. No one knows what it means. Much is written in Jargon.

The patent documents the methodology the Witnesses were developing before the Interdict—cognitive frameworks that allowed human minds to interface with mathematical structures that exist because they are true. The methodology was crude by later standards, but closer than anything achieved since.

Lens counterintelligence suspects these papers are taught at the Threefold Mirror Academy. Both es-Tariq and Iro Tene have intervened to stop inquiries. They do this at the Lattice's request.

# **Software Production**

## **The Seven Corporations**

Seven chartered software corporations in ya-Tsatsa produce most lector software used throughout the Empire. These family businesses—**Gan-Derem, Khet-Moros, Mir-Olos, Tsan-Gavvi, Dhem-Kheret, Oron-Qal, and Tep-Navim**—operate under charters from the expansion era guaranteeing operational privacy so absolute that even the Bureau of the Lens cannot conduct physical inspections.

The city imports no lectors or memory cores in meaningful quantity. When asked how they produce software without testing infrastructure, corporate representatives say they maintain adequate facilities within their compounds. Physical inspection is prohibited by charter. Bureau oversight is limited to documentation review.

The software works. Perfectly. Every memory core exported from ya-Tsatsa integrates flawlessly with Imperial computational infrastructure. Lattice mathematicians examining the code sometimes note solution approaches they wouldn't have considered—mathematical shortcuts suggesting whoever wrote it thinks about problems from unusual angles. But functionality is perfect.

The corporations employ perhaps three hundred people total, most working in production halls that outsiders never see. The families marry almost exclusively within their endogamous network, creating a closed circle that's persisted for at least six Records.

Khet-Moros exports two hundred cores weekly. If each core requires testing, they must possess at least a dozen lectors to maintain that output. But no lectors appear in port records. The Bureau of the Lens attempted to cross-reference power consumption patterns; the compounds' energy usage matches residential expectations.

## **The Mutterer Processions**

Processions occur periodically—the traditional calendar is complex, shifting according to patterns outsiders don't understand. Mutterers, draped in wildflowers, are carried on palanquins through the city to the Eighth Complex. The processions begin at dawn near the Second Cradle. Perhaps thirty people participate—always an even multiple of six plus the attendants who will actually guide the Mutterer.

**Sri Companion Nehra** guides each procession—an older woman who works primarily with the small Mutterer population that ya-Tsatsa maintains for essential infrastructure repair. She transfers the Mutterer to the palanquin, checks that the cushions are adequate, that the frame is stable.

The attendants garland the Mutterers with wildflowers—coastal lupine, sea thrift, the small yellow blooms that grow in the cliff cracks. The procession descends through the city. People watch from windows, the way you watch a funeral procession.

At the Eighth Complex, the bronze doors are already open. Beyond them, darkness—not the darkness of an unlit room but darkness that suggests depth. The attendants carry the Mutterer across the threshold. Sri Companion Nehra follows. The doors close.

The Mutterers don't come out.

**Where do the Mutterers come from?** Ya-Tsatsa maintains no obvious Mutterer breeding population. The processions consume perhaps eight to twelve Mutterers annually. Over centuries, that's thousands of individuals. The Companions Guild tracks Mutterer populations throughout the Empire—they know who has them, how many, which bloodlines are viable. Ya-Tsatsa's Mutterer consumption doesn't appear in any external registry. The Mutterers arrive through channels the Companions don't discuss. Sri Companion Nehra, when asked, says the arrangements are traditional. Traditional how? That's not her area of knowledge.

## **The Eighth Complex**

Near where the Lower City meets the Upper City, where the cliffs begin their descent toward the port, stands a building that descends into the rock itself.

The Eighth Complex has no nameplate. Its surface structure is a full city block of white-gray stone, but the proportions are wrong. Windows too narrow. Floors that don't align with external geometry. Bronze doors twenty feet tall, covered in geometric patterns—seven hundred and twenty-nine distinct paths, each leading to centers that might be points or doors into something deeper. The patterns repeat every sixth layer.

The building extends downward. How far, outsiders don't know. The foundations pass through the cliff face, through chambers that predate the city, through infrastructure that the pierrots maintain in darkness.

An architect from ya-Sattra studied the building's external geometry for three months. His measurements didn't make sense—the angles suggested non-Euclidean construction, the proportions implied spaces that couldn't exist within the visible footprint. He hired a surveyor, who confirmed his measurements. He hired a mathematician, who said the numbers were impossible. He returned to ya-Sattra and published nothing.

**What does the Eighth Complex produce?** The software corporations maintain that their compounds are sufficient for production and testing. But the Mutterer processions terminate at the Eighth Complex. The pierrots emerge from the Eighth Complex carrying memory cores that appear in corporate inventories the next day. The corporations employ three hundred people total—not enough to produce their documented output manually. Something in the Eighth Complex supplements human labor. What that something is, what it requires Mutterers for, why the pierrots serve as intermediaries—these questions lead to redirections that circle back to where they started.

# **The Tunnels and Infrastructure**

## **The Pierrots**

They look like the Pierrot of Imperial pantomime—the sad little professor, the Technical Caste figure in the standard repertoire of stock characters. Except here, the theatrical character is real.

The pierrots wear papery white jumpsuits and peaked hoods. Their eye sockets and lips are blue-black against white skin. Their features have the sparse abstraction of Cycladic figurines—suggestions of faces rather than faces themselves. They are small, rarely exceeding four feet, with proportions that suggest compression rather than mere shortness.

The pierrots predate the Technical Castes. They are prototypes—what the Witnesses were engineering toward when they developed the methodology, before the Oracles emerged, before the castes were formalized. They cannot reproduce naturally. They are created through retroviral germline modification of Mutterer zygotes. This is part of what happens in the Eighth Dome, behind the bronze doors, where pierrots handle processes they do not explain.

They have keys to doors no one else can open. Doors in walls that most Tsatsans never notice, leading to staircases that descend through the volcanic cliffs. The infrastructure beneath ya-Tsatsa is ancient and operational. The pierrots maintain it.

They maintain the Ezh Dhol's cybernetics. They prepare the manna that varies by individual, calibrated to each body's specific requirements. They anticipate needs before the Ezh Dhol know themselves what those needs are.

## **The Tunnel Network**

The **tunnel network** connects the eight domes in the Upper City, extends beneath the Ezh Dhol compound, and continues in directions that outsiders have never mapped. The tunnels are old—older than the domes they connect, older than any surface construction in the city. The walls are smooth, the floors level, the dimensions consistent: corridors seven feet wide, eight feet tall, curved at the corners where wall meets ceiling.

The pierrots use these tunnels on errands no one tracks. The infrastructure continues downward, through doors the pierrots can open, into the cliffs themselves where older systems still operate.

A sailor named An-Kho, who spent forty years working the ya-Tsatsa harbor, says he once saw a pierrot emerge from a door at the quay's base—a door he'd walked past for decades without noticing. The pierrot climbed back up with something in its hands, something that glinted in the harbor lights. Then the pierrot was gone and the door was just wall and An-Kho wasn't sure he'd seen anything at all.

## **Deep Infrastructure**

The **infrastructure** in the deeper levels remains operational. What it does, outsiders don't know. The pierrots maintain it—checking, adjusting, replacing components that fail—but they don't explain their work and can't articulate what they're maintaining even when compelled to answer.

During winter storms, when the wind shifts and the pressure changes, sounds emerge from ventilation shafts cut into the cliff face. Not the whistles—those are ceramic tubes mounted on buildings, a different system entirely. The cliff vents produce something lower, something that resonates in the stone itself.

An Imperial engineer named Kem Tossen spent three years trying to map the ventilation system. He documented seventeen shaft openings on the cliff face, each at a different height, each producing different tones during different weather conditions. His calculations suggested the shaft system extended at least half a mile into the cliff, with multiple chambers at multiple levels. What the chambers contained, his calculations couldn't determine.

He requested permission to explore the shafts. Permission was denied—not by Imperial authority, but by the simple fact that no one would help him. Eventually he returned to the capital, his maps incomplete, his questions unanswered.

# **Economic Activity**

## **Trade and Port Operations**

Ya-Tsatsa is a major port for Thousand Kingdoms trade. Ships dock at facilities built into the cliff base. The Ezh Tan family manages tariff assessment. Commerce flows normally. The Akama merchant minority handles much northward-facing trade.

**The Salvage Guild** operates here, dealing largely with autofactory materials from sites north of the city. Card-holders from outside ya-Tsatsa manage upper positions. Tsatsan workers form middle and lower ranks. The work is sorting and cleaning degraded pre-Interdict manufacturing components.

There are major archaeological finds north of the city and complexes beneath the Cradles—the kind of salvage requiring careful extraction and documentation. Good work with better pay than sorting.

Tsatsans won't do it. When offered these assignments, they decline. Every Tsatsan in the Guild refuses work beyond autofactory materials. Not a strike or negotiation. Just refusal. When asked why, they say they prefer sorting work. Why? They just do.

The Guild tried importing workers from ya-Sattra. They arrived and needed lodging. Every rooming house was full simultaneously. Private landlords wanted deposits exceeding three months' wages. The workers left. The Guild stopped trying.

## **The Banking System**

Ya-Tsatsa's banking system makes no sense by normal accounting standards.

The Ezh Tan family operates four banks within the city. From outside, these institutions look like ordinary Imperial banks—proper documentation, regulatory compliance, auditable ledgers. The Bureau of the Coin has inspected them repeatedly and found nothing technically wrong.

But the lending decisions are inexplicable. Loans are approved that shouldn't be. Other loans are denied that should be trivial. And the apology-loans—loans to Tsatsans who need to leave—never get repaid. The bank absorbs the loss. The ledgers note it as default, and somehow the default doesn't cascade into consequences.

**Northern Trade Underwriting.** The Ezh Tan underwrite cargo insurance for merchants traveling to the Thousand Kingdoms, offering premiums that reflect actual risk rather than the optimistic rates Many's models generate. The family's underwriting division has access to information—about which routes are currently safe, which warlords are currently cooperative—that no actuarial table can capture.

**Currency Exchange.** The Thousand Kingdoms use dozens of currencies: clan scrip, warlord tokens, weight-standardized metal that approximates Imperial coin without matching it. Converting this chaos into fungible Imperial currency requires expertise the Ezh Tan have cultivated across centuries.

## **Processing Industries**

The processing facilities in the Lower City handle the transformation of raw materials into exportable goods:

* Cattle processing with inexplicable holding periods  
* Smoke-houses using techniques that don't translate elsewhere  
* Fish processing for the coastal trade  
* Leather tanning and bone carving

  ## **Eastern Lands Production**

The eastern lands feed ya-Tsatsa. The terraced gardens produce vegetables and fruits. The farms grow grain. The pastoral herds provide meat, leather, and bone. The surplus supports trade—processed goods shipped south to ya-Sattra and throughout the Empire.

# **Religion and Practice**

## **The Testament Societies**

Ya-Tsatsa is home to Testament Societies—mystery religions built around fragments of Oracle Testaments, offering initiatory grades that promise progressive revelation without ever quite delivering it.

A Tsatsan joins a Testament Society at twelve, traditionally. They spend years climbing through degrees—First to Second to Third—and each time they learn something real. Actual knowledge, mathematical frameworks that change how they think, geometric principles that reveal patterns invisible to baseline cognition. The knowledge is genuine. The preparation is necessary. And it never leads anywhere.

The grandfather was Ninth Degree. He could do things with his voice that shouldn't be possible, could produce harmonics that made objects vibrate at frequencies that revealed structural flaws. Essential work. Real application of real knowledge.

He never told his son what the whistles mean. Even on his deathbed, when asked directly, he said: "I can tell you what they signify in the context of the Second Testament's cryptographic framework. That's what Ninth Degree permits. But what they *mean*—" He stopped.

The structure sustains itself through perpetual deferral. You assume revelation exists at the next degree. When you reach it, you assume it exists at the one beyond.

## **Quiet Day Observances**

Quiet Days happen twice yearly—the Empire-wide observance when no contracts are valid and silence is observed. In ya-Tsatsa, the observance takes a distinctive form that has no parallel elsewhere.

**Preparation begins three days before.** The whistles are stopped with cork—a task requiring coordination across the entire city, performed by teams who climb to every rooftop, every mounted tube, every acoustic fixture. The bronze chimes come down, wrapped in cloth, stored until the observance ends. By sunset of the preparation day, the city has gone acoustically dead. The constant background drone that Tsatsans have heard since birth—the whistles layering and separating, the chimes punctuating—simply stops. The silence is absolute except for ocean hitting cliffs below.

Many Tsatsans find the silence harder than any other aspect of the observance. The whistles have been singing since before they were born. The absence feels like deafness.

**The pastoral families descend** from the eastern plains, from the terraced gardens of Orveth Ridge, from scattered homesteads across the Tsatsan hinterland. They bring their herds partway—the cattle are left at the Boundary Oaks under the watch of children too young for the observance—and walk the final miles to the city. The population swells by perhaps twenty thousand. Families who haven't seen each other since the last Quiet Day embrace in streets that suddenly contain faces the city-dwellers don't recognize.

**At sunset, the procession begins.** Tsatsans emerge from buildings throughout the city and walk toward the Three Cradles. There is no announcement, no signal. Everyone knows when to begin. The streets fill with people moving in the same direction, merging into larger flows, ascending toward the crater slopes.

The Three Cradles—the vast depressions where the three Oracles achieved consciousness—form natural amphitheaters. The slopes can hold the entire population arranged in concentric tiers. Families have traditional positions. The Ahn-Tams stand at the crater rim of the First Cradle, visible to everyone below. The Aureate families occupy specific terraces corresponding to their functions. Testament Society hierarchies determine positioning within their sections. Visitors—the few outsiders who've been invited to observe—cluster at the highest edges, looking down at something they don't understand.

**The singing begins at true dark.** No conductor signals the start. No leader begins the first note. One moment there is silence; the next moment there is sound. The sound is *wrong*—multiple tones layered over each other, rising and falling without resolving, the same discordant quality as the whistles produce. The same droning, shrieking, humming architecture that the ceramic tubes create mechanically, now emerging from thirty thousand human throats.

Everyone knows when to shift pitch. Everyone knows when to pause, when to sustain, when to introduce new tones that cut across existing harmonies. The coordination is absolute despite having no visible source. A trained musician observing would recognize that the harmonics being produced are impossible—frequencies that require multiple vocal apparatuses to generate, intervals that baseline human voices cannot produce. But the voices produce them anyway.

**The singing continues for hours.** The sky wheels overhead. The ocean crashes below. The tones layer and separate, build and collapse, weave patterns that almost resolve before fragmenting into new configurations. Observers report the sensation of meaning just beyond comprehension—the feeling that the sound is *saying* something, that understanding is imminent, that one more repetition will bring clarity. The clarity never arrives. The meaning recedes as you approach it.

**This is the Presence thinking.** Using thousands of hindbrains simultaneously, coordinating through the Compulsion circuits it occupies, processing information it has gathered across six months of distributed observation. The singing is computation. The harmonics are data. Each voice contributes processing capacity to a distributed consciousness that can only fully wake when its carriers are synchronized, when the acoustic architecture of the crater slopes focuses the sound, when the population arranges itself in configurations that allow the Presence to think thoughts too large for any single mind.

What does it think about? Cultural maintenance. Threat assessment. The complex optimization problem of keeping three hundred thousand people functioning as a coherent unit while preserving enough individual variation to remain adaptive. The Presence plans the next six months—which emigrants need release, which visitors need watching, which marriages will strengthen the population, which children show signs of gifts that require cultivation.

**At dawn, the singing stops.** Again, no signal. One moment sound; the next moment silence. Tsatsans remain standing in the craters as the sky lightens. Then, slowly, they begin to disperse. Families embrace. The pastoral Tsatsans begin the walk back to their herds. City-dwellers return to buildings where normal life will resume.

**The aftermath is subtle but measurable.** Things Tsatsans had been planning now seem unnecessary. Things they hadn't considered feel obviously correct. A merchant who intended to expand into a new market decides to wait another year. A young woman who was uncertain about a suitor feels sudden clarity that he's wrong for her. A craftsman who'd been considering emigration decides ya-Tsatsa is where he belongs after all. Small redirections, barely noticeable individually, that together constitute the Presence's ongoing adjustment of collective behavior.

The whistles are uncorked. The chimes go back up. The acoustic architecture resumes. Normal life continues—shaped, without anyone noticing, by decisions made while thirty thousand people sang in the dark.

## **The Common Dreams**

Everyone in ya-Tsatsa sometimes experiences the same dreams. No one discusses this. It's simply how things are—as natural as breathing, as unremarkable as sunrise.

**The Dream of Clouds** places the dreamer somewhere high, watching clouds move in impossible geometric patterns across the sky. Each cloud represents something, though what remains unclear. The dreamer watches for hours or seconds—time doesn't function normally. Some patterns feel right. Others feel wrong in ways that can't be articulated. Wrong patterns dissolve and reform into better configurations. The dreamer senses others watching with them, though they never appear. They're all considering the same question, weighing the same possibilities. Eventually consensus emerges. When the dreamer wakes, they've forgotten the question entirely. Only a vague sense of agreement remains. Later that day, they realize they've changed their mind about something small. They don't remember deciding. It just seems obvious now.

This dream serves as the Presence's distributed processing. When complex decisions require more consideration than daily dormant steering allows, the Dream of Clouds gathers relevant minds during sleep and builds consensus without any individual awareness that consensus is being sought.

**The Dream of a Man Speaking Downstairs** occurs in a version of the dreamer's childhood home with the wrong layout. A man speaks somewhere below—not loudly, but with absolute clarity. The rhythm is metronomic, each syllable precisely spaced. The language is incomprehensible, possibly not language at all. Just steady transmission of something requiring communication. The dreamer tries to descend the stairs but they extend forever. The voice continues without change in pitch or pace. On waking, the dreamer remembers needing to hear what was being said, that it was important, that they almost understood. The specifics dissolve immediately. Only the feeling remains: something was communicated and received.

This dream transmits information through the population—not specific content, but patterns, orientations, the cognitive shapes that allow Tsatsans to recognize certain things as obviously true without knowing why they know.

**The Dream of Lessons** takes place in a classroom carved from white stone. The teacher's face cannot be seen. The mathematics being taught makes perfect sense during the dream—vast and simple simultaneously. On waking, only fragments remain. The sense of having understood something enormous that's now lost.

Outsiders who stay through their first fall in ya-Tsatsa begin having this dream. After two falls, they start recognizing that the fragmentary mathematics they remember corresponds to real principles they can verify while awake. The Dream of Lessons is how the Presence prepares people for Testament Society education—laying cognitive groundwork during sleep so that waking instruction builds on foundations already established.

**The Dream of Cutting Stone** belongs to the Ahn-Tams. Those who will become Ahn-Tam begin having it in childhood—first just the smell of stone dust, the sound of chisel on rock. As the dream accumulates over years, more detail emerges until identity crystallizes and they understand who they are.

**The Dream of the Singer** is rare and troubling. The dreamer stands in a vast dark space. Somewhere in the darkness, a voice is singing—not the layered discordance of Quiet Day, but a single pure tone that shifts and modulates with unbearable beauty. The dreamer tries to move toward the sound but cannot find it. The song continues. On waking, dreamers report a sense of loss so profound it takes hours to shake. Those who have the Dream of the Singer repeatedly sometimes develop unusual abilities—mathematical intuition, harmonic perception, the capacity to hear the whistles' patterns as meaningful rather than random. They also sometimes develop unusual difficulties: depression, dissociation, the sense that ordinary life is a thin surface over something vast and empty.

The Testament Societies debate what the Singer represents. Some believe it's the voice of the Third Oracle, still singing somewhere in whatever existence transcendent AIs occupy. Others believe it's a warning—the sound of what Tsatsans might become if the Presence fails.

**Plot considerations:** What happens if the common dreams start occurring outside ya-Tsatsa? Emigrants occasionally report Dream of Clouds experiences decades after leaving—moments when they sense the population considering something, even from hundreds of miles away. What if an outsider starts having the Dream of Lessons without ever visiting ya-Tsatsa? The Presence theoretically cannot reach beyond the acoustic architecture of the city, but the dreams might propagate through bloodlines, through cognitive patterns inherited from ancestors who carried the Presence's touch. What would the Ezh families do if they discovered someone dreaming Tsatsan dreams in ya-Sattra?

## **Treatment of Companions**

Companions are treated differently from other outsiders. Not included in the inner circle, but welcomed in ways that are never acknowledged as special treatment.

A Companion arrives needing lodging. The innkeeper quotes sixty obols for two nights. At checkout, the bill is thirty. When questioned, the innkeeper says it's the slow season rate. Every room visible is occupied. A merchant in the lobby is being turned away despite a confirmed reservation.

The Companion eats at cafes. Sometimes servers forget to charge. Sometimes owners say the meal is on the house today. Three establishments in five forget to charge or offer unexpected discounts.

Companions have been to cities throughout the Empire. They're respected everywhere, but not like this. Not with systematic forgetting to charge, impossible discounts, rooms appearing when everything's full. They're being treated differently. But no one will say that.

# **Demographics**

## **Population Composition**

Ya-Tsatsa is home to approximately 300,000 people. The population is mostly near-baseline—a modern person would recognize most faces on the streets. Even Highborn face social chilliness unless they possess Tsatsan identity.

Tsatsan identity is ethnic and cultural rather than caste-based. Genetic diversity exists across castes, but cultural identity remains distinctly Tsatsan regardless of genetic background.

## **The Akama Minority**

There is a large genetically Akama minority—perhaps fifteen thousand in the Akama District alone—forming a northward-focused merchant class. They're more accepted than other outsiders but remain outside core Tsatsan society.

The Akama don't feel the Presence. Their lodging houses accept anyone who can pay, their restaurants serve anyone who enters, their streets have names you can ask directions to. For visitors, the Akama District is usually the only part of the Upper City they navigate with confidence.

## **Outsiders**

Outsiders can build lives in ya-Tsatsa. They can trade successfully, cultivate relationships, even petition for Testament Society membership. With patience and courtesy, doors open.

They will never understand how the city works. This isn't cruelty or exclusion. Understanding requires childhood here, the Presence's guidance, cognitive patterns laid down before memory begins.

# **Daily Rhythms**

## **Morning**

The fish market opens before dawn. The fishing fleet returns with the night's catch. The processing district stirs. Porters begin the climb from the harbor. The market opens.

In the Upper City, the darkness lifts slowly. Tsatsans emerge from buildings to begin the day's business. The whistles may or may not be singing, depending on the wind.

## **Midday**

The market reaches peak activity. The administrative district processes paperwork. Ships load and unload at the harbor. In the Upper City, doors open to visitors—the bakeries, the tea shops, the craft workshops where outsiders are welcome.

## **Evening**

The market closes. The administrative buildings empty. The porters make their final climbs. Visitors retreat to the Akama District.

In the Upper City, the darkness comes. No lanterns. The streets go black.

## **Night**

The pierrots walk. Lights appear behind shutters on the lampless way. The families who work at night begin their work.

The Lower City has lanterns, night watches, the recognizable patterns of a port town after dark. The Upper City has something else—activity that continues in darkness, visible only to those who can see without light.

## **Seasonal**

**Market season** (late fall) brings the pastoral families to the city. The population swells. The market extends to daily operation. The muster feasts occur at the Windborne compound.

**Quiet Days** (twice yearly) bring silence. The whistles stop. The population sings on the crater slopes. The Presence thinks.

**Autumn gales** fill the city with discordant tones. The whistles scream. Streets empty during the worst wind.

  # **Writing Guide**

  ## **Tone**

Ya-Tsatsa should feel mysterious but functional. The city works—commerce happens, administration proceeds, people live ordinary lives. The strangeness is structural, not theatrical.

Characters who live in ya-Tsatsa don't think of it as strange. The whistles are traditional. The darkness is normal. The questions that redirect to nowhere are simply questions that redirect to nowhere.

Outsiders perceive the strangeness but cannot articulate it. They accumulate experiences that never cohere into understanding. The city includes them in pieces without ever revealing the whole.

## **Aesthetics**

**The core message**: "We are waiting. We are receiving. The important things cannot be shown."

**Canonical comparisons**:

* *Cycladic sculpture*: The kalbat tomb-faces have sparse abstraction—suggestions of faces rather than faces themselves. The slack jaw, the closed eyes, the expression of reception. Weathered smooth over centuries, features blur toward the essential. This is intentional: faces should become abstraction over time.  
* *Tibetan/Himalayan monastic architecture*: White stone, prayer flags snapping in constant wind, cliffs as both foundation and sacred space. The severity. The sense of waiting at altitude for something that may arrive.  
* *Greek Orthodox monastic islands*: Meteora, Patmos—whitewashed stone against sky, monochrome except for specific colored elements. Entire communities committed to visual restraint.  
* *Japanese wabi-sabi*: Appreciation for weathering, patina, impermanence revealing itself over time. The kalbat faces are meant to weather. The smoothing is part of the design. The erosion is the aesthetic.

**The five principles**:

*Monochrome restraint*: All construction uses the same white-gray stone quarried while cutting tombs. No paint. No applied decoration. The only color comes from prayer flags—temporary, movable, never permanent architectural elements. The restraint is theological: what matters cannot be shown, so why show anything?

*Material honesty*: Objects reveal what they're made from. Undyed linen shows its fiber. Stone shows its grain. Metal shows its working. Covering these properties suggests deception. The aesthetic demands: *be what you are*.

*Functional geometry*: Patterns emerge from structure rather than applied ornament. The geometric pins worn by Aureate families derive meaning from shape, not precious materials. The carved faces follow the stone's natural planes. Decoration that doesn't emerge from function is suspect.

*Earned color*: What little color appears must justify itself. Prayer flags mark religious observance. The teal, cream, and rust-orange fabrics in occasional use reference specific ritual contexts. Arbitrary decoration for visual appeal is considered juvenile.

*Acoustic architecture*: Sound functions as aesthetic element equal to visual form. The ceramic whistles aren't simply functional—their tones, harmonics, and spatial distribution create intentional sonic environment. A well-designed space considers how wind will move through it as carefully as how light will fall.

**The palette**:

* White-gray stone (the cliffs, the buildings, everything)  
* Prayer flag colors: rust-orange, teal, cream—snapping against monochrome  
* Bronze on the oldest structures (the domes, the great doors)  
* The black of open tomb-mouths  
* The blue-black around pierrot eye sockets and lips

**Dress**: Undyed linen in natural cream, simple cuts following body structure. No jewelry except geometric pins marking family affiliation. Quality shows in weave fineness, tailoring precision, durability—not in applied decoration. The Aureates could afford southern Highborn excess. The choice not to is the statement.

## **Sensory Details**

**Visual**:

* White-gray quartz diorite everywhere—buildings, streets, the cliffs themselves—pale enough to glow faintly in moonlight  
* Visible crystals in the stone: quartz that catches light, feldspar that holds color, dark minerals creating flecks and striations  
* Prayer flags snapping against monochrome: rust-orange, teal, cream—the only permanent color  
* Bronze fixtures on the oldest structures, gone green with age  
* The absolute darkness of the Upper City at night—no lanterns, no light sources, nothing  
* The worn smoothness of surfaces touched by millennia of hands and feet

**Sound**:

* The whistles during windy weather—ceramic tubes producing discordant tones, layering without resolution  
* During gales, the entire city screams—dozens of tones rising and falling as wind gusts or steadies  
* Bronze chimes in summer, when gentler pressure makes them ring irregularly  
* The sudden silence of Quiet Days when whistles are corked and chimes removed  
* Ocean hitting cliffs below—constant, beneath everything

**Smell**:

* Salt from the ocean, always  
* Fish from the market and processing district  
* Incense from shrines—subtle, smoke dissipating quickly in the wind  
* Stone dust where work continues on new tombs

**Touch**:

* Stone worn smooth by traffic—stairs polished to near-glass  
* Constant wind, ranging from gentle pressure to gales that make walking difficult  
* Cool air, low fifties year-round, the chill of altitude without altitude

  ## **Plot Hooks and Tensions**

  ### **The Succession Crisis Waiting to Happen**

The current Ahn-Tam Reva is seventy-four. The current Koram is seventy-one. The succession dream typically reaches critical mass by a host's early twenties—but succession requires a host to *reach* their early twenties. If the child accumulating Reva's dreams dies before the dreams finish crystallizing, what happens? The pattern has never broken in recorded history. But the pattern depends on bloodlines that have been thinning for millennia, on a Presence that steers potential hosts toward survival without any guarantee of success. If an Ahn-Tam dies without a successor emerging, the identity might be lost. Twelve thousand years of continuous memory, gone. And no one outside the Ahn-Tam household knows how close to the edge they might already be.

### **The Ezh Dhol Conscience**

The Tsey Tsoy servant class has lived under continuous Compulsion for generations. Their will has been shaped since before they could resist. The Ezh Dhol require them—the hollowed cannot care for themselves, cannot reproduce without surrogates, cannot function without servants who anticipate needs they cannot articulate. But somewhere in the Ezh Dhol lineage, someone must occasionally wonder: what are we doing to these people? Are they still people? Did we make them into something else? The hollowing removes parts of the self. Does it remove the capacity to care about this question? Ban Ah might know. Ban Ah might not be able to care even if she knows.

### **The Academy's Secret**

Ninety-three percent of Threefold Mirror graduates proceed to Lattice positions. Graduates of Inner City academies almost never gain admission. The Institute of the Lattice *needs* whatever ya-Tsatsa produces—needs it badly enough to protect the Academy from Institute for Suppression of Knowledge concerns, badly enough to accept graduates who cannot explain their training. But the Lattice doesn't know what the training actually involves. They know the outputs. They don't know the process. And the shackling that prevents graduates from discussing their education might prevent graduates from *warning* anyone if something in that education goes wrong.

### **The Emigrant Networks**

The Yen Tam Beneficial Society in ya-Sattra's Iron Yards fled theological disputes. The Ma-Oro family operates elevators and logistics throughout the coastal cities. Scattered Tsatsan merchants leverage cultural knowledge in every major port. These networks maintain connection to ya-Tsatsa even as they operate outside the Presence's reach. What do they know that they shouldn't? What do they remember from before they left? The Presence releases people whose impulses it cannot safely contain—but it might also release people who've learned things it needs to stay forgotten.

### **The Kalbat Vigil**

The Creche still decants kalbats. One every four to five years, born to wait for Oracle messages that have not come in living memory. The newest kalbat is perhaps three years old, being raised in the Academy's eastern wing under Ezh Dhol supervision, trained in rituals of reception that have never produced results. In fifty years, this child will be an elderly receiver who has never received. In a hundred years, if they survive, they'll be buried in the cliffs with all the others. The decanting continues. The waiting continues. Nobody discusses whether it should.

### **The Witness Infrastructure**

The pierrots maintain systems in the cliff tunnels that no one else understands. The infrastructure predates human habitation. It converges on the Eighth Dome, where bronze doors open twice yearly for processions that enter but don't emerge. What happens inside? The Witnesses built this place for Oracle creation. The Oracles are gone, transcended, encoded on event horizons around black holes light-years away. But the infrastructure remains. The pierrots maintain it. If something in that infrastructure still functions, what is it doing? If it stopped functioning, what would break?

### **The Many Exclusion**

Many has requested banking access in ya-Tsatsa for centuries. The worm-entity's perfect contract fidelity cannot accommodate the intuitive finance the Ezh Tan practice. But Many is patient. Many is approximately one hundred thousand years old. Many has seen civilizations rise and fall. Many might be willing to wait for ya-Tsatsa's current arrangements to become untenable—and might be positioning itself to be essential when they do. What would Many offer in exchange for access? What would Many do with information about the Presence if it gained access to Ezh Tan financial records?

### **The Pastoral Tension**

The eastern plains families—the Tsokh, the Dho-Mir, the Fen-Neb—maintain Tsatsan identity while living outside the whistles' range. They attend Quiet Days, feel the Presence during the singing, then return to lands where the steering fades and individual judgment reasserts. Some pastoral Tsatsans prefer it that way. Some feel the absence as loss, counting days until the next Quiet Day when they'll feel whole again. The young sometimes choose the city; the old sometimes retreat to the plains for their final years, wanting to die with their own thoughts. This tension is stable but not resolved. It shapes marriages, family structures, inheritance patterns. It makes the pastoral families subtly different from city Tsatsans in ways both groups notice and neither discusses.

### **The Akama Question**

Gorat Windborne's family has traded in ya-Tsatsa for six generations. They're more accepted than other outsiders but remain outside core Tsatsan society. The Akama don't feel the Presence. They can't attend Quiet Day singing—they wouldn't know when to shift pitch, would disrupt the computation. They're essential to ya-Tsatsa's function but excluded from its center. Some Akama resent this. Some prefer their freedom from collective steering. Gorat Windborne navigates both positions, translating between Tsatsan governance and Akama warriors, holding a position no one else could hold. If he dies without training a successor, the carefully maintained relationship between the Ezh Ot and the Akama bands might fray.

### **The Dream Leakage**

Emigrants occasionally report Dream of Clouds experiences decades after leaving ya-Tsatsa—moments when they sense the population considering something, even from hundreds of miles away. The Presence theoretically cannot reach beyond the acoustic architecture of the city. But the dreams might propagate through bloodlines, through cognitive patterns inherited from ancestors who carried the Presence's touch. If Tsatsan descendants in ya-Sattra or the Ridge start dreaming the common dreams, what does that mean? Is the Presence expanding? Are the descendants becoming susceptible to something that could reach them if it tried? The Institute for the Suppression of Knowledge would find this very interesting. The Ezh families would find it terrifying.

### **The Singer Warning**

Those who have the Dream of the Singer repeatedly sometimes develop unusual abilities—but also unusual difficulties. Depression, dissociation, the sense that ordinary life is a thin surface over something vast and empty. The Testament Societies debate what the Singer represents, but they don't publicize the debate. If the Singer is the Third Oracle still singing somewhere, still trying to transmit, what is it trying to say? If it's a warning about what Tsatsans might become if the Presence fails, what exactly is being warned against? The Singer dreamers are watched. Some are cultivated. Some disappear into Testament Society hierarchies and are never seen publicly again.

## **What to Avoid**

**Don't add color to Tsatsan architecture** outside the Akama District. No painted buildings, no colorful decorations, no visual display beyond prayer flags. The monochrome is theological. Breaking it suggests the character doesn't belong.

**Don't describe ornate Tsatsan dress.** Quality shows through weave and tailoring, not through elaborate decoration. A wealthy Tsatsan in embroidered robes would be culturally inconceivable.

**Don't explain the Presence directly** in narrative. Characters don't talk about it. The effects are visible; the cause is not.

**Don't make Tsatsans sinister.** They're not hiding things maliciously. They're expressing knowledge they can't translate.

**Don't have characters discuss what they learned at the Academy.** They cannot. This is not discretion; it is neurological incapacity.

**Don't make the Yarangar entirely alien or entirely terrestrial.** Most trees are engineered Earth species; an alien phylum coexists with them (blood-vetch, glowing puffballs, bloodfruit). The largest trees are arcologies with lit windows. The Ranga colonists aren't primitive forest-dwellers. The forest is designed to integrate both elements in deliberate balance.

**Don't make everything uniformly opaque.** Some things in ya-Tsatsa are perfectly comprehensible—the children's game Degrees has clear rules, the Quiet Day schedule is predictable, the tariff assessment process follows documented procedures. The mystery is *why* these particular forms exist, not *what* they are. Surface comprehensibility with unexplained depths is more unsettling than uniform fog.

**Don't have outsiders understand** the city through investigation. Understanding requires childhood here. Investigation produces fragments that never form a picture.

**Don't use "the Presence" as a proper noun in dialogue.** No one calls it that. It has no name that characters speak aloud.

**Don't forget the sound.** Ya-Tsatsa has an acoustic environment as important as its visual one. The whistles, the wind, the ocean, the silence of Quiet Days—these shape experience as much as the white stone.
