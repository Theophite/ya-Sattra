# Imperial Vernacular Architecture: Regional Variation

## Three Architectural Traditions

The Empire contains three distinct building traditions. Ya-Don and ya-Sattra share a common vernacular adapted to different climates. Ya-Tsatsa stands apart—its architecture emerges from religious practice and geology rather than the vernacular tradition.

---

## Ya-Don: The Shade Tradition

Central Valley heat shapes everything. Summer temperatures exceed 110°F for weeks. Shadows become precious resources.

**Core elements:**
- Thick stone walls (two feet or more) in local tan and grey
- Small windows, deep-set, positioned for ventilation without direct sun
- Curved arches: doorways, windows, passages—organic forms absorbed from Furnace proximity
- Flat roofs with reflective surfaces, OR domed roofs echoing Furnace geometry
- Courtyard organization: families face inward where shade accumulates
- Buildings present blank faces to streets, enclosing protected interior spaces
- **Furnace pipes threading through everything**: thesad and conventional bronze, running along walls, crossing streets, diving underground

**The Pipe Networks**: Ya-Don is threaded with Furnace infrastructure. Pipes six inches to twenty feet in diameter extend beyond the Furnaces into every district. Some are thesad—bronze-colored, self-repairing, pristine after millennia. Others are conventional bronze and copper, oxidized to verdigris, patched where they've failed. The two kinds run side by side. Equipment housings (valve assemblies, junction boxes, pressure vessels) squat at intersections. Buildings were constructed AROUND the pipes; the infrastructure came first. Residents have learned which pipes are hot, which carry vibration, which sometimes leak.

**Materials:** Local stone (tan, grey, ochre from foothills), adobe, salvaged composite panels, bronze threshold fittings, timber framing, metal roofing on domed structures. Furnace pipes: thesad (pristine) and conventional (oxidized, patched).

**Colors:** Cream, tan, ochre, rust/copper. Teal accents where power permits (Imperial color in technology and formal spaces). Thesad pipes (pristine bronze), conventional pipes (verdigris and rust).

**Climate response:** Passive cooling through thermal mass. The walls absorb heat during the day, release it at night. Courtyards create shade wells. The architecture works WITH brutal temperatures rather than against them.

### Ancient Buildings in Ya-Don

Ya-Don has pre-Interdict structures too—but they're DIFFERENT from ya-Sattra's. Not Art Deco bronze geometry, but industrial ceramic sublime.

**The Furnaces** (~2 square miles): Massive domes ranging fifty to two hundred feet in diameter. The curves follow geometries that baseline mathematics cannot easily describe—bulging asymmetrically, surfaces interrupted by ports and vents. The material is ceramic or fused stone: seamless, jointless, colored in shades of grey and cream that have weathered into patterns resembling geological strata. Obelisk towers taper to glowing amber tops three hundred feet up. Pipe networks extend from the domes into the surrounding city—thesad (self-repairing, pristine) running alongside conventional bronze (oxidized, patched). Equipment housings emerge everywhere: valve assemblies, junction boxes, monitoring stations.

**Rail Terminus foundations**: Ancient structure, original function unknown. Pre-Interdict foundations under conventional platforms—the basic infrastructure remains ancient, incomprehensible, apparently eternal.

**The Verethani Compound** (~500 residents): Buildings "contemporaneous with the Furnaces"—unusual proportions, acoustic features baseline construction lacks, materials whose composition resists analysis. Could pass for ordinary worker housing until close inspection reveals something older.

**The Verethani Chapel**: Acoustic properties "designed—by whom, and when, no one knows—for these specific resonances." Pre-Interdict construction anticipating Verethani voices.

**Hermitage of St. Caris the Still** (4 hours from city): Pre-Interdict domed structure of pale concrete with "massive circular void where a window would be expected." Pilgrims seek its particular silence.

**What makes ya-Don's Antediluvian aesthetic different from ya-Sattra's:**

Ya-Sattra's pre-Interdict buildings are Art Deco/Cycladic/Byzantine: bronze-colored thesad, geometric patterns, "circuit board" surface decoration, designed to communicate. The arcologies are monuments that speak.

Ya-Don's pre-Interdict buildings are industrial ceramic: cream and grey fused stone, asymmetrical curves, seamless jointless surfaces, NO decoration. The Furnaces weren't built to express truth or address audiences—they were built to produce. The domes house production. The towers exhaust heat. The pipes carry materials. There are no statues, no carved reliefs, no geometric patterns encoding meaning.

**How this shapes ya-Don's vernacular:**

The Furnaces ARE beautiful—industrial sublime, not decorative, but sublime nonetheless. And the vernacular absorbed their forms more than first appears:

**Dome forms absorbed into vernacular**: Farm storage buildings, granaries, some housing—all show domed roofs echoing Furnace geometry. Not the seamless fused ceramic of the Antediluvian originals, but metal-roofed domes, adobe domes, conventional construction shaped by millennia of living near the great domes. The St. Brennam's Garden farms show this clearly: domed storage buildings with cylindrical silos, low stone walls, the agricultural vernacular echoing industrial forms.

**Curved arches as dominant feature**: Where ya-Sattra's vernacular absorbed geometric patterns, ya-Don's absorbed curves. Arched doorways, arched windows, vaulted passages. Not rectangular but organic. The curves say "we live near the Furnaces" even when the building is conventional stone and adobe.

**The aspirational register has TWO modes**:

1. *Smooth and domed*: Cream/grey colors, curved forms, plaster hiding joints—directly echoing Furnace ceramic. Guild halls, meeting rooms, spaces wanting to feel industrial-sacred.

2. *Vertical and crystalline*: Gothic-meets-Art-Deco verticality, geometric spires, grey stone rising in angular formations. Churches attempting architectural beauty. The Cathedral of St. Akhmet. St. Calis in Ember Row "attempts architectural beauty with mixed results—a spire whose proportions are slightly wrong." The aspiration reaches UPWARD, toward the obelisk towers, not just outward toward the domes.

**Interior decoration**: Patchwork textiles covering walls. Salvaged Art Deco elements (stools, fixtures) from sources nobody remembers. Geometric patterns that echo Antediluvian forms but rendered in conventional materials. Teal accent lighting where power permits—the Imperial color appearing in technology and formal spaces. The Burning Rest tavern: "Art deco stools salvaged from pre-Interdict sources, patchwork Penitent-guild wall hangings."

**The es-Etaie Residence: Deliberate Modesty**

The es-Etaie family chose the opposite of aspiration. Their residence is "deliberately modest by Aureate standards"—a three-story stone building that would not be out of place in the Threshold. The contrast with es-Kiral ostentation is intentional. This represents a register of REFUSING aesthetic display.

Ya-Don's vernacular exists across a spectrum: the domed agricultural echoes, the curved arches of everyday construction, the patchwork interiors of accumulated salvage, the vertical aspirations of religious architecture, and the deliberate modesty of those who choose not to display.

### Registers

**Cross-cutting element: Furnace pipe networks** run through ALL registers except the most distant (St. Brennam's Garden farms see them only at the edges). Density decreases with distance from Furnaces. Threshold: dense, buildings shaped around pipes. Ember Row: sparser, more patched, domesticated. Thornback Ridge: opportunistic connections. Underground: pipes often run through Antediluvian tunnels.

- *Foundational* (Threshold): Massive stone, curved arches, buildings shaped around pipe infrastructure, oldest construction
- *Adaptive* (Ember Row): Lighter materials, pipes integrated into patchwork, infrastructure domesticated
- *Agricultural* (St. Brennam's Garden): Domed storage buildings, cylindrical silos, low stone walls—Furnace geometry absorbed, pipes minimal
- *Overflow* (Thornback Ridge): Where the shade tradition breaks down—improvised awnings, opportunistic pipe connections
- *Industrial poverty* (Sifting Grounds): Furnace waste processing, contaminated ground, salvage construction
- *Caste-adapted* (St. Oreli's Shade): Extended overhangs, muted colors, dim lighting for Orevet vision
- *Miniaturized* (Quick Lanes): Ya-Don patterns at Astal scale, painted aphorism lintels
- *Ancient industrial* (Verethani Compound, Rail Terminus): Pre-Interdict cream-and-grey ceramic, unusual proportions
- *Aspirational-domed* (guild halls, some churches): Cream/grey colors, curved forms, smooth plaster—echoing Furnace ceramic
- *Aspirational-vertical* (Cathedral of St. Akhmet, St. Calis): Gothic-meets-Art-Deco crystalline spires, geometric verticality reaching toward obelisk towers
- *Interior-salvage* (taverns, guild rooms): Patchwork textiles, salvaged Art Deco elements, teal accent lighting
- *Deliberate modesty* (es-Etaie Residence): Refusing aesthetic display, blending with worker housing by choice

### Building in the Ancients

Ya-Sattra's vernacular builds ON and BETWEEN Antediluvian structures. Ya-Don's vernacular moved INTO them. The Antediluvians carved infrastructure beneath ya-Don—ventilation shafts, conduit routes, equipment chambers, maintenance crawlways. None of it was meant for habitation. People moved in anyway.

- *Depths of St. Yerren* (~50,000): Karst-majority district in carved tunnels. Chambers converted from equipment housings. Platforms laid across uneven floors. Doorways cut through walls that were never meant to have doorways. The stone cuts remain precise after millennia; human additions wear and crumble around them.

- *Warren-Under-Stone* (~20,000): Ductworker quarter in spaces too small for anyone else. Four-foot ceilings. Compression passages. Junction boxes converted to sleeping alcoves. The Warren Tavern has a ceiling so low baseline visitors cannot enter upright.

**Comparisons:**

*Foundational/Threshold*: Persian courtyard houses (Yazd, Isfahan)—thick adobe walls, small windows, courtyards as shade wells, blank faces to streets. North African medinas (Fez, Marrakech). Pueblo architecture. But with curved arches throughout—more Ottoman/Byzantine influence in the openings.

*Agricultural/St. Brennam's Garden*: The domed farm buildings echo Mediterranean grain storage (trulli of Puglia, North African granaries), but the dome form comes from Furnace proximity, not separate tradition. Soviet collective farm architecture if built near nuclear plants that happened to be beautiful.

*Adaptive/Ember Row*: Accumulated modification resembles formalized favela patterns—additions upon additions, but accepted practice rather than improvisation.

*Overflow/Thornback Ridge*: Where coordinated shade architecture fails—improvised solutions, canvas awnings, streets too wide for proper shade canyons.

*Industrial poverty/Sifting Grounds*: Waste processing settlements. Living on contaminated ground because land is worthless.

*Underground*: Infrastructure tunnels converted to housing. The Depths and Warren occupy spaces carved for purposes nobody remembers.

*Ancient industrial*: Pre-Interdict worker housing—materials resisting analysis, continuous occupation across millennia.

*Aspirational-domed*: Islamic architecture's domed forms (Persian mosques, Ottoman baths), but referencing industrial rather than sacred sources. The Furnace domes provide the template.

*Aspirational-vertical*: Gothic cathedral meets Art Deco skyscraper. The Liverpool Metropolitan Cathedral, Gaudi's Sagrada Familia, Chrysler Building crown—verticality as aspiration. Ya-Don's churches reach toward the obelisk towers.

*Interior-salvage*: The Burning Rest tavern—patchwork textiles, salvaged Art Deco stools. Like Berlin bars in repurposed industrial spaces, accumulating decoration from salvage. Teal accent lighting where Imperial technology provides power.

*Deliberate modesty*: Quaker plainness, Shaker simplicity—refusing display as theological statement.

## Ya-Sattra: The Coastal Tradition

Mediterranean climate. Mild temperatures (65-70°F most of the year). Fog from the basin. The architecture doesn't fight heat—it manages moisture and wind.

**But also**: 50,000 years of building next to Art Deco/Cycladic/Byzantine monuments. The vernacular absorbed their aesthetic.

**Core elements (Rim vernacular):**
- Regional stone and timber construction
- Terracotta roof tiles (pitched, not flat—rain happens here)
- Terraced streets following hillside contours
- Larger windows than ya-Don (less heat to exclude)
- Views as architectural feature (the Rim looks DOWN at the arcologies)

**Antediluvian influence on conventional construction:**

People don't just build AROUND the arcologies—they've been unconsciously quoting them for millennia. The vernacular absorbed Antediluvian aesthetic pressure. The intensity varies by district:

**ANTEDILUVIAN QUARTER** (highest aspiration): 48,000 people, only 3,200 Aureates. The 32,000 hereditary servants and 12,000 commercial workers need housing. Conventional construction fills spaces BETWEEN the arcologies—and it's the most refined Antediluvian-influenced vernacular in the Empire:
- Geometric border patterns on EVERY formal entrance—elaborate "circuit board" motifs
- Smooth stucco finishes attempting seamless thesad—failing, but the attempt visible everywhere
- Bronze EVERYWHERE: threshold fittings, door hardware, railings, window frames, decorative panels
- Streamlined proportions echoing arcology geometry
- Shadowless interiors: multiple windows, light wells, reflective surfaces
- Servant quarters here are more architecturally ambitious than guild halls elsewhere

**MEDINA QUARTER** (commercial aspiration): 300,000 people in the commercial gateway. Three layers visible everywhere:
- ANCIENT: Three surviving arcologies, Processional Tower, Stewards' Block, Lens-Grinders' Courtyard
- INTERSTITIAL: Stone/brick/timber buildings (3-5 stories) built BETWEEN ancient structures, never touching them. This layer absorbed aesthetic pressure:
  - Geometric border patterns on guild halls, churches, high-status buildings
  - Smooth stucco attempting thesad seamlessness
  - Bronze fittings as aspirational material
  - Streamlined proportions echoing arcology geometry
- ACCUMULATED: Eight thousand years of wear—stairs worn into curves, handrails polished to mirrors

The conventional construction WANTS to be Antediluvian. It fails, but the attempt shapes everything.

**THE APPROACH / "The Fifth"** (bronze buildings): Pre-Interdict bronze structures, 2-4 stories—not arcologies but ancient nonetheless. Power conduits from Fourth Whorl. The aesthetic here IS Antediluvian, not aspiring toward it.

**OLD CUSTOMS** (boundary vernacular): Where Medina's aspiration fades toward the Iron Yards. Grey granite, timber, salvage repairs. Less geometric decoration, fewer bronze fittings. The Antediluvian influence weakens at the district's edges.

**Materials:** Regional stone (tan and grey, weathered naturally), local timber, terracotta, stucco (smooth finishes preferred), bronze fittings (aspirational). Geometric relief patterns. Porcelain-coral streets (in Antediluvian Quarter and the Processional).

**Climate response:** Different problem than ya-Don. The coast is mild but damp. Buildings manage moisture rather than heat. Terracing manages slope drainage. The cliff-edge winds that channel down into the basin require windbreaks.

### Ya-Sattra Registers

Aspiration intensity varies by district—highest near arcologies, fading toward industrial edges:

- *Highest aspiration* (Antediluvian Quarter conventional construction): Geometric border patterns on EVERY formal entrance. Smooth stucco attempting thesad seamlessness. Bronze EVERYWHERE. Servant quarters more ambitious than guild halls elsewhere.
- *Commercial aspiration* (Medina Quarter foundation-top): Guild halls with geometric borders. Smooth stucco on high-status buildings. Bronze fittings aspirational. Buildings WANT to be Antediluvian.
- *Street-canyon practical* (Great Bazaar canyon-level): Temporary timber, salvage. Aspiration abandoned for flexibility. Legitimacy traded for possibility.
- *Boundary vernacular* (Old Customs): Aspiration fading. Simpler decoration, exposed stone, fewer bronze fittings. Iron Yards aesthetic bleeding across boundary.
- *Actually Antediluvian* (The Approach / "The Fifth"): Bronze pre-Interdict buildings—not aspiring, IS ancient. Conventional infill between them shows highest aspiration.
- *Carved-space* (Foundation Market, Bazaar depths): Commerce INTO ancient substrate. Similar to ya-Don underground—colonizing spaces never meant for habitation.
- *Rotten Quarter* (Middens, Iron Yards, Ridge): Survival architecture. Aspiration irrelevant. Salvage construction, improvisation, making do.

**Comparisons:**

The dual register is like Byzantine architecture in Constantinople—practical Roman engineering techniques overlaid with Eastern decorative vocabulary. Or Art Deco apartment buildings in Manhattan—modern steel construction clothed in geometric ornament derived from ancient sources. The underlying structure is practical; the surface vocabulary aspires toward something grander.

The key difference from ya-Don: ya-Sattra's vernacular evolved under AESTHETIC pressure from beautiful monuments. Ya-Don's vernacular evolved under PRACTICAL pressure from industrial infrastructure.

### Building on the Ancients

Ya-Don's vernacular moved INTO Antediluvian infrastructure—tunnels and crawlways never meant for habitation. Ya-Sattra's vernacular had to build ON and BETWEEN Antediluvian structures never meant for conventional construction. The foundation platforms are permanent features. The arcology columns are permanent features. The subsidence bowls are permanent features. Construction works around them because nothing human-made can move, modify, or penetrate them.

**Foundation-top construction**: Buildings sitting ON 3-5 meter high arcology foundation platforms. Mediterranean vernacular—stone, timber, terracotta—but constrained by foundation edges. Can't expand beyond the platform. Can't dig basements (can't penetrate thesad). Building placement follows foundation geometry, not street grids. The Great Bazaar: white market on foundation tops, grey market in the street-canyons between.

**Interstitial construction**: Buildings BETWEEN ancient bronze columns, never touching them. The five-foot gap maintained for reasons no one remembers. Normal stone-and-timber construction (3-5 stories) that looks ordinary until you notice it's surrounded by bronze-alloy pillars. Commerce colonizes the gap—stalls, awnings stretching toward columns without touching. The formality of the monument creates the informality of the margin.

**Shadow-of-arcology neighborhoods**: Conventional construction in the shadow of 100-meter structures. The arcologies shape everything: drainage, sight lines, the angle of sunlight. Everyday Mediterranean vernacular with impossible structures looming nearby. Three arcologies survive in the Medina: The Angle (sheared at 30°), Calibration Hall (fully operational), Kethesir's Pillar (gutted, dark above floor four).

**Carved-space adaptation**: Foundation Market, Great Bazaar carved chambers. Subsidence exposing infrastructure never meant to see daylight; commerce colonizing equipment housings, conduit channels, alcoves carved from substrate. More parallel to ya-Don's underground—colonizing Antediluvian spaces never meant for habitation. Tunnels extending into depths nobody has surveyed.

**Archaeological strata**: 50,000 years of occupation visible in construction. Floors worn into canyons by hundreds of millions of footsteps. Thresholds worn through to substrate. Handrails sculpted by touch into organic curves the original builders never intended. Self-repair systems creating juxtapositions—pristine ancient sections next to worn-through human additions. Each era's modifications visible: ancient alloys, then less sophisticated metals, then stone, then timber.

### The Basin/Rim Divide

The Rim (2.5-3 million residents) represents conventional vernacular—"buildings humans built for humans." The basin districts below inherit arcology infrastructure they live within rather than buildings they constructed.

**Comparisons:**

*Rim*: Mediterranean hillside towns—Positano, Amalfi, Cinque Terre. Terraced streets, terracotta roofs, views as organizing principle. Clifftop towns above harbors (Bonifacio, Ronda).

*Foundation-top and interstitial*: Medieval European cities built on Roman foundations, but more extreme because Antediluvian materials cannot be modified. Rome's Campo de' Fiori—layering of old and new, continuous use for millennia, buildings growing in the gaps.

*Carved-space*: Similar to ya-Don's underground—colonizing spaces never meant for habitation.

*Archaeological strata*: No real parallel. 50,000 years of continuous occupation has no Earth equivalent.

### Rotten Quarter Registers

The Rotten Quarter shows what happens when conventional construction meets arcology ruins:

- *Middens*: Vertical slum on pre-Interdict tenement cores. External catwalks, rope bridges, improvised additions in scrap and salvage. Kowloon Walled City density—organic growth without master plan, every surface used.

- *Iron Yards*: Industrial vernacular in a quarried depression. Shipbreaking yard architecture—massive salvage operations, functional structures built from salvage materials. Dry docks cut into bedrock. Worker housing climbing the southern edge.

- *Ridge*: A fallen arcology rotated 90 degrees. The Ninth Horizon Terrace lies ON ITS SIDE—what was 100 meters wide is now 100 meters high. Floors became walls. Elevator shafts became corridors. Pristine self-repairing thesad, but sideways. Generations of cutting and adaptation to make it usable. Shipwreck architecture—a vessel on its side, inhabitants making do.

**Comparisons:**

*Middens*: Kowloon Walled City. Favela stacking. Industrial waterfront slums—structures rising from flooded foundations.

*Iron Yards*: Shipbreaking yards at Alang or Chittagong. Steel mill towns. Quarry architecture where the landscape was the resource.

*Ridge*: Shipwreck architecture. A rotated vessel made habitable. Nothing quite matches it.

**What distinguishes coastal from Central Valley:**
- Pitched roofs vs. flat roofs (rain vs. heat)
- Larger windows vs. small deep-set windows
- Terracotta vs. reflective surfaces
- Views incorporated vs. views blocked
- Moisture management vs. thermal mass
- Building ON/BETWEEN ancients vs. building INTO ancients

## Ya-Tsatsa: The Separate Tradition

Ya-Tsatsa is not Imperial vernacular. Its architecture emerges from different sources.

**Origins:**
- The geology: white-grey quartz diorite cliffs, 300+ feet, hard enough to hold carved detail for millennia
- The religion: Oracle Cult theology that treats showing as suspicious, restraint as virtue
- The climate: constant wind, cool temperatures (50s year-round), salt spray weathering everything

**Core principles:**
- *Monochrome restraint*: All construction uses the same white-gray stone quarried while cutting tombs. No paint. No applied decoration.
- *Material honesty*: Objects reveal what they're made from. Covering materials suggests deception.
- *Functional geometry*: Patterns emerge from structure rather than applied ornament.
- *Earned color*: What little color appears must justify itself (prayer flags marking religious observance, ritual fabrics).
- *Acoustic architecture*: Sound functions as aesthetic element. The ceramic whistles create intentional sonic environment.

**Materials:** Quartz diorite (the cliff itself, cut and shaped but never disguised), undyed linen, bronze for oldest structures, ceramic wind-whistles.

**Colors:** White and pale grey stone. Undyed natural cream for high-status clothing. No bright colors—restraint is the statement.

**What makes it separate:**
- No courtyard organization (cliff-carved, following rock rather than social pattern)
- No bronze threshold fittings (bronze appears on oldest structures as material, not as quotation)
- No accommodation to heat (climate is cool and constant)
- Decoration is suspect rather than restrained

**Comparisons:**

The cliff-carving suggests Petra, but without Petra's ornamentation. The monochrome restraint and material honesty suggest Cycladic architecture (Santorini, Mykonos)—whitewashed, minimal, everything the same color. The religious austerity echoes Tibetan monastic architecture (Meteora, Mount Athos) or Shaker design principles—decoration is suspect, function is virtue.

The acoustic dimension (wind-whistles, chimes creating intentional sonic environment) doesn't have a clear parallel. Aeolian harps scaled to architecture. The city as wind instrument.

Ya-Tsatsa's architecture would look wrong anywhere else. It grows from specific cliffs, specific theology, specific climate. Transplanted to ya-Don, it would be bizarre. Transplanted to ya-Sattra, it would be pretentious.

## Common Elements Across Ya-Don and Ya-Sattra

Despite climate differences and different Antediluvian aesthetics, the two vernacular traditions share:

**Bronze threshold fittings:** Strips at doorways, catches light, marks transitions. Present throughout Imperial conventional construction. A shared Imperial element that appears in both cities regardless of local Antediluvian sources.

**Courtyard organization:** Families face inward to shared space. Ya-Don courtyards manage heat; ya-Sattra courtyards manage social organization. Same pattern, different function.

**Accumulated modification as record:** Buildings show their history. Each generation's repairs visible. Unlike self-repairing thesad, conventional buildings age.

**Teal as Imperial accent:** Where technology permits—lighting, formal spaces, Bureau facilities—teal appears as the Imperial color. More common in ya-Sattra (more power, more technology), but present in ya-Don's aspirational spaces.

**The Antediluvian relationship—two different aesthetics, both absorbed:**

Both cities contain Antediluvian structures. Both vernaculars absorbed their aesthetic—but in different ways:

- *Ya-Sattra*: Art Deco/Cycladic/Byzantine bronze monuments. Geometric patterns. "Circuit board" surface decoration. The vernacular absorbed this as geometric border patterns, bronze fittings, streamlined proportions, smooth stucco finishes. The aspiration is toward elegance.

- *Ya-Don*: Industrial ceramic sublime. Cream and grey fused stone. Asymmetrical domes. Curved forms. The vernacular absorbed this as domed farm buildings, curved arches throughout, smooth plaster surfaces—and as vertical Gothic-meets-Art-Deco spires in aspirational architecture. The aspiration is toward the sublime.

Ya-Sattra's vernacular says "we live near beautiful monuments." Ya-Don's vernacular says "we live near the Furnaces."

## Foreign Enclaves

**Ganati (Amber Quarter, Ganati Enclave):** Rounded adobe forms, copper-inlaid decoration, organic curves, amber-tinted windows. Makes no attempt to fit Imperial vernacular—and doesn't need to.

**Colonial (Coolant District):** Ya-Sattra architecture imposed on ya-Don without adaptation. Climate control as statement of origin. The buildings would look at home in the Medina Quarter; they look wrong in ya-Don.

## Caste Adaptation

Both ya-Don and ya-Sattra vernacular adapt to caste requirements:

**Orevet:** Extended overhangs, muted colors, dim lighting, signage in color ranges baseline eyes conflate.

**Astal:** Miniaturized proportions (doorways that force baseline adults to duck), faster construction, painted aphorism lintels. In ya-Don: Quick Lanes. In the Thousand Kingdoms: Ghaur-Dan's message poles (a different tradition).

**Karst:** Underground construction following geological opportunity rather than surface property lines. Ceilings lower than baseline standard.

**Ductworker:** Infrastructure spaces adapted for residence. Ceiling heights as low as four feet.

The adaptations are structural, not decorative. They address biological requirements, not aesthetic preferences.
