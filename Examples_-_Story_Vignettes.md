# **Vignettes Collection: A RAG Guide to Storytelling in the Post-Interdict Empire**

## **Executive Summary**

This document contains approximately thirty-seven narrative pieces demonstrating how to write fiction in this setting. The vignettes are not reference material in the way the Index or location documents areâ€”they don't provide facts to be looked up. They provide models for tone, voice, and the handling of material that could easily become either morally simplistic or aesthetically overwrought.

The guide below extracts principles for storytelling, identifies recurring themes that define the setting's concerns, and critically examines where these pieces fall into patterns that should be avoided.

---

## **Recurring Themes**

### **Maintained Fictions**

The setting is full of people sustaining systems they know are broken. The Scale Bureau advocate maintains a docket for interstellar courts that will never convene. The frontier tax collector reports fictional numbers to satisfy ledgers designed for an empire that no longer exists. The ya-Tsatsa software companies export products through processes no one observes. The DraÃ«then who returns home after twenty-seven years finds a collective that may take decades to decide whether she belongs.

This is not hypocrisy. The characters understand what they're doing. They maintain the fiction because the alternativeâ€”acknowledging that the court will never sit, that the Empire claims territories it doesn't control, that no one knows what happens in the production hallsâ€”would require confronting a collapse that everyone has agreed to pretend hasn't happened.

Stories in this setting often work by taking a maintained fiction seriously. What does it actually mean to inherit responsibility for a docket that generates forty obols monthly and will never produce a judgment? What does it feel like to file quarterly reports for a facility you're forbidden to enter?

### **Inheritance as Burden**

The plasma lances kill their wielders. The patent requires midnight procedures no one understands. The hereditary position pays a pension insufficient for survival. The biological modifications that made ancestors useful for colonizing gas giant moons now trap their descendants in radioactive cities.

Characters inherit obligations they didn't choose, technologies they can't comprehend, and positions that constrain without empowering. The es-Norrel colonel can fire weapons that will poison him. The es-Tel custodian can implement patterns that change reality in ways she cannot observe. The es-Kallo advocate can maintain precedents for a jurisdiction forty light-years away.

This theme connects to the setting's central premise: the Oracles encoded hierarchy into biology before departing, leaving humans to navigate systems designed by intelligences that no longer explain themselves.

### **The Gap Between Official and Actual**

The Empire claims sovereignty over all human-inhabited space. It controls three cities on the former California coast. The Bureau of the Scale maintains courts for disputes between star systems. The Bureau of the Lens classifies information about technologies that stopped working millennia ago. The tax collector assesses villages where people think "the Empire" is a legend about walls beyond the burning mountains.

Characters navigate this gap constantly. They know what the ledgers require and what reality permits. They develop the skills to translate between systems that assume universal authority and circumstances that demonstrate its absence. The tax collector takes three hundred obols and reports three thousand. The advocate files forms acknowledging a court "available for emergency convention." The provincial aristocrat demonstrates sword techniques with antiques the capital nobles have never touched.

### **Biology as Social Position**

The Sickly cannot perceive negative outcomes. The Ashrat run hot and die young. The Avouvar live centuries in radioactive cities. The DraÃ«then cannot make decisions without collective alignment. The es-Valenti sisters are iterations of the same genome, raised in isolation, preparing for marriages to cousins they'll never meet.

Class is not merely economic or politicalâ€”it's engineered into bodies. The labor castes aren't workers who happen to be modified; they're modifications that happen to be workers. This creates moral complexity that simple oppression narratives can't capture. The Sickly narrator isn't sufferingâ€”she genuinely experiences her life as pleasant. The Ashrat workers aren't exploited in ways they resentâ€”their metabolisms require the conditions that shorten their lives.

Stories that flatten this into "the castes are oppressed" miss the setting's point. The horror is precisely that the system works as designed. The modifications produce the intended results. The question isn't whether the system is cruel but whether cruelty can be built so deep into reality that it stops being cruelty and becomes simply how things are.

### **Competence Under Constraint**

Characters in these vignettes are generally good at what they do. The advocate knows his precedents. The tax collector understands his ledgers. The craftsperson can identify pre-Interdict metallurgy by touch. The parish curate manages logistics that keep three hundred people fed.

They're trapped not by stupidity but by systems. The es-Kostrel son understands exactly why the Bureau appointment will alienate him from industrial reality; he also understands why refusing it will limit his family's influence. The Ganati senator understands exactly how the intelligence services are manipulating him; his confession is a calculated move, not a breakdown.

This creates a different kind of tragedy than stories about naive characters discovering corruption. These characters know. They've always known. The question is what they do with knowledge that doesn't translate into freedom.

## **Principles for Storytelling**

### **Start with Work**

Most effective vignettes begin with someone doing their job. The advocate dusts books. The tax collector reviews ledgers. The craftsperson shapes copper. The strangeness of the setting emerges through the work rather than being announced.

This grounds fantastic elements in practical reality. We understand what a plasma lance is because we watch someone inherit the responsibility to maintain it. We understand what the Testament Society does because we follow someone through initiation. The work provides structure; the implications accumulate.

### **Characters Should Understand Their Situations**

No one in these vignettes is discovering the Empire's dysfunction for the first time. The advocate knows his docket is fiction. The tax collector knows the assessment is impossible. The Testament Society initiate knows the degrees may never lead to revelation.

This creates different dramatic possibilities than stories about awakening or discovery. The drama comes from how characters navigate knowledge they already possessâ€”what accommodations they make, what resistances they maintain, what prices they pay for understanding without power.

### **Sensory Specificity Over Abstraction**

The destrier's clicking. The ash taste on the tongue. The particular shimmer of Ashrat skin. The aurora-colors against absolute black. The weight of copper in the hand.

Abstract statements about "the Empire's decay" or "the burden of inheritance" communicate less than concrete details that imply these conditions. The advocate's books that no one buys, the pension that doesn't cover food, the shop built into arcology foundationsâ€”these create meaning that summary statements cannot.

### **Exposition Through Implication**

The Binary Stars advocate doesn't explain the Interdict. He maintains a docket for courts that will never convene, and the Interdict's consequences emerge from that activity. The ya-Tsatsa narrator doesn't explain the Testament Society's theology. She describes climbing through degrees that promise revelation and deliver only further preparation.

Readers should feel they're seeing a small portion of something vast, not receiving a tour of the complete system. The setting's depth comes from what's implied by what's shown, not from what's explained alongside what's shown.

### **Moral Complexity Through Restraint**

The tax collector destroys a family. The story doesn't condemn this. The revolutionary kills a guard. The story doesn't celebrate this. The Sickly narrator describes plagues in terms of peaceful faces. The story doesn't correct her perception.

This restraint is difficult. The impulse to editorializeâ€”to ensure readers understand what they should feelâ€”produces stories that are morally simple in ways that betray the setting. The Post-Interdict Empire is designed to explore situations where different moral frameworks are internally consistent but incompatible. Stories that resolve this complexity into clear judgments miss the point.

### **The Weird Should Be Ordinary**

Characters who live with strange things treat them as normal. The Mutterer processions are how ya-Tsatsa works. The clicking of destriers is what you hear on the scablands. The Furnaces' hum is the sound of ya-Don at night.

Wonder belongs to outsiders and newcomers. The Kma-Dhol traveler who sees ya-Sattra for the first time can experience awe. The advocate who has maintained the Binary Stars docket for forty years experiences only the weight of routine. Matching character reaction to character experience prevents the setting from feeling like a tour designed to impress.

## **Where These Stories Fail**

The vignettes are generally successful, but they exhibit patterns that should be avoided:

### **Over-Explaining the Point**

Several pieces end with statements that make their themes explicit. "The system worked, after a fashion. Even if no one could explain exactly why." "It was, after all, his inheritance." These conclusions feel neat in ways that undercut the ambiguity the stories otherwise maintain.

The fix is to end earlier, or to end on action rather than reflection. Trust readers to understand what the story has shown them.

### **Repetitive Sentence Structures**

Some pieces fall into rhythmic patterns that become monotonous: "X did Y. Then X did Z. X felt A." Varying sentence length and structure, interrupting sequences with dialogue or sensory detail, prevents this mechanical quality.

### **Contrastive Structures**

"Not X but Y" and "Not X exactly, but something like X" appear too frequently. "Not kindness exactly, but something that served the same function." "Not a smile but something adjacent to one." These constructions become tics. Find other ways to express nuance.

### **The Word "Particular"**

Overused throughout as an intensifier. "That particular exhaustion." "The particular weight of." "Something particular in his expression." Once noticed, it becomes distracting.

### **Sameness of Narrative Voice**

Many pieces share a similar authorial tone regardless of whose perspective they occupy. The Sickly piece succeeds partly because its voice is genuinely differentâ€”the narrator's inability to perceive negativity shapes the prose itself. Other first-person pieces don't achieve this distinctiveness; they sound like the same narrator wearing different costumes.

When writing in close perspective, the character's cognition should shape the prose. An Ashrat worker thinks in terms of heat, shift schedules, guild politics. A Creche Aureate thinks in terms of iteration, optimization, genomic legacy. A DraÃ«then thinks in terms of consensus, alignment, collective purpose. These different cognitive styles should produce different sentence structures, different vocabularies, different rhythms.

### **Lists Disguised as Prose**

Some passages catalog elements rather than integrating them into narrative flow. This creates the impression of thoroughness without the experience of immersion. If a scene requires mentioning many things, find ways to make the enumeration serve a narrative purposeâ€”a character counting, a process unfolding, a journey progressingâ€”rather than simply listing.

### **Theatrical Epiphanies**

Several pieces end with characters realizing something significant. This can work, but it often feels too neatâ€”as though the story exists to deliver the realization. The strongest pieces end in action or continuation rather than insight. The advocate files his report. The tax collector moves to the next village. The coppersmith picks up the pipe again.

## **When to Consult This Document**

Before writing fiction in this setting, read several vignettes to calibrate tone. When struggling with how to handle morally complex material, examine how these pieces maintain restraint. When unsure how to integrate exposition, study how the Binary Stars or ya-Tsatsa pieces imply rather than explain.

Do not treat these pieces as templates to be imitated phrase by phrase. The goal is to internalize approaches, not to reproduce specific executions.

Es-Norrel began the targeting sequence. The lances tracked in perfect unison, following his baton's movements like flowers following the sun. The mathematics carved in stone aligned with the weapons' internal calculations, centuries of preparation crystallizing into a single moment of potential.

He could glass the entire warband. Turn them into radioactive ash that would poison the ground for decades. Remind the Thousand Kingdoms why the es-Norrel holdings had stood inviolate for three centuries.

And then what? Wait another sixty years for the next excuse? Die like his mother, cooked from within by weapons he owned but couldn't repair? Continue this vigil over technology that killed its wielders as often as its targets?

The raiders were already retreating, horses wheeling away from the wrongness in the air. They'd return, eventually. They always did. But not today.

Es-Norrel lowered the baton. The lances powered down with a descending whine, plasma seeds dissolving back into potential. The heat distortion faded. Reality solidified.

"Sir?" Cray asked.

"Mark it in the log," es-Norrel said. "Successful deterrence. No ammunition expended."

He climbed down from the platform, joints aching from tension he hadn't noticed. Tomorrow he'd run the maintenance protocols again, check the containment readings, update the targeting mathematics. The cycle would continue.

**The Lesson of Five Mirrors**

"In markets," Teacher continued, "different people exchange objects for coins."

Teacher gestured, and Assistant entered. Assistant was iteration twelve of a line designed for visual demonstrations. She was shorter than Teacher, paler, with green eyes instead of brown. The difference was deliberately jarring.

"Imagine," Teacher said, "that I want Assistant's water, and Assistant wants my food."

The sisters watched, fascinated, as Teacher and Assistant performed an elaborate pantomime of exchange. It looked like a dance they didn't know the steps to.

"But why wouldn't Assistant just give you water?" Sava-4 (secretly Questions) interrupted. "She's yours."

Another careful pause. Teacher had been modified for patienceâ€”her endocrine system literally couldn't produce frustration. "In the outside world, people are not owned by other people."

Door-Warden enteredâ€”massive, silent, from a guardian line that hadn't been updated in three hundred years. His presence meant the lesson was disturbing the es-Valenti Matriarch three floors below. "Perhaps," Teacher suggested, "we should visit Lower Gallery."

Lower Gallery was their favorite placeâ€”a vast hall where previous iterations of their genome lined the walls in portrait form. Sava-1-through-5 of six generations ago. As they walkedâ€”in perfect synchronization, because they always walked in perfect synchronizationâ€”Sava-5 (secretly Echo, because she always spoke last) whispered to her sisters: "Do you think we're random to them?"

"No," Sava-1 finally answered. "We're the opposite of random. That's why we're valuable."

They nodded in perfect unison, five identical heads moving as one. Outside their arcology, ya-Sattra sprawled with its millions of different people, its markets and conflicts and negotiations. Teacher watched them walk ahead, her modified neurology processing their question about randomness. She'd been designed to find the answer obvious: of course the sisters weren't random to outsiders. They were being prepared to rule an Empire they'd never see, through marriages to cousins they'd never meet, creating children who would be iterations of iterations, each generation more perfectly isolated from the random world they nominally controlled.
