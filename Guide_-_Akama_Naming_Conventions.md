# Akama Naming Conventions

This guide outlines the structural and phonetic parameters for generating or analyzing names associated with the Akama cultural complex of the Thousand Kingdoms. These conventions apply primarily to ethnic Akama and Malpais-descended populations, though Imperial-origin individuals integrated into Akama society follow variant patterns.

## I. Phonology and Sound Patterns

Akama phonology draws from influences somewhere between Tibeto-Burman and Turkish languages, producing a harsh, throat-focused sound that most Imperial and Ganati speakers find difficult to replicate without childhood training.

### Consonant Patterns

The consonant inventory emphasizes:

* **Harsh clusters:** ts, gh, kh, dh  
* **Hard stops:** k, t, d (prominently featured)  
* **Liquids and nasals:** r, l, m, n (frequent)  
* **Glottal stops:** Multiple stops within single words are common

The phrase "standing in the rain" (*ghol krom dhaal*) exemplifies typical consonant clustering requiring three distinct glottal stops.

### Vowel Patterns

Vowels tend toward:

* **Dark vowels:** a, o, u predominate  
* **Reduced articulation:** Vowels are described as "almost swallowed," emerging from deep in the throat  
* **Secondary vowels:** e and i appear but less frequently

The overall acoustic quality is harsh and rough to ears trained on Imperial Common or Ganati trade-tongue, with sounds living "deep in the throatâ€”gutturals that rumble in the chest, vowels so dark they seem to emerge from the stomach."

### Name Length

Personal names range from monosyllabic to trisyllabic, with disyllabic forms most common.

**Observed Examples:**

* Monosyllabic: Som, Gan, Khol, Tam, Vey  
* Disyllabic: Tsoka, Ataru, Imek, Ghom, Khol, Sevak, Kelam, Gorin  
* Trisyllabic: Kherzen, Tselkor, Buragh

## II. Personal Names (Public)

Public personal names function as the standard address form in most contexts. These names are not calqued and, though they derive from Akama words, are not considered to have lexical meaning outside their use as names.

**Usage:** These serve as the basic identification in public contexts, business dealings, and formal address. They may be used alone or combined with other naming elements depending on context and social relationship.

## III. Astrological Names

The Akama employ a complex phasal calendar combining 7-13 day phase counts with cycles of named phases related to Malpais animals, plants, and natural phenomena. This calendar system generates two types of astrological names.

### Family Astrological Names

**Structure:** Number \+ Phase Element

**Function:** Captures the astrological character of the family line. These are publicly used surnames, particularly common in the Malpais and among Kma-Dhol populations.

**Documented Examples:**

* Thirteen-Hawk (as in Khol Thirteen-Hawk)  
* Nine Birds (as in Vey Nine Birds)

**Representative Forms:**

* Nine Reed  
* Thirteen Yarrow  
* Ten Bear  
* Seven Elk  
* Twelve Salmon  
* Five Stone  
* Eight Wind

**Cultural Context:** These names carry lexical meaning and astrological significance. The specific day and phase combination is believed to reflect the family's essential character and fortune. Certain combinations are considered particularly auspicious or inauspicious, influencing marriage negotiations and alliance formation.

### Private Astrological Names

**Structure:** Number \+ Phase Element (same pattern as family names)

**Function:** Reflects the specific day on which a child was named (not necessarily born). Each individual receives a private astrological name used only among family members.

**Usage Rules:**

* Used exclusively in private, familial contexts  
* Never employed in public address or formal settings  
* Maintained even by families who have adopted non-astrological public surnames  
* Persist among Akama-cultured populations in Ogon who otherwise use epithets or Imperial names

**Naming Timing:** Children born on unlucky days are not named on their birth date. The family waits until the next day with favorable fortune, ensuring the child receives an auspicious private name regardless of their actual birth timing.

**Cultural Significance:** The persistence of this practice even among populations who have abandoned other traditional naming conventions demonstrates its deep cultural importance. Families without astrological surnames still use these private names among themselves, maintaining intimate connection to traditional calendrical systems.

## IV. Descriptive Epithets

Epithets are earned or descriptive names common in Ogon and among warbands, often replacing or supplementing astrological surnames.

**Structure:** Personal Name \+ Epithet

**Function:** These descriptive additions may reference physical characteristics, achievements, personal qualities, or significant events. They carry lexical meaning.

**Documented Examples:**

* Tsoka Red Deer  
* Ataru Blackfish  
* Imek Stonefoot  
* Iron-Rain

**Usage Patterns:**

*Standard form:* The epithet follows the personal name (Imek Stonefoot), functioning similarly to a surname while remaining descriptively meaningful.

*Inherited form:* Epithets may function as family names, passing through generations. Alternatively, they may be replaced each generation with new descriptive terms, or dropped entirely if deemed no longer applicable.

*Institutional form:* Some epithets become so dominant they completely replace the personal name in common usage. The individual known as "Iron-Rain" possesses a personal name that is not used in practiceâ€”the epithet has become their identity. This pattern is particularly common among successful war leaders and prominent figures.

*Warband identification:* When additional identification is needed, the warband name may be appended (e.g., "Imek Stonefoot of the Cracked Shield Band").

## V. Imperial Names

**Structure:** Given Name \+ Family Name (Imperial style)

**Example:** Bal Tama

**Context:** These individuals participate fully in Akama cultural life, including adoption of private astrological names for family use, while maintaining Imperial naming conventions for public identification. The retention of Imperial names does not indicate lesser integration or status within Akama societyâ€”it simply reflects their genetic origin while acknowledging their cultural affiliation.

**Tama Family Note:** The Tama family of Ogon exemplifies this pattern. Despite maintaining Imperial names across generations and employing strategies characteristic of Imperial merchant families, they are fully integrated into Akama political structures, with family members holding Junta tokens and participating in Akama governance.

## VI. Social Context and Usage

### Public vs. Private Names

Akama naming operates on two parallel tracks:

* **Public sphere:** Personal names, astrological surnames (when used), epithets, or Imperial names  
* **Private sphere:** Astrological personal names used exclusively among family

This dual system persists even among populations who have largely abandoned traditional practices in other areas. An Ogon merchant with an Imperial name and no astrological surname still uses a private calendrical name with their children and siblings.

### Regional Variations

**Malpais populations:** Heavily favor astrological surnames combined with personal names. The traditional pattern remains strongest here, with families maintaining detailed calendrical records and consulting them for major decisions.

**Ogon populations:** More likely to use epithets as surnames or adopt Imperial naming patterns. However, private astrological names remain nearly universal in family contexts.

**Kma-Dhol populations:** Consistently use astrological surnames even when integrated into Ogon society. The technical specialist community maintains traditional naming as cultural marker.

**Warband-affiliated populations:** Epithets are most common, often institutionalized to the point where personal names are forgotten. The epithet becomes the identity.

### Naming and Status

Unlike Imperial or Ganati naming conventions, Akama names do not systematically encode social status or lineage prestige. The system values different qualities:

**Astrological names** indicate connection to traditional calendar systems and Malpais cultural roots, but don't necessarily confer higher status than epithets or Imperial names.

**Epithets** may indicate achievement and recognition within warrior culture, but inherited epithets carry no more weight than newly earned ones.

**Imperial names** among culturally Akama populations indicate mixed heritage but not subordinate statusâ€”the Tamas' Imperial names don't diminish their position in Ogon's power structure.

What matters is not the type of name but the individual's demonstrated capability, fortune, and adherence to Akama cultural values of endurance and direct action.

### Translation and Meaning

**Public names and Imperial names:** Not considered to have lexical meaning outside their function as names. Though they may derive from Akama or Imperial words historically, they are treated as proper nouns without translatable content.

**Astrological names (both family and private):** Explicitly carry lexical meaning related to the calendar phase elements (Reed, Yarrow, Bear, etc.) and are interpreted for astrological significance.

**Epithets:** Retain clear lexical meaning (Red Deer, Blackfish, Stonefoot, Iron-Rain), describing characteristics or achievements.

This creates interesting dynamics where some elements of a person's full name are semantically transparent (the epithet "Stonefoot" means exactly what it says) while others are opaque (the personal name "Imek" is simply a name).
