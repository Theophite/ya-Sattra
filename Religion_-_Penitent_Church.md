# **The Penitents**

## **RAG Navigation Guide**

### **Executive Summary**

The Penitent Church is the principal religious institution in the Post-Interdict world—a transnational body spanning the Undying Empire, the Republic of Ganat, and the Thousand Kingdoms, unified by shared liturgy and divided by irreconcilable interpretations of that liturgy's meaning. This document covers approximately 8,000 words across five major sections: hierarchy, liturgy, the Church's position in the Empire, the Church's position in Ganat, and detailed profiles of six representative parishes.

The document's central concern is managed contradiction. The Church teaches universal equality before cosmic judgment while operating within political systems that deny it. The Plea—the fifty-four part liturgical cycle commemorating humanity's quarantine—provides shared temporal framework while allowing interpretations ranging from passive acceptance to active revolution. The autocephalous structure that protects the Church from decapitation also prevents unified action on anything beyond the most basic liturgical elements.

The Penitent Church is not the Oracle Cult. The Oracle Cult is the state religion of the Empire, venerating the Oracles as sources of divine guidance. Penitentism teaches that humanity shares collective guilt for the Interdict and must demonstrate wisdom to earn eventual liberation. The two traditions coexist uneasily: most Imperial subjects observe Oracle feast days and maintain household shrines while some also attend Penitent services. The Empire designates Penitentism as a "discouraged form of belief" subject to capitation tax.

## **Hierarchy**

The Penitent Church maintains a formal ecclesiastical structure that operates through careful tension between centralized spiritual authority and regional autonomy. The hierarchy functions less as a unified command chain than as a collection of semi-independent bodies bound by shared liturgy and contested theology.

### **The Intercessor**

At the apex sits the Intercessor, currently Esthan Omir, who speaks from the Cathedral of the Plea in ya-Sattra. His position carries symbolic weight as the voice of unified Penitentism, but his actual authority extends primarily through influence and precedent rather than direct command. Omir navigates this limitation through extensive political connections within the Imperial bureaucracy, leveraging his Highborn status and intellectual credentials to maintain the Church's precarious position within the Empire. His authority derives from control of the Cathedral itself—the site where the original Witnesses knelt—and his role in coordinating the annual Tenth Observance when the complete Plea transmits into space.

Omir embodies the Church's contradictions personally. His Highborn status and failed examination create a figure who belongs to the elite while resenting its structures. He maintains extensive Bureau connections, leveraging personal relationships to protect Church operations while never directly challenging Imperial authority. His intellectual faith allows him to preach equality as theological abstraction while practicing the patrician condescension that keeps Bureau suspicions manageable. Through Omir, the Church speaks a language of compliance that permits continued operation.

### **The Patriarchates and Matriarchates**

The autocephalous Patriarchates and Matriarchates form the true power centers of the transnational Church. Each maintains complete administrative independence, interpreting doctrine according to local political realities and theological priorities.

**The Imperial Matriarchate** under Elara Vess operates under constant Bureau surveillance, preaching passive atonement to avoid sedition charges while protecting parishes through careful legal maneuvering. Every theological position must be calibrated against probable Bureau reaction.

**The Ganati Patriarchate**, led by the revolutionary veteran Kaleb, champions a works-based theology where atonement comes through active opposition to Imperial authority. Kaleb is formally subordinate to the Intercessor, but this is theoretical—the Intercessor's authority is persuasive rather than coercive, and the Ganati Church is functionally autocephalous.

**The Thousand Kingdoms** maintain their own Warlord-Patriarch who interprets the Oracles as war-spirits and conducts services that would be unrecognizable in ya-Sattra's Cathedral.

These national churches meet in synods that are sites of theological warfare rather than unified governance. No Patriarch or Matriarch can compel another; their autocephaly is absolute. The Intercessor theoretically mediates these disputes but lacks enforcement mechanisms beyond moral suasion and control of certain ceremonial functions. This structural independence protects the Church from complete suppression—the Empire cannot decapitate what has multiple, self-governing heads—but prevents unified action on anything beyond the most basic liturgical elements.

### **The Arch-Curacies**

Below the Patriarchates, Arch-Curacies oversee large dioceses with considerable local autonomy. The Arch-Curate of ya-Don has effectively merged Church functions with industrial unions, while the Arch-Curate of ya-Sattra manages a portfolio ranging from high mysticism in the Antediluvian Quarter to desperate poverty relief in the Midden Tenements. These regional authorities interpret their Patriarch or Matriarch's theological position through local needs, creating further doctrinal variation.

In Ganat, the Church divides into three arch-curacies reflecting geographic and political tensions:

**Taho** operates under a Dawn Party arch-curate appointed for political reasons, while virtually every parish curate maintains Dusk Party affiliation. These clergy interpret works-based theology through commercial lens: successful trade as wisdom, profitable investment as good stewardship.

**Ganat proper** hosts the Patriarchate and manages the impossible balance between competing consuls and political factions.

**The Eastern Territories** encompass vast sparse settlements where the Church provides essential services and early warning against external threats.

### **Local Parishes**

Local parishes operate under Curates whose selection varies dramatically by location—elected by union membership in ya-Don, appointed through careful political calculation in ya-Sattra, claimed through combat visions in the Thousand Kingdoms. This bottom level maintains the Church's actual presence in communities, adapting universal doctrine to specific circumstances while preserving enough orthodox elements to remain nominally Penitent.

## **Liturgy**

### **The Plea**

The liturgical calendar of the Plea forms the rhythm of Penitent life. The fifty-four part cycle, divided into the Common Confession (which is the same every year) and the Particular Confession (which forms a sort of variable service) creates a shared temporal framework across the fractured Penitent world—every congregation, from the Cathedral to war-chapels, moves through the same sequence.

### **The Common Confession and Its Schisms**

The Common Confession's opening Testimony introduces an immediate schism: when Imperial congregations recite the "refusal of the Archons," they perform a delicate reinterpretation, treating their Emperor as a holy being led astray rather than a tyrant who chose damnation. This weekly parsing of blame becomes particularly acute during the verses describing the Testimony of the Witnesses.

In ya-Sattra's Cathedral, Intercessor Omir delivers these passages with studied neutrality, allowing his Highborn congregation to hear what they need—that unnamed counselors corrupted their sovereign, that the Imperial structure remains fundamentally legitimate despite its failures. The term "occultation" itself carries careful ambiguity. Whether the Emperor has withdrawn into seclusion, undergone some transformation, or simply died matters less than maintaining the fiction that he might return, purified and ready to lead humanity from its quarantine.

Meanwhile, in Ganati parishes or the Thousand Kingdoms, the same verses condemn the Empire in the harshest possible terms. The Emperor's refusal joins the Archons' transgression as active betrayal rather than passive failure. The war-priests frame his occultation as divine justice, proof that those who violate the cosmic order eventually consume themselves.

### **The Particular Confession**

The Particular Confession offers even more interpretive latitude. A Curate in the Furnaces of ya-Don might confess their "participation" in unsafe working conditions—technically admitting personal fault while systematically documenting management's negligence. The rhetorical structure protects the speaker through formal compliance while enabling radical critique.

Revolutionary cells have perfected this technique, transforming personal admissions into collective indictments. Sari Loak, when she confesses her "submission before unjust authority," is understood by her prayer circle to be referring to her payment of Chain-Man tolls.

### **The Tenth Observance**

The annual transmission of the complete Plea during the Tenth Observance adds another dimension. The dish pointed carefully away from the galactic core becomes humanity's annual report to no one—or perhaps to anyone else who might be listening in directions the Foreigners haven't forbidden. Every Particular Confession from the year streams into space. The Empire tolerates this because it must; to forbid the transmission would acknowledge that words have power, that collective testimony might matter.

Major observances bring diverse choirs together. The Tenth Observance assembles fifteen voices from each participating congregation, five sections of three representing mathematical perfection. For communities like Valve-Saint Parish—sewer-fishers, dock workers, the forgotten—an invitation to sing at the Cathedral is an opportunity to be seen, to exist publicly in ways their daily lives do not permit.

### **The Quiet Days**

The Quiet Days, when the final two verses are sung, mark periods of special vulnerability. These verses, held in reserve from the weekly cycle, contain the most direct acknowledgments of humanity's state—passages too raw for regular services but necessary for complete testimony. Different traditions emerge around these days: fasting in orthodox parishes, gatherings in revolutionary cells, careful silence in Imperial territories where certain interpretations edge toward sedition.

### **Accommodation of Contradiction**

The genius of the liturgical structure lies in its accommodation of contradiction. The Common Confession provides unity through shared sequence while allowing infinite local variation. The Particular Confession enables every specific grievance to become liturgy. The liturgical calendar ensures that even parishes that despise each other move through the same ritual time. The Plea becomes less a prayer than a framework for containing the uncontainable—humanity's rage and contrition, formatted into verses that can be sung by every prisoner no matter their status, each hearing their own meaning in the harmonies.

## **The Church in the Undying Empire**

The Penitent Church occupies a precarious position within the Imperial structure—simultaneously necessary and suspect, legally protected yet constantly surveilled, doctrinally opposed to the caste system while operating within its constraints. This relationship functions through multiple overlapping mechanisms of control and accommodation that create a sustained institutional tension without resolution.

### **Surveillance and Ideological Management**

The Bureau of the Lens maintains continuous surveillance of Church activities, correctly identifying Penitent egalitarian theology as fundamentally incompatible with Imperial hierarchy. The Church's central doctrine—that all humans share equal guilt for the Interdict regardless of caste—directly contradicts the Empire's foundational premise that different human categories serve different purposes. Bureau analysts classify even moderate Church activities as potential sedition, documenting everything from sermon content to attendance patterns. The Imperial Matriarchate under Elara Vess operates with this surveillance as a given constraint, calibrating every theological position against probable Bureau reaction.

### **Economic Control Through Taxation**

The designation of Penitentism as a "discouraged form of belief" manifests most clearly through the capitation tax—forty obols per person per quarter, a sum calibrated to be manageable for middle-class congregations while devastating for the poor. This base rate excludes processing fees, verification fees for those lacking birth documentation, back taxes for previous unregistered quarters, and escalating penalties for late registration. For a parish like Valve-Saint, registering fifteen choir members approaches six hundred obols before additional fees—several months of collective income.

The tax transforms religious affiliation into an economic decision, forcing potential converts to weigh spiritual conviction against material survival. It generates a web of dependency as wealthier parishes routinely pay for poorer congregations, creating patron-client relationships that fragment Church unity. The Congregation of the Drowned Star might fund Valve-Saint's registration through "theological research grants," but this charity reinforces the very hierarchies that Penitent doctrine supposedly rejects. The intellectuals study poverty while their financial support maintains it as a separate phenomenon rather than shared condition.

In practice, most capitation is paid by wealthy Penitents. A sewer-fisher at Valve-Saint might pay twelve obols per year for registration—a sum that still hurts but doesn't destroy—while an Aureate benefactor covers the difference through one of many charitable mechanisms. The Bureau tolerates this arrangement because making the poor depend on the rich is the point. The formal rate exists not to be collected uniformly but to create the dependency it inevitably produces.

This system weaponizes Church theology against itself. While doctrine teaches universal equality before cosmic judgment, the capitation tax ensures wealthy and poor Penitents experience faith through fundamentally different material conditions. An Aureate at Stars-Under-Stone pays without consideration; a sewer-fisher at Valve-Saint chooses between registration and feeding their family. The equality promised by doctrine becomes impossible when declaring faith carries unequal cost.

### **Legal Precedents and Parallel Authority**

The Empire cannot simply suppress the Church. The Third Suppression, a historical uprising whose details remain classified, resulted in legal precedents that protect certain Church functions. The Church maintains its own documentary system—baptismal certificates, marriage records, burial registrations—that legally supersedes Bureau requirements for religious purposes. This creates a parallel authentication system through which undocumented populations can achieve limited recognition. Church documentation can provide identity for those the Bureau refuses to acknowledge: refugees, deserters, failed clone lines, and the undocumented poor.

The Bureau tolerates this parallel system because the alternative, demonstrated during the Third Suppression, proved more destabilizing than accommodation. Yet this tolerance comes with conditions. Baptismal registration requires theological sponsors whose credentials the Bureau won't question, maintaining class distinctions even within the supposedly egalitarian Church structure. The very populations most needing Church sanctuary face impossible choices: remain invisible and excluded from formal participation, or register and expose themselves to Bureau scrutiny.

### **Institutional Interdependence**

The relationship functions through calculated ambiguity and mutual recognition of limits. When the Church transmits the complete Plea into space during the Tenth Observance, the Bureau permits this technically illegal broadcast because forbidding it would acknowledge that words directed at nothing might matter. When parishes provide essential social services in areas the state has abandoned—healthcare in the Furnaces, food distribution in the Middens, education for undocumented children—Bureau agents document these as potential sedition while depending on them to prevent actual uprising.

Different parishes navigate this relationship through distinct strategies that exploit various vulnerabilities in Imperial governance:

* **The Congregation of the Drowned Star** attracts surplus intelligentsia whose theoretical radicalism the Bureau considers harmless academic exercise.  
* **Stars-Under-Stone** serves Aureates whose devotion to Penitent doctrine creates a paradox the Bureau cannot easily resolve.  
* **The Furnace parishes** merge religious authority with union power, making suppression economically catastrophic—the three hundred obols that might destroy a Midden parish represents less than an hour's production value from the Furnaces.

### **Structural Tension Without Resolution**

The relationship ultimately rests on sustained institutional conflict maintained below the threshold of open confrontation. The Church understands that explicit political action triggers suppression; the Empire understands that eliminating the Church creates vacuums it cannot fill. The capitation tax maps these boundaries precisely—high enough to burden, not high enough to provoke uprising, documenting the exact limits of what the Empire can enforce without triggering systemic resistance.

Every quarter, as parishes scramble to pay taxes and wealthier congregations calculate their charity, the system reproduces the inequality that Penitent theology condemns while preventing that condemnation from achieving material force. The Bureau documents everything, the Church continues operating, and both institutions maneuver within constraints that have persisted for centuries, creating a relationship defined not by resolution but by the careful management of irreconcilable opposition.

## **The Church in Ganat**

The Ganati Church operates as an institutionally independent religious body whose authority predates both the Republic and the Sultanate. Ancient parishes possess property rights and legal standing that survived multiple political transitions, creating a parallel power structure that Patriarch Kaleb inherited rather than created. His revolutionary theology—works-based atonement achieved through material progress—represents an attempt to harness these ancient institutions for modern political purposes, with mixed results.

### **Theological Innovation and Political Reality**

Kaleb's transformation of Penitent doctrine from passive acceptance to active struggle fundamentally altered the Church's role in Ganati society. By redefining atonement as progress through human works—building infrastructure, advancing technology, resisting oppression—he created theological justification for the Republic's revolutionary program. This doctrine argues that humanity demonstrates growing wisdom through material success, proving readiness for eventual liberation from the Interdict.

Kaleb practices Orthodox Penitentism himself, believing in the Plea and demonstrating wisdom to rejoin the interstellar community. Yet he tolerates radicals in Ganat proper who advocate that material progress alone suffices, that the Interdict was unjust, that humanity should overcome rather than accept limitations.

### **Institutional Contradictions**

The Church's advancement structure creates a peculiar disconnect where revolutionary affiliation determines promotion despite most congregations holding conservative views. Rising clergy attend political meetings and write liberation theology while the actual work of ministering to believers—marriages, funerals, education—offers no advancement path. This produces parallel structures: an official hierarchy of revolutionary appointees who issue proclamations, and a permanent class of local curates who actually run parishes and command congregational loyalty.

The Eastern Territories exemplify these contradictions most starkly. Conservative Serrulata pastoralists practice ancient syncretist traditions, interpreting orbital platforms as divine intermediaries while wanting minimal interference with their traditional lifeways. They vote Dusk Party not from commercial interest but from desire to be left alone. Meanwhile, yeoman farmers in new settlements—descendants of revolutionary veterans or recent land grant recipients—embrace Dawn Party ideology and view pastoralists as obstacles to progress. The Church cannot satisfy both constituencies without undermining its claimed theological coherence.

### **Political Entanglement and Independence**

Despite formal independence, the Church cannot escape political entanglement in the Republic's fundamental tensions. Both consuls require religious legitimization, yet neither can control Church institutions. Dawn Consul Rasma Dhal-Setil's austere personal Penitentism provides revolutionary authenticity, while Dusk Consul Datho Kel al-Marwen leverages conservative parishes for commercial stability. The Church provides essential services—education, dispute resolution, social safety nets—that the state cannot replicate, while requiring state protection from potential schism.

Kaleb's authority, rooted in revolutionary credentials rather than institutional position, cannot compel obedience from congregations that predate his theology. Conservative parishes maintain formal allegiance while ignoring revolutionary directives. The appointment of politically reliable but locally powerless arch-curates, like Taho's Dawn Party overseer of Dusk Party parishes, demonstrates both the reach and limits of patriarchal power.

### **Functional Equilibrium**

The current system persists through careful maintenance of useful fictions. Kaleb claims to lead a unified Church while his authority extends only to already sympathetic clergy. Conservative congregations nominally accept revolutionary theology while practicing unchanged traditions. The state treats the Church as legitimizing authority while knowing religious support remains contingent and fractured. These mutual pretenses enable governance despite their fragility.

The Church's genuine independence—economic resources, ancient property rights, congregational loyalty—prevents complete capture by any faction. This preserves institutional survival but undermines the unifying function both political parties expect. Instead of legitimizing state authority, the Church's internal contradictions mirror and amplify political divisions. Every theological dispute acquires political dimension; every political conflict gains religious significance.

The long-term sustainability of this arrangement remains questionable. Kaleb's eventual death will remove the personal authority preventing open schism. Victory by either political party would force the Church to explicitly choose sides. The growing disconnect between revolutionary leadership and conservative congregations must eventually resolve through either hierarchical accommodation or institutional fragmentation. The Church that survived by adapting to successive regimes finds itself unable to fully adapt to a regime requiring active participation rather than passive endurance—a paradox that reflects the broader contradictions of Ganati republicanism itself.

## **Parish Profiles**

### **St. Akhmet's Hammer, ya-Don**

The theological library consists of three books, two of which are safety manuals. The third is a Penitent text so oil-stained that half the pages have fused together. Nobody minds.

St. Akhmet's operates from what was once a turbine maintenance hall, its vaulted ceiling still bearing the crane tracks that now hold union banners. The altar is a decommissioned reactor core housing—perfectly safe, the radiation long since decayed, but newcomers still flinch when they're told to place offerings inside it. Services run exactly forty-five minutes because that's when the shift change happens. The congregation files in still wearing their protective gear, sits for the communal meal (industrial-grade stimulants blessed by whoever's senior that week), and files out to their posts.

"Curate" is an elected position here, rotating every six months among the Master Tenders. The current one, Jerem Solk, treats sermons like safety briefings—clear, practical, focused on keeping everyone alive through another week. When he speaks of sin, it's the sin of skipped maintenance checks. When he speaks of salvation, it's the collective bargaining agreement that ensures death benefits. The Bureau of the Lens has given up trying to determine if this constitutes actual Penitentism or just industrial relations with ceremonial elements.

The real power lies with the Confession Booths—sealed meeting rooms where union representatives conduct negotiations. What's discussed there stays there, protected by both religious privilege and the knowledge that without the Order of St. Akhmet's Furnace, the lights go out everywhere.

### **Stars-Under-Stone, Antediluvian Quarter**

The chapel predates the parish by sixteen hundred years, built into subasements that the Technical Castes once used for purposes no one remembers. The walls still bear their modifications—crystalline structures that hum at frequencies just below hearing, surfaces that seem to fold in on themselves if you stare too long. The Aureates who worship here find it appropriate. They, too, are remnants of a grander age.

The congregation never exceeds thirty. These are the devout among the hereditary nobility—those who genuinely believe in Penitent doctrine despite it undermining everything their class represents. They arrive in unmarked conveyances and kneel as equals. The Curate, a severe woman named Pella (no surname, although there is speculation about which family is hers), conducts services in High Imperial, the archaic dialect that predates the Interdict.

They practice a rarefied mysticism here, seeking to understand the Technical Castes' relationship with transcendence. The wine at the community meal is laced with mild nootropics—nothing illegal, but enough to create the sensation of expanded consciousness. Their confessions are philosophical dissertations on the nature of inherited sin. When they sing the Plea, the cavern's acoustics transform it into something that sounds almost like the Jargon.

The parish survives on anonymous donations that arrive in untraceable forms—rare metals, energy credits, parcels of land suddenly transferred to Church ownership. The Bureau pretends not to notice. These are still Aureates, after all, even if they're playing at poverty.

### **Gridward-Approach, Medina Quarter**

Built in what was once a freight depot, the parish serves the Ganati exile community and a few Imperial families with close Ganati connections—merchants, aristocratic exiles from the Sultanate era, and the occasional spy maintaining deep cover. The politics in the home country don't matter so much in foreign lands. The architecture has been modified with imported sensibilities: geometric patterns in red sand pressed between glass sheets, ablution fountains that serve both Penitent ritual and Ganati custom.

Services are conducted in three languages—Imperial, Ganati, and the pidgin that emerges when the two collide in the marketplace. Children play between the benches while adults exchange news that won't appear in any official record.

### **Valve-Saint Parish, Midden Tenements**

Pumping Station \#7 continues to move water through the district's substrate despite three centuries of deferred maintenance. The parish occupies the overflow chamber at sea level—a cavern designed to handle storm surge, now repurposed for worship. Rows of salvaged benches face the wheel altar, a four-meter valve wheel that local tradition claims once saved the building from catastrophic flooding. Engineers from the Bureau of the Rod periodically confirm it connects to nothing.

The floor uses the original drainage grates. During spring tides and storms, seawater seeps through, never more than ankle-deep but enough that congregants learn to wear appropriate footwear. The pumps themselves occupy the eight levels below, their sound a constant background thrum that occasionally shifts pitch for reasons the remaining Technical documentation doesn't explain.

Above the congregation, Mutterers have colonized the machinery spaces. They've modified components in ways that correlate with improved pump efficiency, though the causation remains unclear. Kesh Ritt rings disruption chimes hourly to prevent the cascading vocalizations that once resulted in three Mutterers attempting to rebuild a pressure valve while congregants were still arriving for service.

**Staff and Operations:** Curate Aram es-Thabit documents everything—not from paranoia but from experience. Two years ago, a Bureau inspector attempted to claim the parish was selling donated grain. The ledgers Mer Hasket maintains, despite requiring his fingers to trace each line multiple times, proved otherwise. The inspector left. The documentation habit remained. Sela Loak runs the kitchen with the efficiency of someone who learned resource management through scarcity.

**The Call for Solidarity:** The parish's innovation emerged from practical need. Direct requests for help imply weakness that could be exploited. Instead, neighbors identify others' struggles using careful language that maintains dignity. "The household with the sick child near the pump access needs medicine." Someone stands, declares they'll provide what they can. Others join or remain silent. The church supplements when possible.

The declarations map the community's internal support networks. Who helps whom reveals clan alliances, emerging romances, old debts being repaid. Luka's lieutenants take notes, understanding these relationships as potential leverage points. The congregation knows this happens. The practice continues anyway because the alternative—no support at all—remains worse than documented vulnerability.

**The Persistence of Function:** The parish serves three thousand people whose documentation ranges from questionable to nonexistent. They arrive for services, receive what support the community can provide, disperse to jobs that barely cover survival. The building continues to pump water, the Mutterers continue their modifications, the congregation continues to gather despite having every reason not to.

This isn't triumph or tragedy but simple continuation. The parish provides structure for mutual support that would exist anyway but might lack coordination. It offers vocabulary for discussing suffering that acknowledges without wallowing. It creates space where gang leader and day laborer stand in the same salt water, even if they leave through different doors.

Valve-Saint persists because the alternative—nothing—serves no one's interests. Even Luka needs the intelligence, the legitimacy, the weekly reminder that his power has limits. Even the Bureau needs someone managing this population. Even the Mutterers need their chimes rung regularly.

### **The Congregation of the Drowned Star**

The parish attracts surplus intelligentsia—academics, failed examination candidates, theologians whose positions are too nuanced for practical ministry. Their theoretical radicalism is genuine: they debate revolutionary theology, analyze the structures of Imperial oppression, propose alternatives to caste hierarchy. The Bureau of the Lens considers them harmless. Academic exercise. Words without consequence.

They fund parishes like Valve-Saint through "theological research grants," maintaining the fiction that their charity is scholarly rather than political. The arrangement preserves their self-image as serious thinkers while ensuring their resources flow to those who need them. Whether this constitutes meaningful action or elaborate self-justification depends on who you ask.

### **Three Red Hawks' Warband Chapel, Thousand Kingdoms**

Whether Three Red Hawks' ordination constitutes legitimate ordination is a question the Imperial Matriarchate refuses to address, but the Bureau of the Sword has pressured her into accepting his claim—it buys the loyalty of the warband to Imperial priorities and keeps them from crossing the border.

The warband maintains strict codes about prisoner treatment and slave management that they insist derive from Penitent doctrine, though no theological scholar can find the relevant passages. Their tithes to the Church arrive as caravans of stolen goods and occasional freed prisoners who "found faith" rather than face the alternative. The Church accepts both, officially condemning the methods while pragmatically recognizing that in the Kingdoms, this is what Penitentism entails.

The chapel's most sacred relic is an actual Technical Caste calculation engine—a significant advance over the lector, non-functional but beautiful—that Three Red Hawks wears into battle believing it makes him immune to bullets. So far, the evidence supports this claim.
