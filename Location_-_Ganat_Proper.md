# Ganat Proper: The Revolutionary Heart

## Executive Summary

Ganat proper occupies Glen Canyon where the Teph River carved through sandstone over countless Records, creating walls 500 feet high. The city of 500,000 sits at the bottom of this canyon, built into and through the sandstone cliffs on both sides of the river—the Near Wall closest to the main road, the Far Wall opposite. Between them spans the Dam: pre-Interdict structure rediscovered long ago, re-engineered in the decades before the Revolution with modern turbines replacing broken archaeotech. The Dam provides power too cheap to meter, flowing through spiderweb transmission lines to every structure in the city and creeping outward to settlements beyond. This abundance of electricity has created an explosion of industry and engineering unique in the Republic—whole factories built inside the Dam itself, working-class neighborhoods carved through sandstone terminating in the Dam's interior, workshops running 24 hours on power that costs almost nothing. Ganat is simultaneously capital city, industrial center, ideological heart, and the place where revolutionary principles meet the complications of actually governing. It's fractious, energetic, ideological, and perpetually arguing with itself through networks of hookah dens and drinking clubs that define political and social life as much as formal institutions do.

## Geography and Structure

The Teph River carved Glen Canyon through salmon-colored sandstone over geological time measured in Records—hundreds? thousands?—leaving walls rising 500 feet on either side. The river flows south through the lower canyon where Ganat proper sits, then continues through increasingly dramatic gorges toward distant desert. Behind the Dam, the river backs up into Lake Ganat in the upper canyon—a reservoir created when the Dam was built, long ago in some forgotten Record, by builders whose purposes remain unknown.

The city exists in three connected but distinct zones: the Near Wall, the Far Wall, and the Dam between them. You cannot easily cross from one side to the other—the river is fast, deep, dangerous. You cross via the Tyrant's Walk (formerly Ulayah's Walk before the Revolution), a catwalk through the Dam itself that passes above the industrial district, or by walking over the top of the Dam where it rises 400 feet above the river.

### The Sandstone

The canyon walls are Navajo sandstone: salmon to cream colored, fine-grained, soft enough to carve with hand tools but durable enough to stand for Records once carved. The stone formed from ancient desert dunes, compressed and cemented over deep time. You can see the original dune structure in the walls—sweeping diagonal lines called cross-bedding, showing which way wind blew in a desert that existed before humans, before the Interdict, before civilization.

The sandstone carves beautifully. You can create smooth curves, flowing organic forms, elaborate decoration with patience and sharp tools. This is why Ganat looks like it does—not just cave-homes and tunnels but art, whole facades carved with flowing Art Nouveau curves and figurative scenes, relief work showing revolutionary ideals or Penitent theology or just decorative beauty for its own sake.

The stone weathers in patterns. Water seeping through creates dark streaks—desert varnish, iron and manganese oxides deposited on the surface. Some walls show centuries of weathering, streaks running down like permanent rain. Other sections are freshly carved, bright salmon revealing where workers recently expanded tunnels or cut new cave-homes.

The canyon itself creates specific climate. The walls provide shade for much of the day. Air moving through the canyon creates breeze even when surrounding desert is still. In summer when the high desert bakes, the canyon is 10-15 degrees cooler. In winter when the desert freezes, the canyon provides shelter from wind. The river moderates temperature further. This is why humans built here—the canyon makes survival possible where open desert would kill.

### The Dam

The Dam spans the canyon at its narrowest point—maybe 300 feet across, rising 400 feet from river level to its top. The structure is ancient, built from materials that look like stone but aren't quite—grey substance harder than granite, smooth to the touch, impervious to weathering. The builders' purposes are unknown. Perhaps they wanted to store water. Perhaps they wanted power generation. Perhaps this was infrastructure for something else entirely and current residents simply adapted whatever they found.

When the Dam was rediscovered—how many Records ago, no one knows precisely; the documentation is fragmentary and confusing—its turbines had long broken. The structure stood but generated nothing. The lake behind it existed but served mainly fishing and irrigation. For Records the Dam was just enormous ruin, impressive but non-functional.

Then, in the decades before the Revolution—40 years ago? 50?—Ganati engineers broke out the broken archaeotech turbines. They studied the spaces where turbines had been, measured the water flow, calculated pressure and volume. They designed new turbines based on principles reverse-engineered from studying other archaeotech, built them from materials they could source or manufacture, installed them in spaces designed for different technology entirely. They cut new spillways through the ancient material (difficult work—the grey pseudo-stone resists cutting). They ran new transmission lines. They created power generation infrastructure of modern design occupying ancient structure.

The turbines work. The power flows. The quantity is enormous—the lake behind the Dam is large, the head is high, the water flow is controlled and constant. The Dam produces more electricity than Ganat can consume, though the city tries hard to consume it all.

### The Industrial District

Much of this industry is inside the Dam itself. The ancient structure is massive—hollow in places, containing spaces whose original purposes are mysterious. The revolutionary engineers installing turbines discovered rooms, tunnels, vertical shafts, horizontal galleries. Some of these spaces now contain turbines and generators. Others became workshops, factories, storage areas.

The logic is simple: the shorter the transmission line, the cheaper the hookup, the less power lost to resistance. If your workshop is inside the Dam, ten feet from the generators, you pay almost nothing for connection and the power arrives at full strength. This created immediate incentive to move industry inside the Dam itself.

So now the Dam contains: turbine rooms where generators spin constantly, producing low thrum that's audible throughout the structure; metalworking shops forging, casting, machining; textile mills with electric looms running 24 hours; carpentry workshops with powered saws; chemical processing facilities; assembly plants; storage warehouses; and connecting all of this, tunnels and catwalks and vertical shafts with ladders or occasionally pre-Interdict elevators that still function mysteriously.

The working-class neighborhoods emerged organically. Workers needed to live near their jobs. The Dam has vast interior space. The sandstone around the Dam can be carved relatively easily. So workers started cutting tunnels through the canyon walls that terminate inside the Dam itself. A tunnel might run 200 feet through solid sandstone, branching occasionally into chambers carved for living quarters, then opening into the Dam's interior where a workshop or factory provides employment.

These neighborhoods are warren-like: narrow tunnels, small chambers carved off main passages, vertical connections between levels, lighting provided by electric bulbs running off power too cheap to meter. A worker might live in a carved chamber 12 feet by 8 feet, shared with family, lit continuously because there's no natural light and power is essentially free. The chamber connects to a tunnel that connects to other tunnels that eventually terminate in the Dam where the worker's job is.

The surface might be a half-hour's walk through brightly-lit sandstone corridors. You navigate by landmarks: "Turn left at the broken window," "Go down three levels at the shaft with the blue marking," "Follow the tunnel with the water pipes." The warrens are comprehensible to residents and completely disorienting to visitors. There's no map because the tunnels expand constantly—whenever someone needs more space, they carve it if they can, paying for the work themselves or organizing kilit-based collective excavation.

The Dam runs continuously. The turbines never stop. The workshops operate in shifts—24 hours of activity because power is abundant and free and stopping machines costs opportunity. At any hour, the Dam thrums with work: hammers on metal, electric saws, looms clacking, voices echoing through corridors, the constant background sound of turbines spinning and water flowing. The smell is industrial: hot metal, chemicals, saw dust, human sweat, machine oil, ozone from electrical sparks.

### The Near Wall

The Near Wall is the canyon side closest to the main road entering Ganat—the route most travelers use arriving from the eastern settlements or from points north. This wall hosts markets, commerce, and wealthy residential areas. The logic is simple: if you control access to trade, you become wealthy. If you become wealthy, you build where prestige and convenience intersect.

The lower levels of the Near Wall—closest to the river—are markets. Carved spaces opening onto streets that run along the canyon floor, creating covered bazaars where merchants display goods: food from upper canyon farms, textiles from Dam workshops, copper work from local artisans, imported goods from Imperial trade via Taho, salvaged archaeotech and reverse-engineered products. The markets are crowded, noisy, fragrant with food and spices and human density. Electric lights illuminate the covered markets constantly—important because they're carved into the cliff and would be dark otherwise.

Higher up the Near Wall, residential areas. The genuinely wealthy have cave-homes carved close enough to the canyon wall that they can install vast stained glass windows—massive panels showing geometric patterns or figurative scenes, lit from behind by electric light so they glow both day and night. These homes might be 2,000-3,000 square feet carved into sandstone: multiple chambers, high ceilings, carved decoration showing Art Nouveau curves and revolutionary or Penitent imagery. The stained glass windows face the canyon, visible from the Far Wall and from below, announcing wealth through beauty that costs enormous amounts to commission and install.

Between the wealthy cave-homes with windows and the market level: middle-class residences carved deeper into the sandstone. These homes don't have windows—they're too far from the canyon face—but they have electric lighting, carved decoration (simpler than wealthy homes but still present), shared access to water systems and sewage infrastructure. A middle-class family might occupy three or four connected chambers carved in sequence: sleeping areas, cooking area, gathering space.

The poorer you are, the deeper you live. The deepest residential tunnels are narrow, minimally decorated, farthest from both the canyon face and from the markets and main thoroughfares. But even these have electric lighting and sanitation infrastructure. Nobody in Ganat proper lives in darkness or without sewage systems that prevent disease. The Dam provides power for everyone. The ancient infrastructure—or modern additions to it—handles waste for everyone. The baseline standard of living is higher than anywhere else in the Republic because the city's fundamental resources are abundant rather than scarce.

Terraces carved into the Near Wall provide outdoor space: gardens requiring constant irrigation, gathering areas where residents socialize, lookout points showing the canyon below and the Far Wall opposite. The terraces are connected by staircases carved into the sandstone, some of them ancient and weathered, others obviously recent and sharply cut. The stairs are lit at night by electric bulbs every twenty feet, making navigation safe and demonstrating the abundance that Ganat enjoys.

### The Far Wall

The Far Wall—opposite side of the canyon—hosts government, bureaucracy, and the transformed wealth of the Hereditary Debtors. This is where political power concentrates, where the Senate meets, where ministries maintain offices, where the Cathedral stands.

The lower levels contain bureaucratic warrens: endless tunnels carved through sandstone connecting chambers that serve as offices, meeting rooms, archive storage, administrative spaces. The warrens are notorious for being confusing—deliberate complexity or organic growth, depending on who you ask. A petitioner seeking administrative approval might spend hours navigating tunnels trying to find the correct office, asking directions from bureaucrats who may or may not help, eventually locating the chamber where some official can stamp the necessary paperwork.

The warrens are lit aggressively—electric bulbs every few feet, creating bright environment that makes the bureaucracy feel exposed and efficient even when it's neither. The lighting is Dawn Party aesthetic: transparency through illumination, nothing hidden in darkness, everything visible to collective scrutiny. The reality is that the bureaucracy is just as capable of hiding things in bright light as in darkness, but the aesthetic matters politically.

The Senate chamber sits mid-level in the Far Wall: a vast space carved from sandstone, 150 feet across, tiered seating for 750 senators arranged in concentric circles facing a central floor. The acoustics were designed for a smaller body—perhaps 200, back when the kilit-zem-kilitam advised the Sultana. Now 750 voices overwhelm them. Sessions dissolve into competing chants, coordinated heckling, demonstrations of factional strength through sheer volume. The presiding officer's bell becomes inaudible. Factions cluster by section: Dawn dominating the eastern tiers, Dusk concentrated westward, swing constituencies scattered between.

The chamber is decorated with carved reliefs showing revolutionary scenes: collective assemblies, workers building infrastructure, soldiers fighting together, citizens deliberating. The reliefs are deep-carved—3-4 inches into the sandstone—suggesting they replaced earlier imagery. If you look closely at certain sections, you can see hints of what came before: smoother stone underneath, faint outlines of different compositions. The revolutionary imagery was carved over monarchical scenes, removing Ulayah's authority and installing collective governance in its physical place.

Higher up the Far Wall: the kilit headquarters of the Hereditary Debtors, occupying what were once slave quarters. The Hereditary Debtors are descendants of slaves—people whose ancestors owed debts under the Sultanate that became hereditary obligations, binding children to repay their parents' debts, creating permanent underclass. The Revolution abolished hereditary debt and granted full citizenship to the debtors. The former slave quarters—carved tunnels and chambers in the Far Wall—became kilit property, owned collectively by the people who once lived there in bondage.

The transformation is visible. The quarters were originally minimal: narrow tunnels, small chambers, basic sanitation. Over twenty years, the Hereditary Debtors have expanded, improved, decorated. They carved larger chambers, connected tunnels more efficiently, installed better lighting and plumbing, added decoration that announces their transformation from slave quarters to wealthy modern residences. The work is ongoing—you see sections in transition, where workers are actively carving new spaces or installing new infrastructure.

The Hereditary Debtors' sudden wealth comes from position more than economic transformation. They own property in the Far Wall adjacent to government warrens and Senate chambers. Property values in this location are high because proximity to power creates opportunity. The former slaves now lease space to bureaucrats, rent offices to kilits needing government access, operate restaurants and lodging for visiting senators and administrators. They didn't become wealthy through commerce or industry—they became wealthy through collective property ownership in a location that became valuable when the Revolution made government accessible.

### The Cathedral

Atop the Far Wall sits the largest free-standing structure in Ganat: what was once Sultana Ulayah's palace, now the cathedral occupied by Patriarch Kaleb. The building is massive: 300 feet long, 150 feet wide, four stories tall, built from sandstone blocks quarried elsewhere and assembled on the canyon rim. It sits at the edge, partially overhanging the cliff, its western face visible from across the canyon—a statement of power that worked equally well for monarchy and now serves religion and revolutionary broadcasting.

The architecture combines Sultanate grandeur with revolutionary adaptation. The exterior shows carved decoration: geometric patterns, figurative scenes, flowing Art Nouveau curves carved into salmon sandstone. Some of this carving is original—Sultanate period showing monarchy's authority and wealth. Some is obviously newer—revolutionary scenes carved deeper where they replaced monarchical imagery, removing Ulayah and installing collective ideals.

Inside, only the reception chambers and Ulayah's private apartments are given over to the Church. The rest houses infrastructure that would have seemed impossible twenty years ago. The transmission facility that once broadcast only the Common Plea—the weekly religious service required under the Sultanate—has been expanded and given over to multiple organizations:

The party bureaucracies of both Dawn and Dusk maintain offices here, using the transmission capacity to broadcast political programming across the Republic. Dawn programming emphasizes revolutionary solidarity, collective capacity-building, preparation for eventual confrontation with the Empire. Dusk programming emphasizes commercial opportunity, pragmatic governance, avoiding unnecessary conflict. Both parties use the same transmission towers, scheduled at different times, creating constant negotiation over airtime and content.

The Ministry of Information occupies substantial space in the old palace. This ministry is so dominated by Dawn Party ideologues that everyone jokes about it—including Dusk administrators who work there and maintain they're providing necessary balance while everyone else knows they're outnumbered and ignored. The Ministry produces official statistics, publishes government reports, manages public information campaigns, and generally tries to shape what citizens know about their government and world. It's propaganda, but sophisticated propaganda that maintains documentary accuracy while controlling narrative framing.

The Ministry of Internal Affairs shares space here too—the blandly titled organization responsible for internal security, counter-intelligence, monitoring political dissent. It's run by an uncomfortable mixture of former Sultanate-era secret police who had no ideological attachment to Ulayah's rule (they served monarchy because it paid, they serve the Republic because it pays better) and Dawn-era ideologues who believe in revolutionary transformation and watch the former secret police carefully while depending on their expertise. The Ministry operates in brightly-lit offices because Dawn aesthetic requires transparency, but everyone understands that what actually happens in those offices is carefully hidden despite the lighting.

The Cathedral itself—the religious portions occupied by Patriarch Kaleb—uses the former reception halls for services and the private apartments for administration. The reception halls are enormous: vaulted ceilings 40 feet high, carved columns supporting weight, space for congregations of 500 or more. The walls show carved Penitent imagery: humanity's transgression, the Interdict, hope for redemption through works. Some of this is original Sultanate carving. Some is revolutionary additions showing more active theology—humans building rather than waiting, demonstrating capacity rather than accepting punishment.

Kaleb maintains modest personal quarters in what were Ulayah's private chambers—several rooms carved with elaborate decoration that he hasn't removed, explaining that the stone remembers history and covering it serves nothing. His office overlooks the canyon from the western edge of the building, providing views of the Near Wall, the Dam, the lower canyon stretching south. He can see the entire city from there—the physical manifestation of revolutionary governance that replaced the monarchy he helped overthrow.

### Crossing Between Walls

You cannot easily cross from Near Wall to Far Wall. The river is fast, deep, dangerous. Swimming is suicide. Boats exist but are expensive and slow and require coordination. For most people, crossing means using one of two routes:

The Tyrant's Walk (formerly Ulayah's Walk—renamed after the Revolution but people still use both names) is a catwalk running through the Dam itself, above the industrial district. The catwalk is metal gridwork suspended from the Dam's interior structure, maybe four feet wide, with railings on both sides. It passes through the Dam at a level 150 feet above the river, providing views down into the industrial spaces where workers operate machinery and move through tunnels.

Walking the Tyrant's Walk takes maybe 15 minutes to cross the 300 feet of the Dam's width. The sound is overwhelming: turbines thrumming, machines operating, voices echoing, water flowing. The smell is industrial: hot metal, machine oil, human sweat, chemicals. You see the entire operation as you cross—the workshops, the workers, the power generation that makes everything else possible. The catwalk is lit continuously but it's still intimidating—metal gridwork with gaps showing the drop below, vibration from machinery transmitting through the structure, the awareness that thousands of people work in the space you're walking through.

The alternative is walking over the top of the Dam—the surface 400 feet above the river. This route is wider, safer, quieter. The top of the Dam is maybe 50 feet wide, flat, running from Near Wall to Far Wall. It's used for moving cargo between walls, for people who prefer open air to the industrial warren, for tourists who want to see the view. Looking upstream shows Lake Ganat stretching into the upper canyon. Looking downstream shows the lower canyon and the city carved into its walls. Looking down shows the river far below and occasionally glimpses of industrial activity through openings in the Dam's structure.

Both routes are free—no tolls, no restrictions. Crossing between walls is fundamental right in a city that's divided by geography but politically unified. The government maintains both routes obsessively. Maintenance workers check the Tyrant's Walk weekly for structural issues. The top of the Dam is cleared of snow in winter and inspected constantly for cracks or problems. Making sure people can cross freely is practical necessity and political statement: we're one city despite the canyon dividing us, one polity despite our fractiousness, one Republic despite our arguments.

## Power and Industry

The Dam provides power too cheap to meter. This phrase isn't rhetoric—it's literal fact. The cost of generating electricity from the Dam's turbines is essentially zero once the infrastructure is installed. Water flows continuously from the lake. Gravity provides the pressure. The turbines spin without fuel or human intervention. Maintenance costs exist but they're minimal compared to power output.

Getting an electric hookup is cheap: maybe 50 obols for the connection, which includes running wire from the nearest distribution point to your workshop or home and installing a cutoff switch. Then the power flows. You pay nothing for usage because metering would cost more than the power consumed. For the vast majority of users—residential, small workshops, standard commercial operations—electricity is free after the initial hookup fee.

(Very large industrial operations pay nominal fees based on estimated consumption, mainly to prevent complete abuse—someone running 50 electric arc furnaces simultaneously would strain even the Dam's capacity. But for normal use, the power is unlimited and unmetered.)

This abundance has created economic transformation unique in the Republic. Everywhere else, electricity is expensive, rationed, carefully managed. In Taho, merchant kilits provide subsidized power as competitive philanthropy. In Ganat, the state provides free power as fundamental infrastructure. This changes what's possible.

### The Industrial Explosion

Workshops that elsewhere would run single shifts because power costs too much run 24 hours in Ganat. Machines that elsewhere would be used selectively run continuously. Manufacturing that elsewhere requires careful planning to minimize power consumption happens freely, experimentally, without constraint.

This has created: Metalworking shops operating electric arc furnaces constantly, producing steel and specialized alloys for Republican use and export. Textile mills with hundreds of electric looms running simultaneously, producing fabric that supplies the entire Republic and competes with Imperial manufactures. Carpentry workshops with powered saws, lathes, planers, producing furniture and structural timber products. Chemical plants running electrolysis operations, producing substances that require enormous power input. Machine shops producing tools, components, precision instruments. Assembly plants where workers combine components into finished products—weapons, tools, household goods.

Much of this industry is inside the Dam where hookup costs essentially zero. But industry also spread throughout both walls, carved into sandstone wherever space permits. A workshop might be 30 feet into the Near Wall, carved from solid sandstone, lit by electric bulbs, running machinery powered by wire running through tunnels from the Dam. The workshop operates continuously because power is free and stopping costs opportunity.

The transmission lines spreading outward from Ganat are visible from distance: spiderweb of copper wire on wooden poles, running along roads, climbing canyon walls, extending toward settlements beyond. The lines are expanding constantly—as soon as a settlement gets connected, someone proposes extending to the next settlement further out. The limiting factor isn't power generation but transmission distance. Eventually resistance in the wire costs more than the power transmitted. Currently that happens around 30 miles from Ganat. Engineers are experimenting with higher voltages and thicker wire to extend the range, but it's slow work requiring careful testing.

Still, the electrification creeps outward. A settlement 15 miles from Ganat that had no electricity five years ago now has lights and electric workshops. A farming village 25 miles out just got connected last year. The transformation happens one settlement at a time: darkness giving way to continuous light, manual labor augmented by electric machines, hours of productivity extending into night because lighting permits it.

But the majority of power produced in Ganat must be consumed in Ganat—transmission losses mean you can't export it far. This creates pressure to use the power locally, which drives industrial development, which attracts workers, which increases population, which requires more housing carved into sandstone, which creates the perpetually expanding warrens that characterize the city.

### The Working-Class Warrens

The working-class neighborhoods carved through sandstone and terminating in the Dam represent revolutionary urbanism that wasn't planned but emerged organically from material conditions. Workers needed housing near employment. The Dam provided employment. The sandstone could be carved. Power and lighting were free. So workers carved homes.

A typical warren might be: A main tunnel 8 feet high, 6 feet wide, running 150 feet through sandstone from the Dam into the cliff. Every 20 feet, a chamber carved off to the side: 12 feet by 10 feet, 7 feet high, home to a family of four or five. The chamber has electric lighting—bulbs wired into ceiling, powered from lines running through the tunnel, essentially free to operate continuously. The chamber has carved shelves, sleeping platforms, possibly a small window if it's close enough to the tunnel opening but more likely just ventilation shaft cut vertically to reach surface 100 feet above.

Multiple families share the tunnel. Everyone knows everyone. Children play in the tunnel because there's no other space. Cooking happens in communal areas carved larger to accommodate multiple families. Sanitation runs through pipes installed in the tunnel—water flows in, sewage flows out, the infrastructure maintained collectively or by kilit organization depending on who carved which warren.

The warrens are bright. This is important—they could be dark caves but they're not because power is free and lighting is simple. Electric bulbs every 10 feet mean the tunnels are well-lit constantly. You navigate warren-space in brightness that makes the sandstone's salmon color visible, shows the carved decoration some residents add to their chambers, creates environment that's underground but doesn't feel oppressive because visibility is good.

The warrens connect. A tunnel from the Dam branches. One branch goes to residential warrens. Another goes to different workshop. A vertical shaft connects levels. Someone carved a shortcut between two previously separate tunnel systems. The result is three-dimensional maze that's comprehensible to residents and completely disorienting to visitors. There's no comprehensive map—nobody knows the full extent of the carved spaces because expansion happens constantly without central coordination.

Navigation relies on landmarks and memory. "Turn left at the blue mark," "Go down two levels at the shaft near the weaving collective," "Follow the tunnel that smells like metal polishing—my chamber is the fourth on the right." Residents learn the territory through daily use. Visitors get lost immediately and require guides. The government tried installing signs but signs disappear or get moved or become obsolete as tunnels change. The warrens are alive—growing, shifting, adapting to resident needs rather than administrative planning.

The surface might be a half-hour walk through brightly-lit corridors from your chamber in the warren to open air on the canyon rim. Some residents go weeks without seeing the surface—work in the Dam, live in the warren, socialize in carved communal spaces, never need to leave the tunnel system. Others emerge daily to market on the canyon floor or to work in surface-level workshops or just to see sky and feel wind.

## Political and Social Life

Ganat proper is the Republic's capital and ideological heart. The Senate meets here. The Consuls maintain offices here. The ministries are based here. Patriarch Kaleb broadcasts from here. This is where revolutionary governance happens, where the contradictions between ideology and practice become most visible, where the fractiousness of Republican politics plays out through formal institutions and informal networks.

### The Senate and Governance

The Senate chamber carved into the Far Wall is vast—one hundred fifty feet across, tiered seating rising in concentric rings, space for 750 senators representing kilits across the Republic: agricultural collectives, pastoral bands, merchant enterprises, labor organizations, professional guilds, veterans groups. Each kilit above 8,000 members gets a Senator. Smaller kilits form coalitions for representation. The result is 750 senators representing perhaps 500 kilits serving six million people.

This is not a deliberative body in the classical sense. It is a parliament, a mass assembly, a permanent negotiation made architectural. The chamber's size defeats intimacy—senators cannot have quiet conversations across the floor. They shout. They heckle. Factions cluster in sections, Dawn dominating the eastern tiers, Dusk concentrated westward, swing constituencies scattered between. The acoustics were designed for a smaller body; 750 voices overwhelm them. Sessions often dissolve into competing chants, coordinated interruptions, demonstrations of strength through volume. The former observation galleries—where spectators once watched the kilit-zem-kilitam advise the Sultana—now hold backbenchers. Condensation drips from the ceiling despite the dehumidifiers installed to prevent it; 750 bodies in carved stone produce moisture the ventilation cannot handle.

Dawn holds roughly 450 seats, Dusk roughly 300. But party discipline is weak because kilits choose senators based on economic interests that transcend partisan loyalty. A Dawn-affiliated agricultural kilit might vote with Dusk merchants on water rights. A Dusk-affiliated professional guild might vote with Dawn veterans on expansion policy. The 60% threshold—exactly 450 votes—means significant legislation requires either perfect Dawn unity or cross-party coalition. Neither is easy to achieve.

The Senate's fractiousness is legendary. Major debates fill every seat, senators crowding the aisles, voices rising until the presiding officer's bell becomes inaudible. Minor business proceeds with half the chamber empty, senators meeting in the corridor warrens where actual deals get made. The formal sessions ratify what informal negotiation has already determined—but the ratification itself is theater, factional demonstration, performance of political identity for constituents who will read about it in tomorrow's papers.

The bureaucracy surrounding the Senate is vast: the ministry warrens carved through the Far Wall, endless offices staffed by administrators processing legislation, managing records, coordinating between kilits and government, implementing decisions the Senate makes when it manages to make them. The bureaucracy is theoretically neutral but actually heavily Dawn-influenced because revolutionary governance requires revolutionary bureaucrats to staff it. Dusk complaints about partisan administration are constant and largely correct, but Dusk can't change this without purging the people who make government function.

### The Ministry Warrens

The bureaucratic warrens in the Far Wall are deliberately confusing—though whether this is intentional opacity or organic growth is debated. The tunnels connect offices, meeting rooms, archive storage in patterns that seem logical to no one except long-term employees who've learned the territory. A petitioner seeking some administrative approval navigates by asking directions: "Where's the Office of Agricultural Subsidies?" "Third level, follow the main tunnel past the ventilation shaft, turn right at the chamber with the painted blue door, it's the seventh office on the left."

The warrens are aggressively lit—Dawn Party aesthetic requiring transparency through illumination. Every tunnel has electric bulbs every few feet. Every office is bright. The lighting is supposed to prevent corruption through visibility: if everything is bright and open, wrongdoing is harder. The reality is that bureaucratic complexity creates opacity regardless of lighting. Someone who knows which office processes which forms can manipulate the system just as effectively in bright light as in darkness.

The Ministry of Information occupies substantial space: offices producing official statistics, reports, propaganda, public information campaigns. The Ministry is dominated by Dawn ideologues who believe in revolutionary transformation and understand that controlling information shapes political reality. They publish accurate statistics when accuracy serves revolutionary goals, emphasize certain facts while downplaying others, frame information to support Dawn's agenda while maintaining they're just presenting neutral facts.

Dusk complains constantly that the Ministry is partisan propaganda organ. Dawn claims it's necessary information management preventing the chaos of unrestricted information flow. Both are partly right. The Ministry does shape information to serve political goals, but those goals include maintaining social stability and revolutionary legitimacy that everyone benefits from—even Dusk, even when they're complaining about it.

The Ministry of Internal Affairs operates in adjacent warrens—the blandly titled security organization responsible for internal threats, counter-intelligence, monitoring political dissent that might threaten the Republic. It's staffed by strange combination: former Sultanate secret police who switched sides (or rather, never had sides and just serve whoever governs) and Dawn ideologues who believe in revolutionary purity and watch the former secret police constantly.

This mixture creates tension. The former secret police are competent at surveillance, infiltration, information gathering—skills learned serving monarchy and transferred to serving republic. The Dawn ideologues are loyal but often incompetent at practical security work. Neither trusts the other. The former police know the ideologues watch them for monarchical sympathies they don't actually have. The ideologues know the former police are cynical professionals who serve for pay rather than revolutionary commitment.

The Ministry operates in bright offices because Dawn aesthetic requires it, but everyone understands that actual security work happens in darkness regardless of how many electric lights illuminate the administrative spaces. The reports are written in lit offices. The interrogations happen elsewhere. The surveillance occurs in shadows. The lighting is performance of transparency masking necessary opacity.

### Hookah Dens and Salon Culture

Before the Revolution, cannabis and alcohol were banned in Ganat proper but available elsewhere in the Sultanate. The ban was ubiquitous—official policy, seriously enforced with penalties including imprisonment—and utterly ignored by middle and upper classes who wanted to drink and smoke and socialize. This created underground culture: networks of private establishments where people gathered to smoke hookah, drink alcohol, discuss politics, literature, theology, and generally engage in social life that required pretending you weren't breaking laws everyone knew you were breaking.

The Revolution legalized cannabis and alcohol in Ganat proper. But the culture that developed during prohibition didn't disappear—it transformed. The hookah dens and drinking clubs that were underground became semi-public, but they retained their character as invitation-only establishments with ideological or social affiliations. You don't just walk into a hookah den and expect to be served. You're invited by existing members. You're vouched for. You demonstrate you belong through connections, through political alignment, through shared interests.

Almost every middle-class family belongs, through kilit membership or social networks, to overlapping networks of restaurants, drinking clubs, hookah dens, reading rooms—establishments that cater to particular crowds. Some are Dawn-affiliated: revolutionary ideologues smoking cannabis and debating expansion policy, drinking tea or weak beer, discussing how to build collective capacity. Others are Dusk-affiliated: merchants and professionals drinking stronger alcohol, smoking tobacco or cannabis, discussing trade opportunities and how to resist Dawn's more radical proposals.

Some establishments are religious: Orthodox Penitents gathering to discuss theology while smoking hookah, radicals debating whether Patriarch Kaleb is too moderate or appropriately gradualist, conservatives worried that the Church is becoming political propaganda organ. Some are artistic: writers, painters, musicians gathering to share work and critique each other while drinking and smoking. Some are professional: engineers discussing technical problems, lawyers debating constitutional questions, administrators comparing bureaucratic war stories.

The establishments vary in sophistication and cost. Wealthy merchant clubs serve imported alcohol, high-quality cannabis, elaborate food. Working-class drinking halls serve grain alcohol, weak beer, simple food, provide space for laborers to socialize after shifts. Middle-class hookah dens fall between: decent cannabis, local wine or beer, conversation-focused rather than luxury-focused.

Though you can purchase street food, pulque and weak beer at the market—casual consumption available to everyone—much of the city's entertainment culture is by invitation only. This creates parallel social networks: formal political structures (kilits, Senate, ministries) and informal social structures (hookah dens, drinking clubs, salon gatherings) where actual political formation happens through conversation, debate, consensus-building among people who trust each other enough to speak candidly.

The government officially supports this because it's preferable to underground organization that breeds conspiracy. Better to have people gathering in establishments where government can monitor them loosely (Ministry of Internal Affairs occasionally infiltrates or observes) than in secret locations where plotting might occur undetected. The Dawn Party even encourages establishment of party-affiliated hookah dens as organizing centers—places where ideology is discussed, candidates are evaluated, positions are coordinated.

The result is that Ganati public life is notoriously fractious but also notoriously well-organized. Everyone argues with everyone constantly—in Senate, in ministry warrens, in hookah dens. But the arguments happen through established social networks that prevent complete fragmentation. You might disagree with someone's position but you know them from the hookah den where you both smoke cannabis and debate politics every few weeks. That personal connection moderates political antagonism, makes coalition-building possible despite ideological differences, creates the social fabric that holds together a fractious revolutionary polity.

## The Upper Canyon

Above the Dam, the Teph River has been transformed into Lake Ganat—the reservoir created when the Dam was built long ago in some forgotten Record. The lake stretches maybe 30 miles upstream through the upper canyon, creating vast water storage that buffers seasonal variation and enables controlled power generation year-round.

The farming and fishing villages around Lake Ganat and in the upper canyon form the wealthiest agricultural areas of the Republic. The explanation is simple: fertilizer and irrigation. The upper canyon has poor soil naturally—desert conditions, minimal organic matter, harsh drainage. But the city of Ganat has a large population and effective sewer systems. For Records—hundreds? thousands? nobody knows exactly—sewage and organic waste from the city has been processed and distributed to the upper canyon as fertilizer.

The scale is enormous. Half a million people produce waste continuously. The city's sanitation infrastructure—ancient pipes integrated with modern additions—collects sewage, processes it in treatment areas, concentrates the nutrients, ships it upstream as fertilizer. Over Records, this transforms soil from desert poverty to agricultural productivity. The fields around Lake Ganat are dark, rich, fertile—not naturally but through millennia of systematic fertilization using city waste.

The irrigation network extends from Lake Ganat through the upper canyon using combination of ancient infrastructure and modern additions. Water flows through canals, pipes, channels, delivering consistent moisture to fields that otherwise would only support scrub vegetation. The combination of fertilization and irrigation makes the upper canyon capable of supporting intensive agriculture that feeds Ganat proper and produces surplus for trade.

The crops themselves are bioengineered—Serrulata-associated cultivars designed for whatever aborted colonization program the Serrulata were engineered for. These plants tolerate desert conditions, use water efficiently, produce good yields with proper fertilization, grow in soil that would defeat baseline crops. The genetic engineering is ancient—nobody knows how it was done or could reproduce it—but the plants reproduce true and adapt slowly to local conditions.

The result is agricultural kilits around Lake Ganat that are wealthy in ways agricultural communities elsewhere aren't. They produce reliable surplus. They sell to Ganat proper at good prices (city needs food, farmers have captive market). They've invested in infrastructure: better irrigation, storage facilities, processing equipment. Their wealth is visible in their settlements: well-maintained structures, electric lighting powered by transmission lines from Ganat, amenities that frontier agricultural communities lack.

The lake itself supports fishing: species introduced long ago that thrive in the reservoir, providing protein for Ganat and the upper canyon villages. The fish are descendant from Earth species but adapted to this environment over countless Records. Some are familiar: catfish, carp, tilapia-equivalents. Some are stranger: engineered species whose origins are unknown but whose flesh is edible and nutritious.

The relationship between Ganat proper and the upper canyon is symbiotic but tense. The city provides fertilizer, electrical infrastructure, markets for agricultural products, political representation through the kilit system. The agricultural communities provide food, raw materials, surplus that funds city operations. But the farmers resent being treated as secondary to the city's industrial importance. The city residents think of farmers as conservative, resistant to change, overly concerned with water rights and land tenure. Both are partly right and neither side is satisfied with the arrangement.

## The Cathedral and Broadcasting

The Cathedral—former palace of Sultana Ulayah—dominates the Far Wall's skyline. The building is massive, visible from anywhere in the lower canyon, announcing religious and political authority through its position and scale. The western face, which overhangs the cliff, shows carved decoration lit by electric lights at night: revolutionary scenes, Penitent imagery, geometric patterns, all glowing in the darkness.

Inside, Patriarch Kaleb maintains modest personal quarters and uses the former reception halls for services. The halls are vast: vaulted ceilings, carved columns, space for congregations of 500 or more during major feast days. The Common Plea is broadcast from here weekly: Kaleb's sermon reaching across the Republic through the transmission towers that crown the building.

But most of the palace is given over to other purposes. The transmission facility that once broadcast only religious programming now serves multiple organizations competing for airtime:

Dawn Party programming emphasizes revolutionary solidarity: addresses from the Dawn Consul, discussions of expansion policy, educational content about collective capacity-building, inspirational stories from eastern settlements demonstrating what dedication achieves, warnings about Imperial influence and need for vigilance. The programming is sophisticated—it's not crude propaganda but carefully crafted content that maintains revolutionary enthusiasm while addressing practical concerns.

Dusk Party programming emphasizes commercial opportunity and stable governance: the Dusk Consul discussing trade policy, merchants explaining how commerce benefits everyone, technical content about improved agricultural techniques or manufacturing processes, cautious warnings about Dawn's more aggressive expansion proposals creating conflict the Republic isn't ready for. The programming is less ideologically consistent than Dawn's—Dusk is coalition of interests rather than unified ideology—but it's still carefully produced to counter Dawn's narrative dominance.

The Ministry of Information produces official content: statistics about agricultural production, reports on infrastructure development, public health announcements, educational programming for children in settlements without schools. The content is nominally neutral but everyone knows the Ministry is Dawn-dominated, so even neutral-seeming content carries ideological framing that serves revolutionary goals.

The transmission towers can reach across most of the Republic when atmospheric conditions permit. In Taho, people hear broadcasts from Ganat. In the eastern settlements, farmers gather around radios to hear the Common Plea and political programming. In Ghemar-yan, the transmitter-dish re-broadcasts Ganat's programming to the northern territories. The content shapes Republican political culture: what people know, what they care about, how they understand the world.

The competition for airtime is constant. Dawn has more slots—they control the Ministry of Information and have political pressure to dominate messaging. Dusk fights for equal representation, claiming the broadcasting monopoly violates consular dyarchy principles. Kaleb mediates when conflicts arise, maintaining that religious broadcasting takes precedence and everyone else shares what remains. This creates three-way negotiation where nobody is fully satisfied but arrangements persist because alternatives would be worse.

Kaleb's own position is complicated. He's revolutionary Orthodox Penitent who believes in the Plea and demonstrating wisdom through works. He transformed Penitent theology from passive to active, creating ideological foundation for the Republic's existence. But he's also uncomfortable with how politicized the Church has become—the Cathedral is now center of political broadcasting, the transmission towers serve parties as much as religion, his own sermons are analyzed for political implications rather than theological content.

He maintains this is necessary compromise. The alternative—religious broadcasting isolated from political reality—would make the Church irrelevant. Better to engage politics directly, provide theological framework for governance, use the Church's authority to moderate political extremism. But privately he wonders whether the Church is guiding the Republic or being consumed by it. The question is unanswerable so he continues the work, preaching each week, mediating conflicts, maintaining that works-based atonement justifies everything if the works actually serve collective capacity.

## Revolutionary Aesthetics

Ganat proper demonstrates revolutionary aesthetics more clearly than anywhere else in the Republic. The city's appearance—carved sandstone, electric lighting, Art Nouveau decoration combined with geometric revolutionary imagery—reflects intentional choices about how revolutionary society should look.

The sandstone carving follows Art Nouveau principles: flowing organic curves, nature-inspired forms, figurative art showing humans and plants and animals in dynamic composition. This aesthetic emerged before the Revolution during the late Sultanate period, when Ganati artisans developed distinctive style combining desert influences with imported techniques. The Revolution embraced this aesthetic as revolutionary rather than monarchical because it emphasized organic growth, natural forms, human labor made visible through craftsmanship.

Revolutionary imagery added to existing structures is carved deeper—2-4 inches into the sandstone—where it replaces monarchical scenes. A facade that originally showed Sultana Ulayah receiving tribute now shows collective assembly distributing resources. The monarchical carving was removed, the revolutionary carving created in its place, and the depth difference shows this transformation physically. The stone remembers what came before even when people prefer to forget.

New construction built after the Revolution shows distinctly revolutionary style: less decorative carving than Sultanate period (claimed as rejection of aristocratic excess, actually reflecting reduced budgets and faster construction), more geometric forms, emphasis on function over decoration, but still carved with care showing human labor and craftsmanship matter. A new tunnel might have simpler decoration than older sections, but it's still decorated because blank utilitarian tunnels feel Imperial or colonial or wrong somehow.

The lighting is revolutionary aesthetic made practical. Every tunnel lit continuously demonstrates abundance—we have so much power we can illuminate everything all the time. Every street lit at night demonstrates safety and transparency—no darkness hiding wrongdoing, everything visible to collective scrutiny. The brightness is sometimes exhausting—Ganati visitors to darker settlements sometimes comment on how pleasant it is to experience nighttime darkness rather than perpetual electric illumination—but it's ideologically important.

The working-class warrens carved through sandstone represent revolutionary urbanism that wasn't planned by architects or mandated by planners but emerged from workers solving their own housing needs with available resources. The warrens are crude by wealthy standards—narrow tunnels, small chambers, minimal decoration—but they're still carved with care, still lit brightly, still show attention to making living spaces human-friendly rather than purely utilitarian. Even the poorest warren has carved details: smoothed walls, carefully cut right angles, attempts at decoration even when skill is limited.

This demonstrates revolutionary principle: workers creating their own solutions with collective resources produces architecture that's functional and beautiful in its own way, superior to top-down planning that ignores resident needs and preferences. The warrens might be confusing to outsiders but they work for residents. That's what matters—function for users rather than aesthetic perfection for observers.

## Daily Life and Rhythms

Life in Ganat proper is defined by industrial rhythm and political fractiousness. The Dam runs continuously—turbines never stop, power flows constantly, workshops operate in shifts 24 hours. Someone is always working. The city never truly sleeps, though activity varies by time.

Morning in the warrens: workers wake in carved chambers lit by electric bulbs that stayed on all night because power is free. They navigate tunnels toward workshops in the Dam or carved into the walls. The tunnels are crowded with people moving toward employment. Street vendors sell breakfast—flatbread, weak tea, preserved fruit, simple foods eaten while walking. The smell is human density: thousands of people in enclosed spaces, sanitation systems working but not eliminating all evidence of habitation.

The markets on the Near Wall open early: farmers from upper canyon bringing produce, fishers selling catch from Lake Ganat, workshop output being sold to consumers. The markets are noisy, fragrant, crowded. Merchants compete for attention. Customers bargain. The covered bazaars carved into the cliff wall are lit brightly despite being carved spaces that would otherwise be dark.

The Senate meets irregularly—sessions scheduled when legislation requires debate or deadlines force decisions. When the Senate is in session, the Far Wall's tunnels fill with senators, their staff, lobbyists representing kilits, petitioners seeking favor, observers watching governance happen. The bureaucratic warrens fill with activity: forms being processed, reports being written, administrators coordinating between government and kilits. The Ministry warrens are bright but exhausting—endless lit tunnels connecting offices where people work at tasks whose collective importance is clear but whose individual contribution feels negligible.

Afternoon brings shift changes in the Dam's industrial district. One group of workers finishes, another begins. The transition happens efficiently—workers know the timing, move through tunnels in opposite directions without collision through practiced coordination. The workshop noise continues uninterrupted. The turbines keep spinning. The power keeps flowing.

Evening in the hookah dens and drinking clubs: people gathering after work to smoke, drink, argue politics, discuss philosophy, share gossip. The establishments are carved into the walls or occupy spaces on the canyon floor, lit brightly, filled with conversation and tobacco smoke and cannabis haze. Someone is always arguing about something—Senate proceedings, Dusk's latest proposal, Dawn's response, whether Patriarch Kaleb is too moderate or too radical, whether expansion east is necessary or reckless, whether accommodation with the Empire is pragmatic or betraying revolutionary principles.

Night in Ganat means continued activity rather than sleep. The workshops keep running. The lights stay on—streets, tunnels, warrens all illuminated continuously because power costs nothing and darkness serves no purpose in revolutionary city. Some workers sleep. Others work night shifts. The city is always active, always producing, always arguing, always building collective capacity through industrial production that the Dam makes possible.

The rhythm is exhausting for visitors and energizing for residents who've adapted to it. Ganat doesn't have quiet contemplative atmosphere. It has fractious energetic intensity—political arguments in hookah dens, industrial production in the Dam, bureaucratic processing in ministry warrens, religious broadcasting from the Cathedral, all happening simultaneously in carved sandstone illuminated by electric lights powered by ancient structure re-engineered to serve revolutionary purposes.

## Relationship to the Republic

Ganat proper is capital, industrial center, and ideological heart. The city produces maybe 30% of Republican manufactured goods through Dam-powered industry. It houses government and broadcasting infrastructure. It provides model for what revolutionary governance and industrial development can achieve when resources permit. Every policy originates here or passes through here. Every major decision involves Ganat in some way.

But Ganat is also increasingly unlike everywhere else in the Republic. The abundance of free electricity, the industrial development it enables, the fractious political culture, the sophisticated salon networks—none of this exists in eastern settlements or pastoral territories or even in Taho where electricity is subsidized but not free. Ganat proper is becoming its own thing: revolutionary capital that's so successful at revolutionary industrialization that it's diverging from the Republic it governs.

The eastern settlements view Ganat as too comfortable, too removed from frontier hardship, too willing to debate fine points of ideology while settlers face material scarcity requiring immediate action. The pastoral kilits view it as oppressively organized, too hierarchical despite revolutionary rhetoric, too eager to extend state authority into territories that function fine through traditional organization. Taho views it as ideologically rigid, too unwilling to accommodate necessary pragmatism, too dominated by Dawn ideologues who've never negotiated with Imperial factors or managed commercial operations.

Ganat views itself as carrying forward revolutionary transformation everyone else benefits from. We develop industry that produces goods you use. We maintain government that protects your rights. We broadcast information that shapes Republican culture. We argue with ourselves constantly because that's what collective governance requires—exposing disagreements, debating solutions, building consensus through fractiousness rather than imposed unity. The arguments are feature not bug. The brightness and transparency are intentional rejection of Imperial opacity. The industrial development is demonstrating what human capacity can achieve when properly organized and supplied with resources.

The future is unclear. Ganat continues growing—population increases as workers arrive seeking employment in Dam industries, warrens expand through sandstone as housing needs grow, transmission lines extend outward bringing electrification to more settlements. But growth also creates tensions: housing becomes scarce despite constant tunnel carving, political divisions intensify as more interests compete for influence, the gap between Ganat's abundant electricity and everywhere else's scarcity becomes harder to justify ideologically.

Some argue Ganat should build more dams—replicate the model elsewhere, spread abundance beyond the capital. Others argue that's impossible—the Ganat dam is unique archaeological find that can't be reproduced with available technology, and building lesser dams elsewhere would cost enormous resources for minimal benefit. Some argue Ganat should accept its unique position and use it to subsidize development elsewhere. Others argue Ganat already subsidizes the Republic through manufactured goods, government services, and ideological leadership, and demanding more is unreasonable.

The arguments continue in Senate, in ministry warrens, in hookah dens, through broadcasting from the Cathedral's transmission towers. The city glows with electric light, hums with industrial production, argues with itself about what revolutionary governance means and whether Ganat represents successful model or dangerous divergence from principles that guided the Revolution. The stone carved with revolutionary imagery—deeper than what came before, removing monarchy to install collective ideals—bears witness to transformation that's ongoing, incomplete, perpetually debated, and undeniably real in its material effects even when its ideological meaning remains contested.

The power flows from the Dam. The lights stay on. The workshops run continuously. The arguments continue. Ganat remains simultaneously the Republic's greatest achievement and its most complicated problem—proof that revolutionary governance and industrial development work, proof that success creates new contradictions requiring new solutions, proof that building collective capacity is possible and that nobody quite knows what to do with that capacity once it's built. The city carved into sandstone and powered by ancient structure keeps growing, keeps producing, keeps arguing with itself, keeps being the revolutionary heart of a Republic that still doesn't know what kind of polity it wants to become.
